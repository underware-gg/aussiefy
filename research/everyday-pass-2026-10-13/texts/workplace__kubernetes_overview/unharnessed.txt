The name Kubernetes comes from the Greek, meaning helmsman or pilot, the bloke at the wheel. K8s as a
short form comes from counting the eight letters between the "K" and the "s". Google chucked the
Kubernetes project open source back in 2014. Kubernetes mixes
[over 15 years of Google's experience](/blog/2015/04/borg-predecessor-to-kubernetes/) running
production workloads at a whopping scale with the pick of the ideas and practices from the community.

## Why you need Kubernetes and what it can do {#why-you-need-kubernetes-and-what-can-it-do}

Containers are a top way to bundle up and run your apps. In a production
environment, you've gotta look after the containers running the apps and
make sure nothing ever falls over. Say a container carks it, another
container's gotta fire up. Wouldn't it be a bloody sight easier if a system handled all that for you?

That's where Kubernetes rides in to save the day! Kubernetes gives you a framework
to run distributed systems that can cop a hit and keep going. It sorts out scaling and failover for
your app, gives you deployment patterns, and a fair bit more. For example: Kubernetes
can handle a canary deployment for your system, no worries.

Kubernetes gives you:

* **Service discovery and load balancing**
  Kubernetes can put a container out there using a DNS name or its own IP address.
  If a container's getting slammed with traffic, Kubernetes can load balance and spread
  the network traffic round so the deployment stays steady.
* **Storage orchestration**
  Kubernetes lets you automatically mount whatever storage system takes your fancy, like
  local storage, public cloud providers, and more.
* **Automated rollouts and rollbacks**
  You can spell out the state you want your deployed containers in using Kubernetes,
  and it'll shift the actual state over to that at a steady, controlled clip.
  For example, you can get Kubernetes to automatically spin up new containers for your
  deployment, turf the old ones and hand all their resources over to the new container.
* **Automatic bin packing**
  You give Kubernetes a cluster of nodes it can use to run containerised tasks.
  You tell Kubernetes how much CPU and memory (RAM) each container needs. Kubernetes can squeeze
  the containers onto your nodes to get the most bang for your buck out of your resources.
* **Self-healing**
  Kubernetes restarts containers that fall over, swaps containers out, knocks off containers that won't
  answer your user-defined health check, and doesn't let on to clients they exist till they're
  ready to serve.
* **Secret and configuration management**
  Kubernetes lets you stash and manage sensitive stuff, like passwords, OAuth tokens,
  and SSH keys. You can deploy and update secrets and app configuration without
  rebuilding your container images, and without leaving secrets hanging out in your stack configuration.
* **Batch execution**
  As well as services, Kubernetes can look after your batch and CI workloads, swapping out containers that fall over, if you want.
* **Horizontal scaling**
  Scale your app up and down with one simple command, with a UI, or automatically based on how hard the CPU's working.
* **IPv4/IPv6 dual-stack**
  Dishing out IPv4 and IPv6 addresses to Pods and Services.
* **Designed for extensibility**
  Bolt features onto your Kubernetes cluster without mucking about with the upstream source code.

## What Kubernetes is not

Kubernetes isn't your traditional, all-in, everything-in-the-box PaaS (Platform as a Service) system.
Since Kubernetes works at the container level rather than down at the hardware level,
it gives you some handy all-round features you'd find in PaaS offerings, like
deployment, scaling and load balancing, and it lets users plug in their own logging, monitoring,
and alerting setups. But Kubernetes isn't one big lump, and these default setups
are optional and you can swap 'em in and out. Kubernetes gives you the building blocks for knocking up developer
platforms, but leaves you the choice and room to move where it counts.
