A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone usually takes part on their feet, normally round about 10 am. The idea is that standing about for ages gets uncomfortable, so the meetings stay short.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing up.[2]

Software development

According to the PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI), the daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3]

Some software development processes have the team get together every day to make commitments to each other. Those daily commitments let everyone hear about any strife coming up and team up to knock over the tricky or time-chewing problems. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development processes,[4][5] like scrum or Kanban, but you can use it in any software development process going.

The meeting should usually happen at the same time and place every working day. Everyone on the team's encouraged to turn up, but the meeting doesn't get put off if a few of 'em aren't there. One of the big things about it is that it's a chance for the team to have a yarn with each other, not a status report for the bosses or stakeholders.[6] Even though it sometimes gets called a kind of status meeting, it's set up to get follow-up chats going and to spot problems before they turn into a right mess. Because it happens so often, needs follow-up chats and is kept short, it also gets people working closer together, which means a lot more know-how gets passed around – a much more hands-on aim than your average status meeting. Team members take turns having their say, sometimes handing round a token to show whose turn it is to talk.[7] Each person runs through what they've got done since the last stand-up, what they reckon they'll be working on till the next one, and anything that's getting in the way, and uses the chance to ask for a hand or team up.[8]

Team members might sometimes ask for a quick clarification or chuck in a short comment, like "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually doesn't get into full-blown discussions.[7]

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and everyone stays standing to remind them to keep it short and to the point, no rabbiting on.[7] The stand-up meeting sometimes also gets called the "stand-up" when you're doing extreme programming, or the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when you're following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) so the team can take stock and size up how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] It's meant to be quick, less than 15 minutes, so any deep-dive yakking about what's in the way gets held over till the thing's done. Since some teams have their meetings standing up, they might call it the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) suggested team members quickly, for one minute each at the most, run through three questions as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

Those questions got the flick from the 2020 Scrum Guide.
