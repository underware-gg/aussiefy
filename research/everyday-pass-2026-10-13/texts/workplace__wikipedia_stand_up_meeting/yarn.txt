Pull up a stool. A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone usually stays on their feet the whole time, generally round about 10 am. The idea is that standing about for ages gets bloody uncomfortable, so nobody lets the thing drag on.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing.[2] Not a chair in sight for the lot of 'em.

Software development

According to the PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI), daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3]

Some ways of building software reckon on the team getting together every day so everyone can commit to their teammates. Those daily promises mean people find out early about any trouble brewing, and can pitch in together to sort out the tricky or time-chewing problems. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development,[4][5] like scrum or Kanban, but you can use it as part of any way of building software you like.

The meeting's meant to happen at the same time and the same spot every working day. Everyone on the team is encouraged to front up, but the meeting doesn't get put off just cos a few of them aren't there. One of the big things about it is that it's a chance for the team to talk to each other, not a progress report for the bosses or the stakeholders.[6] Even though people sometimes call it a kind of status meeting, it's set up to get follow-up conversations going and to spot problems before they turn into a real headache. The practice also brings people closer together at work, what with how often it happens, the follow-up yarns it calls for and how short it is, and all that means a lot more know-how gets passed around. That's a fair bit more get-up-and-go than your average status meeting. Team members take turns to talk, sometimes handing round a token to show whose turn it is.[7] Each one talks about what they've got done since the last stand-up, what they reckon they'll be working on till the next one and anything holding them up, and takes the chance to ask for a hand or team up with someone.[8]

Team members might sometimes ask a quick question to clear something up, or say something short, like "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually isn't the place for a full-blown chinwag.[7]

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and everyone stays standing up to remind 'em to keep it short and to the point.[7] Nobody wants a bloke rabbiting on while their legs go numb. The stand-up meeting is sometimes also called the "stand-up" when doing extreme programming, and the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) for the team to have a think and size up how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] This meeting's meant to be short, under 15 minutes, so any deep dives into what's holding things up get left till after it's over. Cos some teams do theirs standing up, they might call this meeting the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) suggested team members each answer three questions, quick smart, for one minute each at the very most, as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

These questions were taken out of the 2020 Scrum Guide.
