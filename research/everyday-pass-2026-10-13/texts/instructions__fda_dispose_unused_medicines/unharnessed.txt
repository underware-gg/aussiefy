Is your medicine cabinet chockers with expired drugs or medications you don't use any more? The best way to get rid of most kinds of expired, unwanted or unused medicines is through a drug take-back program.

To get rid of your prescription or over-the-counter medicines, you can:

Drop them off at a drug take-back location.

Post them using a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.

If you can't use either of those, follow these guidelines:

If your medicine is on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's flush list, flush these potentially dangerous medicines down the toilet.

If your medicine isn't on the flush list, follow the instructions below to get rid of it at home.

Drug Take-Back Options

The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration runs National Prescription Drug Take Back Day in communities right across the country. Plenty of communities also have their own drug take-back events. Check with your local police to find a location near you, or with the DEA to find a DEA-authorised collector in your area.

Some pharmacies have on-site medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks, mail-back programs and other ways to help you get rid of medicines safely.

Use Prepaid Drug Mail-Back Envelopes

A prepaid drug mail-back envelope is a handy and secure way to get rid of medicines. These envelopes are sold in a few places, including chemists and online. Some pharmacies give them out for free.

To use prepaid drug mail-back envelopes:

Fill the envelope with your unused or expired medicines and seal it.

Post the envelope using the U.S. Postal Service.

How to Dispose of Medicines at Home

When a take-back option isn't easy to get to, there are two ways to get rid of medicines at home, depending on the drug.

Flushing medicines: Because some medicines could be especially harmful to other people, they come with specific directions to flush them down the sink or toilet straight away once they're no longer needed and a take-back option isn't handy.

How will you know? Check the label or the patient information leaflet that came with your medicine. Or have a look at the list of medicines recommended for flushing when a take-back option isn't handy. Remember, don't flush your medicine unless it's on the flush list.

Putting medicines in the household rubbish: If a take-back option isn't available, nearly all medicines, except those on the FDA flush list, can go in your household rubbish bin. That includes prescription and OTC drugs in pills, liquids, drops, patches and creams.

Follow these steps:

Take the drugs out of their original containers and mix them with something unpleasant, like used coffee grounds, dirt or kitty litter. That makes the medicine less appealing to kids and pets – and unrecognisable to anyone who might go rummaging through the bin on purpose looking for drugs.

Put the mixture in something you can close (a resealable zip-lock bag, empty tin or other container) so the drug doesn't leak or spill out.

Chuck the container in the bin at home.

Scratch out all your personal details on the empty medicine packaging to protect your identity and privacy. Throw the packaging out.
If you've got a question about your medicine, ask your health care professional or pharmacist.

Disposing Fentanyl Patches

The fentanyl patch is an example of a product with a powerful opioid medicine in it that can be dangerous to people it's not prescribed for. This sticky patch delivers a strong painkiller through the skin.

Even after a patch has been used, a fair bit of the medicine is still left in it. That's why the drug comes with instructions to flush used or leftover patches.
