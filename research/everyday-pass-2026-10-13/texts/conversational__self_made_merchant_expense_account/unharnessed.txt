CHICAGO, May 4, 189-

_Dear Pierrepont:_ The cashier's just handed me your expense account for the month, and it's enough to make a bloke go round-shouldered just looking at it. When I said I wanted you to get a liberal education, I didn't mean I wanted to buy Cambridge outright. Course, the bills won't send me broke, but they'll send you broke unless you're very, very careful.

I've noticed for the last two years your accounts have been getting heavier every month, but I haven't seen any sign of you taking honours to make up for the extra running costs; and that's bad business, a lot like feeding a mongrel steer that won't fatten up its own weight in corn.

I haven't said anything about it before, since I was banking a fair bit on your own common sense to stop you making a galah of yourself the way some of these young blokes who've never had to work for it do. But just because I've kept me mouth shut, don't go getting it into your head that the old man's loaded and can wear it, because he won't wear it once you're out of college. The sooner you match your spending to what you'll actually be earning, the easier the two of them will find it to get along.

The only sure-fire way a man gets rich quick is to have it handed to him or to inherit it. You're not getting rich that way, not till you've proved you can hold down a pretty important job with the firm, anyway; and of course there's only one spot a man can start from to get to that job with Graham & Co. Doesn't matter whether he's the old man's son or the cellar boss's son: that spot's the bottom. And the bottom on the office side of this business is a seat at the mailing desk, with eight dollars every Saturday night.

I can't hand you success ready-made. It'd do you no good, and it'd do the firm harm. There's plenty of room at the top here, but there's no lift in the building. Starting off, like you are, with a good education, you ought to be able to climb quicker than the bloke who hasn't got one; but there'll come a time when you start at the works when you won't be able to lick stamps as fast as the other lads at the desk. Still, the man who's never licked stamps isn't fit to write letters. Naturally, that's when knowing whether the pie comes before the ice-cream, and how to drive a motor car, won't be a scrap of real use to you.

I'm only bringing this stuff up because I'm afraid your ideas about the terms you're coming into the firm on have got a bit puffed up out East. I can give you a start, but after that you'll have to blast your own way to the front. It's all down to the man. If you gave some blokes a talent wrapped in a napkin to start out in business with, they'd swap the talent for a gold brick and lose the napkin; and there's others you could start off with just the napkin, who'd set up with it in the drapery trade in a small way, and then sweet-talk the other bloke's talent into it.

I've got enough pride to reckon you've got the right stuff in you, but I want to see some of it come out. You'll never make a decent merchant of yourself by doing things backwards from the way the Lord laid down, learning the spending end of business before the earning end. Payday's always a month off for the spendthrift, and he never gets more than sixty cents out of any dollar that comes his way. But a dollar's worth a hundred and six cents to a good businessman, and he never spends the dollar. It's the bloke who keeps his savings up and his expenses down who buys a share in the business. That's where you're going to come up short if your expense accounts aren't telling porkies; and they don't usually tell porkies in that particular direction, though Baron Munchausen was the first travelling salesman, and my reps' bills still show his influence.
