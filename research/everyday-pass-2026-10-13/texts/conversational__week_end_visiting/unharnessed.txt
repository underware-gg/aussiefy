Take, for example, the business of getting up in the morning. Of course, where there's a big house-party it's an easy one, because you can always hear the others pattering about and brushing their teeth. You can time your own getting up by how many people seem to be stirring. But if you're the only guest, there's likely to be a frightful mix-up.

"What time's breakfast?" you ask.

"Oh, whenever you like on Sundays," says the hostess with a generous wave. "Sleep in as long as you like. This is 'Liberty Hall.'"

The feeling behind it is perfectly bonzer, but there's nothing in it you can really get a grip on. It does the job at the time, but come morning there's a vagueness about it that's downright terrifying.

Say you wake up at eight. You listen and hear no one moving. Then it's back onto the cool pillow until eight-twenty. Up on the elbow again, head cocked to one side. There's a creak over towards the stairs. They might all be up and heading down to breakfast! It's the work of a moment to leap out of bed and listen at the door. Maybe open it a modest crack and peer out. Deathly silence, broken only, as they say, by the ticking of the hall clock, and not a soul in sight. They're probably late sleepers. Maybe eleven o'clock is their Sunday getting-up time. Some people _are_ like that.

Shut the door and sit on the edge of the bed. Any more sleep is out of the question. Let's have a look at the pictures in the guest-room, just to pass the time. Here's one of Lorna Doone. How d'e do, Lorna? Here's a group--taken in 1902--showing your host in evening dress, holding a mandolin. Probably in his college musical club. Pretty scruffy-looking mob, you _have_ to say. Well, what about this one? An etching of some dodgy-looking barges on what's probably the Thames. Fair enough, then.

Back to the door to listen again. Tick-tock-tick-tock. If you ran your bath you'd probably wake the whole house. Let's sit back down on the edge of the bed.

Hello, here are some books on the table. "Fifty Famous Sonnets," illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Never touch a sonnet before breakfast. "My experiences in the Alps," by a lady mountain-climber who's written on the fly-leaf, "To my good friends the Elbridges, in memory of many happy days together at Chamounix. October, 1907." That settles _that_. "Essay on Compensation" in limp leather, by R.W. Emerson, published by Houghton, Mifflin & Co. Oh, righto! So they reckoned that'd be over your head, did they? Well, we'll show them! We'll read it just out of spite. Opening it at the red ribbon:

"Of the like nature is that expectation of change which instantly follows the suspension of our voluntary activity. The terror of cloudless noon--"

By the way, it must be nearly noon by now! Only ten past nine! Well, the only thing for it is to get dressed and go out for a wander round the grounds. Scratch the bath as too noisy. And so, very carefully, almost on the sly, you set about getting dressed.
