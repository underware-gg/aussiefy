Okay, so take getting up in the morning, for example. Mind you, when there's a big house-party it's easy enough, because you can always hear the others padding about and brushing their teeth. You can time your own getting up by how many people seem to be up and about. But if you're the only guest, there's every chance of a terrible mix-up.

"What time's brekkie?" you ask.

"Oh, any old time on Sundays," says the hostess with a generous wave. "Sleep in as long as you like. This is 'Liberty Hall.'"

It's a lovely thought, but there's nothing you can really hang your hat on. It's fine at the time, but come the morning it's so vague it's downright scary.

Say you wake up at eight. You listen and can't hear anyone stirring. So it's back over on the cool pillow till eight-twenty. Then up on your elbow again, head cocked to one side. There's a creak over near the stairs. They might all be up and heading down to brekkie! It only takes a second to jump out of bed and listen at the door. Maybe open it a crack and have a peek. Dead quiet, broken only, as they say, by the ticking of the hall clock, and not a soul about. They're probably late sleepers. Maybe eleven o'clock is when they get up on a Sunday. Some people _are_ like that.

Shut the door and sit on the edge of the bed. No chance of more sleep now. Let's have a squiz at the pictures in the guest-room, just to pass the time. Here's one of Lorna Doone. How d'e do, Lorna? Here's a group--taken in 1902--showing your host in a dinner suit, holding a mandolin. Probably in his uni music club. Pretty scruffy-looking bunch, you _must_ say. Well, what about this one? An etching of some dodgy-looking barges on what's probably the Thames. Fair enough, I s'pose.

Back to the door to listen again. Tick-tock-tick-tock. If you ran yourself a bath you'd probably wake the whole house. Better sit down on the edge of the bed again.

Hello, here are some books on the table. "Fifty Famous Sonnets," illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Never touch a sonnet before breakfast. "My experiences in the Alps," by a woman mountain-climber who's written on the fly-leaf, "To my good friends the Elbridges, in memory of many happy days together at Chamounix. October, 1907." Well, that settles _that_. "Essay on Compensation" in limp leather, by R.W. Emerson, published by Houghton, Mifflin & Co. Oh, righto then! Reckon they thought that'd be over your head, did they? Well, we'll show them! We'll read it just to spite them. Opening it at the red ribbon:

"Of the like nature is that expectation of change which instantly follows the suspension of our voluntary activity. The terror of cloudless noon--"

Hang on, it must be nearly noon by now! Only ten past nine! Well, the only thing for it is to get dressed and go for a wander round the grounds. Forget the bath, too noisy. And so, very carefully, almost on the sly, you get dressed.
