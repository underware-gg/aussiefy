Your average household's leaks can chew through more than 9,300 gallons of water a year, and nine percent of homes have leaks chucking away 50 gallons or more a day. The usual suspects round the house are worn-out toilet flappers, drippy taps and other leaky valves. Most of these are a doddle to fix, needing only a few tools and bits of hardware that'll pay for themselves in water savings. Sorting out the easy household leaks can knock about 10 percent off your water bill.

To check your place for leaks, first you've gotta work out whether you're wasting water, then track down where the bugger's coming from. Here's a few tips for finding leaks:

Have a squiz at your water use in one of the colder months, like January or February. If a family of four's going through more than 12,000 gallons a month, you might have some serious leaks on your hands.

Check your water meter before and after a two-hour stretch when nobody's using any water. If the meter's moved at all, you've probably got a leak.

Suss out toilet leaks by dropping a few drops of food colouring into the cistern. If any colour turns up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you've got a leak. (Make sure you flush straight after, or you'll stain the cistern.)

Give the tap washers and pipe fittings a once-over for any water on the outside of the pipe, to check for surface leaks.

Toilet Leaks

Old or clapped-out toilet flappers (that's the valve seal) can cause leaks. This simple rubber gizmo holds the water in the cistern, then lets it go into the bowl when you flush. Because the rubber can wear out, you should check the flapper every so often and replace it at least every five years so it seals properly and doesn't leak. An old or worn flapper can make your dunny flush on its own or quietly leak thousands of gallons a year. Swapping a flapper is a quick, easy fix that'll save you water and a bit of coin. Showerhead Leaks

A showerhead leaking at 10 drips a minute wastes more than 500 gallons a year. That's the water it takes to run 60 loads through your dishwasher. Some leaky showerheads can be fixed by making sure the showerhead's screwed on nice and tight to the pipe stem and using a bit of plumber's tape to hold it. Plumber's tape, also called teflon tape, you can get at most hardware stores, it's easy to put on, and it can help keep leaks in check. For trickier valve leaks in showers that drip when nobody's using them, get on the blower to an experienced handyperson or a licensed plumber.

Tip: While you're at it, it's a good idea to check and, if need be, replace the washer or "o" ring inside the showerhead.
