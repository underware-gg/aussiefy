The average household's leaks can add up to more than 9,300 gallons of water down the drain every year, and nine percent of homes have leaks that waste 50 gallons or more a day. The usual suspects around the house are worn dunny flappers, dripping taps and other leaky valves. These sorts of leaks are often easy to fix, needing only a few tools and bits of hardware that pay for themselves in water savings. Fixing the easy household leaks can save homeowners about 10 percent on their water bills.

To check for leaks at your place, first you need to work out whether you're wasting water, and then track down where the leak's coming from. Here are some tips for finding leaks:

Have a geek at your water use during a colder month, like January or February. If a family of four goes over 12,000 gallons a month, there could be some serious leaks.

Check your water meter before and after a two-hour stretch when no water's being used. If the reading on it moves at all, you've most likely got a leak.

Suss out dunny leaks by putting a few drops of food colouring in the cistern. If any colour rocks up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you've got a leak. (Make sure you flush straight after the test so the tank doesn't stain.)

Go over the tap gaskets and pipe fittings for any water on the outside of the pipe to check for surface leaks.

Dunny Leaks

Old or worn-out toilet flappers (e.g., valve seal) can cause leaks. This simple rubber gizmo holds water in the tank, then lets it go into the bowl when the dunny's flushed. Because the rubber can wear out, the flapper should be checked every so often and replaced at least every five years to make sure you've got a good, tight seal and no leaks. An old or worn flapper can make your dunny flush on its own, or quietly leak thousands of gallons a year without you knowing a thing. Replacing a flapper is a quick, easy fix that'll save water and the folding stuff. Showerhead Leaks

A showerhead leaking at 10 drips per minute wastes more than 500 gallons a year. That's the amount of water it takes to wash 60 loads of dishes in your dishwasher. That's a bloody lot of water for a few drips. Some leaky showerheads can be fixed by making sure there's a tight connection between the showerhead and the pipe stem, and by using pipe tape to secure it. Pipe tape, also called teflon tape, is sold at most hardware shops, is easy to put on, and can help keep leaks in check. For trickier valve leaks in showers that drip when they're not in use, get in an experienced handyperson or a licensed plumber.

Tip: It's also a good idea to check and, if it needs it, replace the washer or "o" ring inside the showerhead while you're doing this repair.
