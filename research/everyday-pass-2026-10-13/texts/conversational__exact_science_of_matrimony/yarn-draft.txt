"Like I've told ya before," says Jeff Peters, "I never had much faith in the treachery of women. As partners or offsiders in even the most innocent sort of swifty, ya can't trust 'em."

"That's a compliment they've earned," says I. "I reckon they've every right to be called the honest sex."

"Course they have," says Jeff. "They've got the other lot either pulling swifties or staying back for 'em. They're right as rain in business till their feelings or their hair get worked up a bit too bloody much. Then ya want a flat-footed, heavy-breathing bloke with sandy whiskers, five nippers and a building and loan mortgage standing by as an understudy to take over her desk. Now, there was that widow lady me and Andy Tucker took on to help us with that little marriage agency caper we got going out in Cairo.

"When you've got enough advertising capital--say a roll as big as the little end of a wagon tongue--there's dosh in marriage agencies. We had about $6,000 and we were counting on doubling it in two months, which is about as long as a caper like ours can run without taking out a New Jersey charter.

"We knocked up an ad that went more or less like this:

"Charming widow, beautiful, home loving, 32 years, possessing $3,000 cash and owning valuable country property, would remarry. Would prefer a poor man with affectionate disposition to one with means, as she realises that the solid virtues are oftenest to be found in the humble walks of life. No objection to elderly man or one of homely appearance if faithful and true and competent to manage property and invest money with judgment. Address, with particulars.

Lonely, Care of Peters & Tucker, agents, Cairo, Ill.

"'So far, so wicked,' says I, when we'd finished cooking up this bit of literature. 'And now,' says I, 'where's the lady?'

"Andy gives me one of his looks, calm but cranky.

"'Jeff,' he goes, 'I thought you'd given up them ideas of realism in your art. Why should there be a lady? When they flog heaps of watered stock on Wall Street, would ya expect to find a mermaid in it? What's a marriage ad got to do with a lady?'

"'Now listen,' says I. 'Ya know my rule, Andy: in all my shonky raids on the letter of the law, the goods sold have got to exist, be seen and be produced. That way, and by keeping a close eye on the city by-laws and the train timetables, I've stayed clear of any strife with the coppers that a five-dollar note and a cigar couldn't square. Now, to work this caper we've got to be able to produce, in the flesh, a charming widow or near enough, with or without the beauty, hereditaments and appurtenances set out in the catalogue and writ of errors, or else end up held by a justice of the peace.'

"'Well,' says Andy, having a bit of a rethink, 'maybe it'd be safer in case the post office or the peace commission tried to sniff round our agency. But where,' he goes, 'could ya hope to find a widow who'd waste her time on a marriage caper with no marrying in it?'

"I told Andy I reckoned I knew just the party. An old mate of mine, Zeke Trotter, who used to pull soda water and teeth in a tent show, had made his missus a widow a year before by drinking some of the old doctor's indigestion cure instead of the liniment he always got boozed up on. I used to drop in at their place a fair bit, and I thought we could talk her into working with us.

"'Twas only sixty miles to the little town where she lived, so I hopped on the I. C. and finds her in the same cottage with the same sunflowers and the same roosters standing on the washtub. Mrs. Trotter fitted our ad a treat, except maybe for beauty and age and what the property was worth. But she looked workable and decent enough to the eye, and it was a kindness to Zeke's memory to give her the job.
