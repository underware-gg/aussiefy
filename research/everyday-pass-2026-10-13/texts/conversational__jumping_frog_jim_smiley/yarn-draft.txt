“Rev. Leonidas W. H'm, Reverend Le--well, there was a bloke here once, by the name of Jim Smiley, in the winter of '49--or maybe it was the spring of '50--I dunno exactly, somehow, though what makes me reckon it was one or the other is cos I remember the big flume wasn't finished when he first come to the camp; but anyway, he was the most curious feller for always bettin' on anythin' that come along you ever seen, if he could get anybody to bet on the other side; and if he couldn't, he'd just change sides. Any way that suited the other bloke would suit HIM--any way at all, just so's he got a bet, he was happy as. And still he was a lucky bugger, uncommon lucky, I s'pose; he near always come out on top. He was always ready and layin' for a chance; there couldn't be one solit'ry thing mentioned but that feller'd offer to bet on it, and take whichever side you please, like I was just tellin' ya. If there was a horse-race, you'd find him cashed up or you'd find him stony broke by the end of it; if there was a dog-fight, he'd bet on it; if there was a cat-fight, he'd bet on it; if there was a chook-fight, he'd bet on it; strewth, if there was two birds sittin' on a fence, he'd bet ya which one would fly off first; or if there was a camp-meetin', he'd be there reg'lar to bet on Parson Walker, who he reckoned was the best preacher about here, and so he was, too, and a decent bloke into the bargain. If he so much as seen a straddle-bug start off anywheres, he'd bet ya how long it'd take him to get to--to wherever the thing was goin', and if you took him up, he'd foller that straddle-bug all the way to Mexico but he'd find out where it was headed and how long it was on the frog and toad. Heaps of the blokes here have seen that Smiley, and they'll tell ya all about him. Why, it never made a skerrick of difference to him--he'd bet on any bloody thing--the dangdest feller you ever struck. Parson Walker's missus was laid up properly crook once, for a fair while, and it looked like they weren't gonna save her; but one mornin' the parson comes in, and Smiley up and asks him how she's goin', and he says she's a good deal better--thank the Lord for his inf'nite mercy--and comin' on so well that with the blessin' of Prov'dence she'd pull through yet; and Smiley, without stoppin' to think, goes, 'Well, I'll resk two-and-a-half she don't, anyway.' Talk about a one-track mind.

“Now, thish-yer Smiley had a mare--the blokes called her the fifteen-minute nag, but that was only in fun, ya know, cos of course she was faster than that--and he used to win a fair bit of dosh on that horse, for all she was slow as buggery and always had the asthma, or the distemper, or the consumption, or somethin' of that kind. They used to give her two or three hundred yards' start, and then pass her on the way; but always at the fag end of the race she'd get all worked up and desperate like, go off like a frog in a sock, and come cavortin' and straddlin' up, flingin' her legs about every which way, sometimes in the air and sometimes out to one side among the fences, kickin' up m-o-r-e dust and makin' m-o-r-e racket with her coughin' and sneezin' and blowin' her nose--and she'd always fetch up at the stand just about a neck ahead, near as you could cipher it. Wheezin' like a busted bellows, and still she'd get up.
