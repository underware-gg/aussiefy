WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, for 28 years the boss of the International Reform Bureau, which he started himself, and known far and wide for his work pushing prohibition and causes of that sort, died in hospital here today of pneumonia. He was 73.

Dr. Crafts, called a "reform fanatic" by the mob who fought the laws he put up, was for years a colourful sight in the lobbies and committee rooms of Congress. Those on his side reckoned he'd had a big hand in getting prohibition through, along with laws to rein in narcotics and other laws of the same kind.

He never kept quiet about how much he hated Sunday entertainment, and he had a red-hot go at getting it banned by law. He also did his level best to stop the Dempsey-Carpentier fight going ahead in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. He wrote a stack on moral questions, too.

After the funeral here tomorrow, the body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher from puritan stock. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long run as a minister in his old man's church, switched to Congregationalism in 1880, and in 1883 went into the Presbyterian ministry, where he stayed for good. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, who turned out books by the stack herself and threw her weight behind religious causes. She was his partner in heaps of his ventures, especially the ones to do with Sunday schools.

Among the reform crowd, few could've matched Dr. Crafts for sheer hard yakka. His pen and his voice barely got a breather in the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, given in twenty-nine different countries, and he'd knocked out a book a year, most, but not all, on religious topics. Here's a funny one, for instance, that crops up in a long list of titles that won't surprise anyone: "Rhetoric Made Racy." On top of turning out papers for religious magazines non-stop, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

To the public at large, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known as a wowser, for his bloody tireless attacks on the things people did for fun. Screen vamps, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, ciggies — they were a few of the targets of his reforming zeal.
