WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, who ran the International Reform Bureau he started himself for 28 years, and who was known far and wide for barracking for prohibition and that sort of carry-on, died of pneumonia in a hospital here today. He was 73.

Dr. Crafts, who the blokes fighting the laws he pushed reckoned was a "reform fanatic," was a real character around the congressional lobbies and committee rooms for years. The folks on his side gave him a fair bit of the credit for getting prohibition through, along with the laws cracking down on narcotics and other legislation of that ilk.

Never one to hold his tongue on Sunday fun and games, Dr. Crafts tried to get the lot of it banned by law. He also had a red-hot go at stopping the Dempsey-Carpentier fight being held in New Jersey in the summer of 1921. He wrote a bloody mountain of stuff on moral questions.

After the funeral service here tomorrow, the body'll be sent to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher with puritan forebears. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long run as a minister in his dad's church, switched to Congregationalism in 1880, and in 1883 went into the Presbyterian ministry, where he stayed put from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a prolific, no-nonsense writer and worker for religious causes, who was his offsider in heaps of his many goings-on, especially anything to do with Sunday schools.

Among the reform mob, not many could've matched Dr. Crafts for sheer hard yakka. His pen and his gob barely got a breather in the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, given in twenty-nine different countries, and he'd written a book a year, most of 'em, but not all, on religious topics. Here's a funny one, for instance, that pops up in a long list of others that won't raise an eyebrow: "Rhetoric Made Racy." On top of always churning out papers for religious magazines, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

To most people, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known for having a go at popular entertainment. Screen vamps, close dancing, "joy rides," which he reckoned "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, ciggies: these were just a few of the things his never-say-die reforming zeal went after.
