Back in the days when pigs could rhyme
And monkeys chewed tobacco,
Hens took snuff to toughen up,
And ducks went quack, quack, quack-o!

There was an old sow with three little pigs, and she couldn't afford to keep them, so she sent them out into the world to make a go of it. The first pig met a bloke carrying a bundle of straw.

“Any chance of that straw, mate? I could build myself a house.”

The bloke handed it over, and the pig built his house. Before long a wolf came knocking.

“Little pig, little pig, let me in.”

“Not bloody likely, by the hair on my chinny-chin-chin.”

“Then I'll huff and I'll puff and I'll blow your house in.”

And he did, too. Huffed, puffed, blew the house in and ate the little pig.

The second pig met a bloke with a bundle of furze.

“Could you let me have that furze, mate? Build myself a house with it.”

The bloke gave it to him, and up went the house. Along came the wolf.

“Little pig, little pig, let me in.”

“Not bloody likely, by the hair on my chinny-chin-chin.”

“Then I'll puff and I'll huff and I'll blow your house in.”

So he huffed and puffed, puffed and huffed, till finally he blew the place down and ate that little pig as well.

The third pig met a bloke with a load of bricks.

“Please, mate, could I have those bricks to build a house?”

The bloke gave him the bricks, and he built his place out of those. Sure enough, up turned the wolf, same as he'd done at the other pigs' places.

“Little pig, little pig, let me in.”

“Not bloody likely, by the hair on my chinny-chin-chin.”

“Then I'll huff and I'll puff and I'll blow your house in.”

Well, he huffed and puffed, huffed and puffed, puffed and huffed, till he was blowing like a busted bellows, but he couldn't shift that house. When he finally twigged that all his puffing wasn't going to do the job, he tried another tack.

“Little pig, I know a nice field of turnips.”

“Whereabouts?” asked the pig.

“Mr. Smith's Home-field. Be ready tomorrow morning and I'll come and get you. We can go together and get some for dinner.”

“Righto,” said the pig. “I'll be ready. What time are we going?”

“Six o'clock.”

So the pig got up at five, fetched the turnips and was back before the wolf turned up, which he did about six.

“Little pig, ready to go?”

“Ready? I've been and come back, mate. Got a lovely potful for dinner.”

The wolf was filthy about being outsmarted, but he reckoned he'd put one over the pig somehow.

“Little pig, I know a lovely apple tree.”

“Whereabouts?”

“Down at Merry-garden,” said the wolf. “And if you don't pull a swifty on me, I'll come for you at five tomorrow and we'll get some apples.”

Next morning the pig was up and at it at four. Off he went for the apples, hoping to be home before the wolf arrived. But it was further away, and he had to climb the tree, so just as he started coming down he spotted the wolf coming. Put the fear of God into him, that did.

“Little pig!” said the wolf when he got there. “Here before me, are you? Apples any good?”

“Beaut,” said the pig. “I'll throw you one.”

He hurled it so far that while the wolf went off to pick it up, the pig jumped down and bolted home.

Next day the wolf came round again.

“Little pig, there's a fair at Shanklin this arvo. Want to go?”

“Too right,” said the pig. “What time'll you be ready?”

“Three.”

As usual, the pig set off early. He got to the fair and bought himself a butter-churn. He was taking it home when he saw the wolf coming. For a moment he didn't know whether he was Arthur or Martha. Then he climbed inside the churn to hide. That tipped it round, and away it rolled down the hill with him inside. The great round thing coming at him scared the living shit out of the wolf, and he raced home without getting to the fair at all.

Later he went to the pig's house and told him how some enormous round thing had come bowling past him down the hill and frightened him half to death.

“Ha! That was me giving you a fright,” said the pig. “I'd been to the fair and bought a butter-churn. Saw you coming, got inside and rolled down the hill.”

Well, now the wolf was mad enough to chew nails. Said he was going to eat that little pig, and he'd come down the chimney to do it. The pig saw what he was up to, hung a pot full of water over the fire and got the flames roaring. Just as the wolf came down, the pig whipped the lid off. In dropped the wolf. The pig slapped the lid straight back on, boiled him up and ate him for supper. And he lived happy ever after.
