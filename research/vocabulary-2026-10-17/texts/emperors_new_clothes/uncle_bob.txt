Years and bloody years ago there was an emperor who was so mad for new clothes he spent every penny on them. All he wanted was to look well dressed. Didn't give a stuff about his soldiers, couldn't be bothered with the theatre. His one pleasure was driving about showing off a fresh suit. Had a coat for every hour of the day. Where you'd normally say, "The king's in his cabinet," with this bloke it was, "The emperor's in his dressing-room."

He lived in a lively great city, with strangers coming in from every corner of the globe each day. One day in wandered two swindlers. Convinced everyone they were weavers, and claimed they could make the finest cloth anyone could imagine. Colours and patterns to knock your socks off, they said. But here's the special trick: clothes made from it were invisible to anyone unfit for their job, or unforgivably stupid.

"That sounds wonderful," thought the emperor. "If I wore a suit of that, I'd find out which people in my empire aren't fit for their positions. Sort the clever ones from the idiots. Must get some woven straightaway."

So he handed the pair a great wad of money in advance to get cracking. They set up two looms and pretended to work like billy-o, doing absolutely bugger-all. Asked for the finest silk and the most precious gold-cloth, then pocketed every bit of it. Stayed up late working away at looms emptier than a pub with no beer.

"I'd like to know how that cloth's coming along," thought the emperor. But remembering that anyone unfit for his office couldn't see it gave him an uneasy feeling. He reckoned he personally had nothing to worry about. Still, better send someone else first, eh? Everyone in town knew what the cloth could do, and they were itching to find out just how useless or stupid their neighbours were.

"I'll send my honest old minister," he decided. "He's intelligent, and nobody knows his job better. He'll be the best judge."

Off went the good old minister to the room where the rogues sat at their empty looms.

"God help us," he thought, eyes widening. "I can't see a bloody thing."

He kept that to himself. Both swindlers beckoned him closer. Didn't he admire that exquisite pattern? Those beautiful colours? Pointed at the empty looms as if they were showing him the crown jewels. He looked for all he was worth. Saw nothing, because there was nothing there.

"Christ," he thought, "am I that stupid? Never thought I was. Nobody can find out. Or am I unfit for my job? No, no, I can't tell them I can't see the cloth."

"Well? Nothing to say?" one of the swindlers asked, still pretending to weave like a man possessed.

"Oh, it's very pretty. Exceedingly beautiful," said the minister, peering through his glasses. "What a pattern! What brilliant colours! I'll tell the emperor I like it very much."

"Pleased to hear it," said the two. Then they described the colours and explained the fancy pattern. The old minister listened carefully so he could repeat it all to the emperor, and that's exactly what he did.

Now the swindlers asked for more money, silk and gold-cloth to keep the weaving going. Kept the whole lot. Not a thread went near a loom, but on they went, making a great show of work.

Soon the emperor sent another honest courtier to see how things were coming along and whether the cloth was nearly finished. Same bloody story. Looked and looked, saw nothing, because there was nothing to see.

"Beautiful piece of cloth, isn't it?" said the pair, showing off and explaining a magnificent pattern that didn't exist.

"I'm not stupid," thought the courtier. "So it must mean I'm not fit for this fine appointment I've got. That's very odd. But nobody must know."

So he praised the unseen cloth, said how delighted he was with the lovely colours and the fine pattern, and told the emperor, "It's excellent."

Now the whole town was talking about this precious cloth. At last the emperor decided he'd see it himself while it was still on the loom. Took a party of courtiers, including the two who'd already been, and went to visit the clever crooks. They were working harder than ever, without a skerrick of thread.

"Magnificent, isn't it?" said those two old statesmen. "Your Majesty must admire the colours and the pattern."

They pointed at the empty looms. Each reckoned the others could see it.

"What the hell?" thought the emperor. "I can't see anything. This is terrible. Am I stupid? Am I unfit to be emperor? Nothing could be worse than that."

Out loud, he said, "Your cloth has our most gracious approval."

He nodded contentedly at the empty loom. Wasn't about to admit he couldn't see a thing. His attendants stared and stared too. Saw exactly as much as everyone else, which was sweet fuck-all, and echoed him: "Very beautiful."

They all urged him to wear the magnificent new clothes at a great procession coming up soon. "Magnificent! Beautiful! Excellent!" The praise was coming out of them like water from a busted main. Everyone seemed delighted, and the emperor made the two swindlers "Imperial Court weavers."

The night before the procession, the crooks pretended to work right through. Burned more than sixteen candles, so everyone could see how busy they were finishing the emperor's new suit. Pretended to lift cloth from the loom. Snipped at empty air with big scissors, sewed with needles that had no thread in them, then finally announced, "The emperor's new suit is ready."

In came the emperor and all his barons. The swindlers held their arms up as if they had clothes in their hands.

"Here are the trousers! Here's the coat! Here's the cloak!"

On they went. "Light as a cobweb, every bit of it. You'll feel as though you've got absolutely nothing on. That's the beauty of them."

"Indeed!" said all the courtiers, who still couldn't see a thing. On account of there being nothing bloody there.

"Would Your Majesty graciously undress," said the swindlers, "so we can help you into your new suit in front of the big looking-glass?"

The emperor stripped off. They pretended to dress him, piece by piece, and he checked himself in the mirror from every angle.

"How well they look! How well they fit!" everyone cried. "Beautiful pattern! Fine colours! What a magnificent suit!"

Then the master of ceremonies announced that the canopy-bearers were ready for the procession.

"I'm ready," said the emperor. "Doesn't my suit fit marvellously?"

One more turn before the mirror, to make sure people thought he was admiring his clothes.

The chamberlains who were supposed to carry the train reached down towards the floor, pretending to lift it. Held their hands as if they were carrying something. No one wanted it getting out that he couldn't see the damn thing.

Off marched the emperor under his lovely canopy, bare-arsed as the day he was born. People in the street and at their windows called out, "The emperor's new suit is beyond compare! Look at that long train! Fits him beautifully!"

Nobody wanted to admit they saw nothing. That'd mean they were unfit for their job or too stupid. Never had an emperor's clothes got such admiration.

Then a little child said, "But he's got nothing on at all."

"Good heavens, listen to an innocent child," said the father. One person whispered it to another, and round it went until the whole crowd was calling, "He's got nothing on at all!"

That hit the emperor like a wet sack. He reckoned they were probably right. But he told himself, "I've got to see it through to the end now."

And the chamberlains walked on with even greater dignity, carrying the train that wasn't there.
