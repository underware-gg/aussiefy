Years and years ago there was an emperor so bloody keen on new clothes he spent every cent he had getting them. Being dressed to the nines was his one ambition. Didn't give a stuff about his soldiers, couldn't get interested in the theatre. What he really liked was heading out so everyone could cop a look at a new suit. He had a coat for every hour of the day. You might say of another king, “He's in his cabinet”; with this bloke it was always, “The emperor's in the dressing-room.”

His great city was a lively joint, with strangers arriving every day from all over the world. One day two con merchants turned up and passed themselves off as weavers. They said they could make the finest cloth anyone could imagine. The colours and patterns were supposed to be absolute beauties. Better still, clothes made from it had a marvellous trick: anyone too bloody stupid, or unfit for the job he held, couldn't see them.

“That sounds like top-shelf cloth,” the emperor thought. “Get myself a suit of that and I'll know which blokes in my empire are no good for their jobs. Sort the smart ones from the drongos in one hit. I need that woven, quick smart.” He handed the pair a big wad of money in advance so they could get cracking straightaway. They set up two looms and carried on as if they were flat out like lizards drinking. In fact, they did sweet bugger-all on those looms. They asked for the finest silk and the most precious gold-cloth, pocketed everything they got, and sat working away at empty looms late into the night.

“I'd like to know how they're getting along with that cloth,” thought the emperor. But he got a bit twitchy remembering that a bloke unfit for his office couldn't see it. He personally reckoned he'd be right, but still thought it'd be wise to send someone else in first to suss things out. Everyone in town knew about the material's special quality and was itching to find out which neighbours were useless or stupid.

“I'll send my honest old minister,” thought the emperor. “He's got brains, and nobody knows his job better. He'll be the best judge of how the stuff looks.”

The good old minister entered the room where the swindlers sat at their empty looms. “Christ almighty!” he thought, his eyes opening wide. “I can't see a bloody thing.” He kept that to himself. Both con men invited him closer and asked whether he admired the beautiful pattern and lovely colours, waving at bare looms. The minister looked for all he was worth, but there was nothing to see because nothing was there. “Oh, hell,” he thought, “am I that stupid? I'd never have reckoned it, and nobody must find out. Or am I not fit for my job? No bloody way can I say I couldn't see the cloth.”

“Got nothing to say about it, then?” one swindler asked, making a great show of being busy at his weaving.

“Oh, it's very pretty. Bloody beautiful, in fact,” said the old minister, peering through his glasses. “What a pattern! What brilliant colours! I'll tell the emperor I'm very taken with it.”

“Glad to hear it,” said the two weavers. They described the colours and talked him through the peculiar pattern. The minister listened carefully so he could repeat it all to the emperor. And that's exactly what he did.

Then the swindlers asked for more money, silk and gold-cloth, saying they needed it for the weaving. They kept every last bit for themselves. Not a thread went anywhere near a loom, but they carried on at the empty frames, busy as a bloke sweeping dust in a sandstorm.

Soon the emperor sent another honest courtier to check how the weavers were going and whether the cloth was nearly finished. Same as the old minister, he stared and stared and saw bugger-all. There was bugger-all there.

“Beautiful piece of cloth, isn't it?” the two swindlers asked, showing off and explaining a magnificent pattern that didn't exist.

“I'm not stupid,” the bloke told himself. “So it must mean I'm no good for this fine job I've landed. That's bloody strange, but I can't let anyone know.” So he praised the cloth he couldn't see, going on about how much he loved the fine pattern and beautiful colours. “It's absolutely first-rate,” he told the emperor.

The whole town was yapping about that precious cloth. Finally the emperor wanted to see it himself before it came off the loom. Off he went with a bunch of courtiers, including the two who'd already visited. The crafty pair were working like billy-o, still without a skerrick of thread.

“Isn't it magnificent?” said the two old statesmen who'd been before. “Your Majesty must admire those colours and that pattern.” They pointed to empty looms, each assuming the others could see the cloth.

“What the hell's going on?” thought the emperor. “I can't see anything. This is terrible. Am I stupid? Am I not fit to be emperor? Nothing could be more bloody dreadful than that.”

“Indeed,” he said, turning to the weavers, “your cloth has our most gracious approval.” He nodded happily at the empty loom, because he wouldn't admit he could see nothing. His whole entourage stared too. None could see any more than the others, but they all said, just like him, “It's very beautiful.” Every one of them recommended wearing the magnificent new clothes in the big procession coming up. “Magnificent, beautiful, excellent,” they all kept saying, pleased as punch. The emperor appointed the two shonks “Imperial Court weavers.”

The night before the procession, the swindlers stayed up pretending to work, burning more than sixteen candles. Everyone had to see how hard they were going to finish the emperor's new suit. They acted out taking cloth off the loom, snipped the empty air with great big scissors and sewed with needles that had no thread. At last they announced, “The emperor's new suit's ready.”

The emperor and all his barons came into the hall. The con men held their arms up as though they were carrying things. “Here's the trousers!” “Here's the coat!” “And here's the cloak!” They went through the lot. “Light as a cobweb, every piece. You'll feel as if you're wearing absolutely nothing, but that's the beauty of them.”

“Indeed!” said the courtiers. None could see a thing, there being nothing to see.

“Would Your Majesty be so kind as to get undressed,” the swindlers said, “so we can help Your Majesty into the new suit in front of the big mirror?”

Off came the emperor's clothes. The pair pretended to dress him, one imaginary piece at a time. He inspected himself in the mirror from every angle.

“Look how good they are! What a fit!” everyone said. “What a lovely pattern! What fine colours! That's a magnificent bloody suit!”

The master of ceremonies announced that the bearers were ready with the canopy for the procession.

“I'm ready,” said the emperor. “Doesn't this suit fit me marvellously?” He turned to the mirror once more, making sure people thought he was admiring his clobber.

The chamberlains who were meant to carry the train reached down as if lifting it off the floor, then pretended to hold it up. They weren't about to let anyone know they couldn't see anything.

So the emperor marched beneath the beautiful canopy. People in the street and at their windows called out, “Nothing compares with the emperor's new suit! What a long train! Fits him like a glove!” Nobody wanted to admit there was nothing to see, because that'd mean being unfit for their job or too bloody stupid. No emperor's clothes had ever had such a wrap.

“But he hasn't got anything on!” a little kid finally said. “Good heavens, listen to that innocent child,” said the father. One person whispered it to another, till at last the whole crowd shouted, “But he hasn't got anything on!” That shook the emperor right down to his bootstraps, because he had a nasty feeling they were right. But he thought, “I've got to see this through to the end now.” And the chamberlains walked even more grandly behind him, carrying on as if they held that train which wasn't bloody there.
