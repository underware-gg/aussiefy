Years and years ago there was an emperor who cared so much about new clothes he spent every last penny getting them. Looking flash was the whole aim of his life. Didn't give a stuff about his soldiers, and the theatre did nothing for him. All he wanted was to drive about showing off a new suit. Had a coat for every hour of the day. You might say of another king, “He's in his council chamber.” With this bloke it was always, “The emperor's in his dressing-room.”

He lived in a great, lively city, with strangers rolling in from all over the world every day. One day a pair of shonks arrived and passed themselves off as weavers. They reckoned they could make the finest cloth anybody could imagine. Not only were its colours and patterns beautiful beyond belief, they said, but it had a special trick: anyone who was no good at their job, or stupid past forgiving, couldn't see it at all.

“Now that's the stuff,” thought the emperor. “A suit of that would show me who's unfit for their position in my empire. I'd sort the clever ones from the drongos quick smart. I must have some woven straightaway.” So he paid the swindlers a hefty sum up front to get cracking. They set up two looms and made out they were working their backsides off, but they weren't doing a blessed thing on them. They asked for the finest silk and the most precious gold-cloth. Everything they got went straight into their own keeping. Then they sat at the empty looms, pretending to work till late at night.

“Wouldn't mind knowing how that cloth's coming along,” thought the emperor. But remembering that anybody unfit for their office couldn't see it made him feel a bit crook in the guts. Naturally, he reckoned he had nothing to worry about himself. Still, best send someone else for the first look. Everyone in town knew this remarkable story about the fabric, and they were all busting to discover how useless or stupid their neighbours were.

“I'll send my honest old minister,” he decided. “He's got the brains to judge it, and nobody understands his job better.”

Off went the good old minister to the room where the swindlers sat at their empty looms. “God help us!” he thought, opening his eyes as wide as dinner plates. “I can't see a bloody thing!” But he kept that to himself. Both swindlers beckoned him closer. Didn't he admire that lovely pattern? Those glorious colours? They pointed away at the empty looms. The poor old bloke tried his damnedest, but he saw nothing, because there was nothing there.

“Oh, hell,” he thought. “Can I be that stupid? Never would've thought it. Nobody must find out. Am I not fit for my position? No, no. I can't tell them I couldn't see the cloth.”

“Well, nothing to say?” asked one of the shonks, still pretending to weave like mad.

“Oh, lovely. Beautiful, absolutely beautiful,” said the minister, peering through his glasses. “What a pattern! What brilliant colours! I'll tell the emperor I'm very taken with it.”

“Glad to hear it,” said the pair. They described all the colours and explained the elaborate pattern. He listened carefully so he could tell the emperor just what they'd told him. Which he did.

Then the swindlers asked for more money, more silk and more gold-cloth to go on weaving. Kept every bit for themselves. Not a thread got anywhere near a loom, but on they went pretending to work at the empty frames.

Soon the emperor sent another honest courtier to see how things were going and whether the cloth was nearly done. Same as the minister: he stared and stared, and saw nothing because there was nothing to see.

“Beautiful bit of cloth, isn't it?” said the pair, showing him and explaining a magnificent pattern that didn't exist.

“I'm not stupid,” the courtier told himself. “Must mean I'm not fit for this fine appointment of mine. Bloody strange, but I can't let anyone know.” So he praised the invisible cloth, raving about the colours and pattern. “First-rate stuff,” he told the emperor.

The whole town was talking about that precious fabric. At last the emperor wanted to see it himself, while it was still on the loom. He went with a crowd of courtiers, including the two who'd already been. The clever swindlers put on a great show of working flat out, without using a scrap of thread.

“Magnificent, isn't it?” said the two officials who'd been before. “Your Majesty must admire those colours and that pattern.” They pointed at the empty looms, each assuming the others could see something.

“What the hell is this?” thought the emperor. “I can't see anything! That's terrible. Am I stupid? Am I not fit to be emperor? Nothing could be worse.”

But aloud he said, “Your cloth has our most gracious approval.” He nodded happily and studied the empty loom, because he wouldn't admit he couldn't see a thing. All the attendants stared too. They could see no more than anybody else, but they all followed his lead. “Very beautiful!” Every one of them urged him to wear these splendid new clothes at the great procession coming up. “Magnificent, beautiful, excellent!” they kept saying. Happy as Larry, the lot of them looked, and the emperor made the two crooks Imperial Court weavers.

The night before the procession, the swindlers pretended to work right through, burning more than sixteen candles. Let people see how hard they were slaving to finish the emperor's new suit! They made out they were lifting cloth off the loom, snipped the air with big scissors, and sewed away with needles carrying no thread. Finally they announced, “The emperor's new suit is ready.”

In came the emperor and all his barons. The swindlers raised their arms as though holding garments. “Here are the trousers! Here's the coat! And here's the cloak!” And so on through the lot. “Light as a cobweb, every piece. You'll feel as though you've got nothing on, but that's the beauty of them.”

“Indeed!” said all the courtiers. Couldn't see anything, of course. There wasn't anything to see.

“Would Your Majesty be so good as to undress?” asked the swindlers. “Then we can help you into your new suit in front of the big looking-glass.”

Off came the emperor's clothes. The pair pretended to dress him, one piece at a time, and he turned this way and that before the mirror.

“What a look! What a fit!” everyone said. “Such a beautiful pattern! Such fine colours! A magnificent suit!”

The master of ceremonies announced that the men carrying the procession's canopy were ready.

“So am I,” said the emperor. “Doesn't my suit fit marvellously?” Then he turned to the looking-glass once more, so people would think he was admiring his clobber.

The chamberlains who were meant to carry the train reached down to the ground as if picking it up, then held their hands as though they'd got it. They weren't about to let anyone think they couldn't see it.

Out marched the emperor under that beautiful canopy. People in the streets and hanging out their windows all cried, “Nothing to touch the emperor's new suit! Look at that long train! Fits him a treat!” Nobody would admit seeing nothing: that'd mean being useless at your job or thick as two short planks. Never had an emperor's clothes got such a wrap.

Then a little kid piped up. “But he hasn't got anything on!”

“Good heavens, listen to the innocent child,” said the father. The word passed from person to person: did you hear what the kid said? “He hasn't got anything on!” At last the whole crowd was calling it out.

That hit the emperor hard, because he had a nasty feeling they were right. But he told himself, “I've got to see this through to the end now.” And on went the chamberlains, walking prouder than ever, carrying a train that wasn't bloody there.
