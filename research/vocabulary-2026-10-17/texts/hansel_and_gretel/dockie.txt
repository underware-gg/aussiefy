Near a great forest lived a poor woodcutter, his wife and his two children, a boy called Hansel and a girl called Grethel. There was precious little to eat or drink, and when a terrible shortage hit the country the poor bloke couldn't even earn their daily bread. One night he lay tossing about, worrying himself sick, and let out a great sigh.

“What's going to become of us?” he asked his wife. “We can't even feed the kids. There's nothing left for ourselves.”

“Tell you what we'll do,” she said. “First thing in the morning we'll take the children into the thickest part of the forest. Make them a fire, give them a bit of bread each, then go off to work and leave them. They'll never find their way home, and we'll be shot of them.”

“No,” said the woodcutter. “I can't do that. I haven't got it in me to take my own kids into the forest and leave them on their own. The wild animals would make a feed of them in no time.”

“Oh, don't be a bloody fool,” she said. “Then all four of us starve. Better get the coffins ready.” She kept at him like a loose sheet of tin in the wind, giving him no peace till he agreed.

“But my heart goes out to those poor kids,” he said.

The children were too hungry to sleep, so they'd heard everything their stepmother said to their father. Grethel cried her heart out. “We're done for,” she told Hansel.

“Steady on, Grethel,” Hansel said. “Don't fret. I'll work something out.” Once the parents were asleep he got up, put on his little coat, opened the back door and slipped outside. The moon was bright, and the white flints in front of the house gleamed like little bits of silver. He bent down and stuffed his little coat pocket with as many as it would take. Then he went back in. “Easy now, little sister,” he said. “Go off to sleep. God won't leave us in the lurch.” And he lay down in his bed again.

Day was just breaking, before the sun was up, when the wife came and woke them.

“Up you get, you lazy little sods. We're going into the forest to cut wood.”

She gave them each a piece of bread.

“That's your dinner. Don't eat it before then, because you're getting no more.”

Grethel carried the bread under her apron, since Hansel's pockets were full of flints. Off they all went towards the forest. After a little way Hansel stopped and looked back at the house. He kept doing it, till his father said, “Hansel, what are you gawking at? Your legs still work, don't they?”

“Dad, I'm looking at my little white kitten,” said Hansel. “It's sitting on the roof saying goodbye to me.”

“You little goose,” said the woman. “That's not your kitten. That's the sun on the chimney-pot.”

Of course he wasn't looking for a kitten at all. Every so often he was taking a flint out of his pocket and dropping it on the road.

When they got to the middle of the forest, their father told them to gather wood for a fire to keep them warm. Hansel and Grethel collected a little mountain of brushwood. They set it alight, and once the flames were well up the wife said, “Lie down by the fire and have a rest, you two. We're off to cut wood. We'll come and get you when we're ready.”

So Hansel and Grethel sat by the fire, and at noon ate their pieces of bread. They thought their dad was still working in the trees, because they could hear what sounded like his axe. But it was only a dry branch hanging from a withered tree, knocking back and forth in the wind. They waited so long their eyelids got heavy, and soon they were sound asleep. When they woke at last it was night. Grethel began to cry. “How the hell are we ever going to get out of this forest?”

Hansel comforted her. “Just hold on a bit till the moon comes up. Then we'll find our way home easy enough.”

Once the full moon rose, he took his little sister's hand and followed the flints, shining silver and showing the road. They walked the whole night through and got to their father's house at daybreak. They knocked. The wife opened the door and saw Hansel and Grethel standing there. “You naughty little devils! Why'd you sleep so long in the forest? We thought you were never coming home!”

But their father was glad as anything to see them. Leaving those two alone in the forest had been eating him alive.
