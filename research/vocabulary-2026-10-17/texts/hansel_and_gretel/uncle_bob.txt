Near a great bloody forest lived a poor woodcutter, his wife and his two kids, Hansel and Grethel. They were scratching for every bite and swallow, and when a real shortage hit the country, the poor bastard couldn't even earn their daily bread. One night he lay there turning like a snag on a hotplate, worrying himself sick.

"What's going to become of us?" he sighed to his wife. "Can't feed the kids, and there's bugger-all left for us."

"I'll tell you what we'll do," she said. "First thing tomorrow, we'll take the children into the thickest part of the forest. Make them a fire, give them a piece of bread each, then go off to work and leave them. They'll never find their way home. That'll be them off our hands."

"No," said the woodcutter. "I can't do that. Haven't got it in me to take my own kids into the forest and leave them on their own. The wild animals would eat them."

"You bloody fool," she said. "Then all four of us can starve. You'd better start knocking the coffins together." She kept at him like a loose gate in a gale until he gave in.

"But I feel terrible for the poor little things," he said.

Now the kids couldn't sleep either, their bellies were that empty. They'd heard their stepmother's whole rotten plan. Grethel cried her eyes out.

"We're done for," she told Hansel.

"Settle down, Grethel. Don't fret. I'll work something out."

Once the grown-ups were asleep, Hansel got up, put his little coat on, opened the back door and slipped outside. The moon was bright, and the white flints out the front shone like bits of silver. He bent down and stuffed his coat pocket till it couldn't hold another stone. Back inside, he told Grethel, "Easy now, little sister. Get some sleep. God won't abandon us." Then he lay down again.

Day was only just breaking, before the sun was up, when the wife woke them.

"Up you get, lazybones. We're off to cut wood."

She handed them a piece of bread each. "That's your dinner. Don't eat it before then, because you won't be getting any more."

Grethel tucked the bread under her apron; Hansel's pockets were chockers with flints. Off the four of them went. Every little while Hansel stopped and looked back at the house, until his father said, "What are you gawping at, Hansel? Your legs haven't fallen off. Keep moving."

"Looking at my little white kitten, Dad. It's up on the roof saying goodbye."

"Don't be a goose," said the woman. "That's sunshine on the chimney-pot, not your kitten."

He wasn't looking at a kitten at all, of course. Every so often he'd been taking a flint out and dropping it on the track.

When they reached the middle of the forest, their father told them to gather wood for a fire to keep warm. Hansel and Grethel piled up a little mountain of brushwood. They lit it, and when the flames were going well, the wife said, "Lie down by the fire and have a rest. We'll cut some wood and come back for you when we're ready."

The kids stayed by the fire. At noon they ate their bread. They reckoned their father was still chopping nearby, because they could hear what sounded like an axe. But it was only a dry branch hanging from a dead tree, knocking back and forth in the wind. They waited and waited, until their eyes were heavy as wet blankets and they fell asleep.

When they woke it was night. Grethel started crying again. "How the hell are we going to get out of this forest?"

"Just wait for the moon," Hansel told her. "Then we'll find our way home easy enough."

Up came the full moon. Hansel took his little sister's hand and followed the flints, shining silver and showing them the way. They walked the whole night, and by daybreak they were back at their father's house. They knocked, and their stepmother opened the door.

"You naughty children!" she said. "What did you sleep in the forest so long for? We thought you weren't coming home at all!"

The hide of her. But their father was glad to see them. Leaving those two alone in the woods had been eating him alive.
