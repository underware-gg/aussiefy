Eighty-seven years ago, our fathers brought a new nation into being on this continent. Born out of Liberty, it stood for a plain proposition: all men are created equal. No bloke born with a better claim to being human than the next.

Now we're up to our necks in a great civil war, finding out whether that nation—or any nation born with those beliefs and committed to them—can last. Here we are on one of the great battlefields of that war. We've come to set aside part of it as the last resting place for the blokes who gave their lives here so the nation could live. Bloody right we should. It's fitting, and it's proper.

But take the bigger view, and we can't dedicate this ground, can't consecrate it, can't make it holy just by standing here and saying so. The brave men who fought here, living and dead, have already done that, miles beyond anything our poor efforts could add or take away. The world won't take much notice of our words, or remember them for long. But it can never forget what those men did here.

It's us still breathing who ought to dedicate ourselves to the work left unfinished, the work the men who fought here have carried so nobly this far. We ought to commit ourselves to the enormous job still ahead: take from these honoured dead a stronger devotion to the cause they gave absolutely everything for. Resolve here, with no bloody wriggle room, that they haven't died for nothing. That this nation, under God, will see freedom born anew. And that government of the people, by the people, for the people will never disappear off the face of the earth.
