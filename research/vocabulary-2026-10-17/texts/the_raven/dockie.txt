One miserable midnight, there I sat, worn thin and tired as a dog,
Bent over stacks of odd old books and knowledge lost before,
Nodding, nearly off to sleep, when sudden came a tapping,
Like somebody softly knocking, knocking at my bedroom door.
“Some visitor,” I muttered, “knocking at my bedroom door—
Just that, and nothing more.”

I remember clear as anything: December, bleak and bitter,
Every ember dying threw its little ghost across the floor.
How I wanted morning! Tried, and failed, to nick a little
Rest from grief out of my books—my grief for lost Lenore,
For that rare and shining girl the angels call Lenore—
Here she's nameless evermore.

And those purple curtains, rustling, silken, sad and shifty,
Put the wind right up me, worse than anything before;
So to stop my heart from hammering like boots on corrugated iron,
I kept saying, “Just some visitor asking at my bedroom door,
Some poor late visitor asking entry at my bedroom door—
That's all, and nothing more.”

Then I got my nerve together, stopped the bloody dithering.
“Sir,” I said, “or Madam, sorry, didn't mean to be a bore;
Truth is I was having forty winks, and you were knocking
So damned gently, barely tapping, tapping at my bedroom door,
I could hardly tell I'd heard you.” Then I flung aside the door—
Pitch-black dark, and nothing more.

Long I stared into that blackness, wondering, scared and doubting,
Dreaming things no mortal ever had the hide to dream before;
Not a sound to split the silence, not a sign out in the darkness,
Till one whispered word slipped out of me: “Lenore!”
I whispered it; an echo muttered back at me, “Lenore!”
Only that, and nothing more.

Back I turned into the bedroom, everything inside me burning,
Soon I heard that tapping, louder than it'd been before.
“Surely,” I said, “something's at the lattice of my window;
Better see what's there, and get this mystery sorted at the core—
Heart, just settle for a moment while I sort this mystery's core—
It's the wind, and nothing more!”
