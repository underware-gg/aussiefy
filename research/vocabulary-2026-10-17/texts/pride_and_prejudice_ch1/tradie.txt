Everyone reckons it's as certain as Monday following Sunday: a single bloke with a decent pile of money must be on the lookout for a wife.

He might roll into the district without anyone knowing the first bloody thing about his feelings or plans. Doesn't matter. The local families have that idea bolted down so tight they've already marked him out as the rightful property of one of their daughters.

“Mr. Bennet, love,” his wife said one day, “have you heard Netherfield Park's finally been rented?”

Mr. Bennet said he hadn't.

“Well, it has,” she said. “Mrs. Long's just been here and given me the whole drum.”

Mr. Bennet didn't answer.

“Don't you want to know who's taken it?” his wife demanded, getting antsy.

“You're busting to tell me, and I've got no objection to listening.”

That was all the green light she needed.

“Well, love, Mrs. Long says a young bloke from the north of England's taken Netherfield, and he's absolutely rolling in it. Came down on Monday in a chaise with four horses to look the place over, loved it, and struck a deal with Mr. Morris there and then. He's moving in before Michaelmas, and some of his servants'll be in the house by the end of next week.”

“What's his name?”

“Bingley.”

“Married or single?”

“Single, love, of course! A single bloke with a great fortune—four or five thousand a year. What a bloody windfall for our girls!”

“How's that? What's it got to do with them?”

“Mr. Bennet, for heaven's sake, don't be such a pain in the arse. You know perfectly well I'm thinking he could marry one of them.”

“Is that what he's moving here for?”

“Moving here for? Don't talk rubbish! But he could very well fall for one of them, so you have to go and visit him the minute he arrives.”

“Don't see why. You and the girls can go. Or send them on their own—that might work even better. You're as good-looking as any of them; Mr. Bingley might fancy you best of the lot.”

“Oh, love, you flatter me. I've had my share of good looks, certainly, but I don't pretend I'm anything out of the box these days. When a woman's got five grown-up daughters, she ought to stop worrying about her own beauty.”

“Generally, by that stage, there isn't much beauty left to worry about.”

“But you really must go and see Mr. Bingley when he moves into the district.”

“I'm promising no such bloody thing, I assure you.”

“Think of your daughters! Just think what a setup it'd be for one of them. Sir William and Lady Lucas are determined to go for that very reason, and you know they don't normally visit new arrivals. You really have to go. We can't possibly visit him unless you do.”

“You're making a mountain out of a molehill. I reckon Mr. Bingley'll be very pleased to see you. I'll send a few lines with you saying he's got my hearty permission to marry whichever of our girls he likes. Though I ought to put in a good word for my little Lizzy.”

“Don't you dare. Lizzy's no better than the others. She's not half as pretty as Jane, and she's not half as cheerful as Lydia. But you're always putting her ahead of the rest.”

“None of them's got much to recommend her,” he said. “They're silly and know bugger-all, much like other girls. But Lizzy's a bit quicker on the uptake than her sisters.”

“Mr. Bennet, how can you run down your own children like that? You enjoy getting under my skin. You've got no feeling for my poor nerves.”

“You've got me wrong, love. I've got a great respect for your nerves. They're old mates of mine. I've heard you speak very thoughtfully about them for twenty years at least.”

“Ah, you haven't got a clue what I suffer.”

“Well, I hope you'll get over it and live to see plenty more young blokes on four thousand a year come into the district.”

“They'll be no bloody use to us. Twenty of them could come and it'd make no difference if you won't visit them.”

“You can bank on it, love: when there's twenty, I'll visit the whole lot.”

Mr. Bennet was a funny old mix of sharp brains, sarcastic humour, keeping himself to himself and changing his mind on a whim. Twenty-three years of experience hadn't been enough for his wife to get the measure of him. Working her out was a simpler job. She wasn't very bright, didn't know much, and her temper could turn like milk in the sun. Whenever she was unhappy, she fancied her nerves were playing up. Getting her daughters married was her life's work; visiting people and getting the latest gossip kept her going.
