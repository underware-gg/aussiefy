Everybody reckons it's a fact carved in stone: give a single bloke a decent fortune and he must be looking for a wife.

Doesn't matter if he's only just moved into the district and nobody knows his feelings or what he's got in mind. The neighbouring families have that notion screwed in so tight, they reckon he's already earmarked for one of their daughters. Poor bastard hasn't unpacked and they're measuring him for a wedding suit.

"My dear Mr. Bennet," his wife said one day, "did you hear Netherfield Park's finally been let?"

He hadn't.

"Well, it has. Mrs. Long's just been here and told me the whole story."

Mr. Bennet said nothing.

"Don't you want to know who's taken it?" she demanded, getting shirty.

"You want to tell me. I've no objection to listening."

That was all the starting gun she needed.

"Well, Mrs. Long says Netherfield's been taken by a young man with a great big fortune, from the north of England. Came down Monday in a chaise with four horses to look at the place, loved it so much he agreed with Mr. Morris on the spot. He's moving in before Michaelmas, and some of his servants will be there by the end of next week."

"What's his name?"

"Bingley."

"Married or single?"

"Single, of course, my dear! A single man with a large fortune—four or five thousand a year. What a bloody wonderful thing for our girls!"

"How's that? What's it got to do with them?"

"Mr. Bennet, how can you be such a pain in the arse? You know perfectly well I'm thinking he'll marry one of them."

"Is that why he's settling here?"

"Why? Don't talk rubbish! But he could very well fall in love with one of them, so you must visit him the moment he arrives."

"Can't see the need. You and the girls can go. Or send them by themselves—that might be better. You're as good-looking as any of them; Mr. Bingley might fancy you most."

"My dear, you flatter me. I've had my share of good looks, certainly, but I'm not claiming to be anything special now. A woman with five grown-up daughters ought to stop thinking about her own beauty."

"In those circumstances, she often hasn't much beauty left to think about."

"But you really must go and see Mr. Bingley when he arrives in the neighbourhood."

"That's more than I'm promising, I can tell you."

"Think of your daughters. Think what a fine establishment one of them could have! Sir William and Lady Lucas are absolutely determined to go, for that very reason, and you know they don't usually visit newcomers. You must go. We can't possibly visit him unless you do."

"You're being too fussy. I'm sure Mr. Bingley will be delighted to see you. I'll send a few lines with you, giving my hearty consent for him to marry whichever girl takes his fancy. Though I'll have to put in a good word for little Lizzy."

"You'll do no such thing! Lizzy's no better than the others. She's not half as pretty as Jane, or half as good-tempered as Lydia. But you always put her first."

"None of them has a great deal going for her," he said. "They're all silly and ignorant, like other girls. But Lizzy's a bit quicker off the mark than her sisters."

"Mr. Bennet, how can you run your own children down like that? You enjoy winding me up. You haven't an ounce of sympathy for my poor nerves."

"You've got me wrong, my dear. I have enormous respect for your nerves. They're old friends of mine. I've heard you speak of them with great consideration these twenty years at least."

"Oh, you don't know what I suffer."

"Well, I hope you get over it and live to see plenty of young men with four thousand a year move into the district."

"Twenty of them could come and it'd do us no bloody good if you won't visit them."

"Depend on it, my dear: once there are twenty, I'll visit every last one."

Mr. Bennet was an odd mixture: sharp mind, sarcastic humour, kept himself to himself, and changed course whenever the fancy took him. After three-and-twenty years, his wife still couldn't get a handle on him. His mind had more doglegs than a country racetrack.

She was easier to work out. Not much understanding, not much information, and a temper that didn't stay put. When she was unhappy, she reckoned it was her nerves. Getting her daughters married was the business of her life. Visiting people and catching up on the news kept her going between campaigns.
