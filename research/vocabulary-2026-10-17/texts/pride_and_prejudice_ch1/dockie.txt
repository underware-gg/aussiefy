It's taken as gospel everywhere: a single bloke with a decent pile of money must be on the lookout for a wife.

Doesn't matter if nobody knows a thing about his feelings or plans when he first moves into the district. The families round about have got that idea nailed down so firmly they reckon he's practically earmarked for one of their daughters already.

“Mr. Bennet, love,” his missus said one day, “have you heard Netherfield Park's finally been let?”

Mr. Bennet said he hadn't.

“Well, it has,” she said. “Mrs. Long was just here and gave me the whole yarn.”

He said nothing.

“Don't you want to know who's taken it?” she demanded, getting impatient.

“You want to tell me, and I've got no objection to listening.”

That was all the opening she needed.

“Well, love, Mrs. Long says a rich young bloke from the north of England's taken Netherfield. Came down on Monday in a chaise with four horses to look the place over, loved it so much he shook on it with Mr. Morris straightaway. He's taking possession before Michaelmas, and some of his servants'll be in by the end of next week.”

“What's his name?”

“Bingley.”

“Married or single?”

“Single, of course, love! Single and loaded: four or five thousand a year. What a turn-up for our girls!”

“How's that? What's it got to do with them?”

“Oh, Mr. Bennet, how can you be such a pain in the arse? You know perfectly well I'm thinking he could marry one of them.”

“Is that what he's moved here for?”

“Moved here for? Don't talk rot. But he might very well fall in love with one of them, so you've got to go and see him as soon as he arrives.”

“Can't see the need myself. You and the girls can go, or send them on their own. That might be better, actually. You're as good-looking as any of them, so Mr. Bingley might fancy you best of the lot.”

“Love, you flatter me. I've had my share of looks, certainly, but I'm not setting myself up as anything special these days. A woman with five grown daughters ought to stop thinking about her own beauty.”

“A woman in that situation hasn't often got much beauty left to think about.”

“But, love, you really must go and see Mr. Bingley when he moves into the neighbourhood.”

“That's more than I'm promising you.”

“Think of your daughters. Think what a fine home and position it'd mean for one of them! Sir William and Lady Lucas are set on going for that very reason, and you know they don't usually visit newcomers. You simply must go. We can't possibly visit him if you don't.”

“You're being terribly fussy about it. I dare say Mr. Bingley'd be delighted to see you. I'll send a few lines along with you, giving him my wholehearted consent to marry whichever of the girls he chooses. Though I'll have to put in a good word for my little Lizzy.”

“Don't you dare do any such thing. Lizzy's no better than the others. She's not half as pretty as Jane, nor half as good-tempered as Lydia. But you're forever putting her ahead of the rest.”

“There's not much to recommend any of them,” he said. “Silly and ignorant, like other girls. But Lizzy's got a bit more wit about her than her sisters.”

“Mr. Bennet! How can you run down your own children like that? You enjoy getting me worked up. You've got no sympathy for my poor nerves.”

“You've got me wrong, love. I've the highest regard for your nerves. Old friends of mine, they are. You've been telling me about them, with great care and attention, for at least twenty years.”

“Oh, you haven't a clue what I go through.”

“Well, I hope you get over it and live to see plenty more young blokes on four thousand a year moving into the district.”

“Wouldn't do us a scrap of good if twenty of them came, since you won't visit.”

“Count on it, love: once there are twenty, I'll visit the bloody lot.”

Mr. Bennet was a queer old mix of quick brains, a sharp tongue, keeping himself to himself and changing his mind with the wind. Twenty-three years hadn't been enough for his wife to work him out. She was easier to read. Not much understanding, not much information, and a temper that could turn like milk in the sun. Whenever she was unhappy she fancied her nerves were playing up. Getting her daughters married was her life's work; visiting people and hearing the latest gossip were what kept her going.
