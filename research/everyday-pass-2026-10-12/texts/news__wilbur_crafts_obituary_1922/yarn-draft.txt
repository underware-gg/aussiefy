WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Listen to this. Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, the bloke who ran the International Reform Bureau for 28 years after startin' it up himself, and who was famous far and wide for barrackin' for prohibition and every other movement of that sort, carked it in a hospital here today. Pneumonia got him. He was 73.

The people fightin' the laws he pushed reckoned he was a "reform fanatic," and for years he was a real character round the lobbies and committee rooms of Congress, hard to miss. The ones on his side gave him a fair bit of the credit for gettin' prohibition through, and laws to rein in narcotics, and other laws of the same sort.

He never kept his trap shut about Sunday amusements, either. Crafts wanted 'em banned by law, the lot. He also had a go at stoppin' the Dempsey-Carpentier stoush from goin' ahead in New Jersey in the summer of 1921. Fancy tryin' to call off the biggest fight of the year. And he churned out writin' on moral questions by the bucketload.

After the funeral here tomorrow, they'll send the body up to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher from good old puritan stock. He finished up at Wesleyan in 1869 and at the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long run as a minister in his old man's church, switched over to the Congregationalists in 1880, and in 1883 joined the Presbyterian ministry, where he stayed put from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a writer who turned out heaps of work herself and threw her weight behind religious causes, and she was his partner in a lot of what he got up to, especially the Sunday school side of things.

Among the reform mob, you'd be hard pressed to find anyone who did as much yakka as Crafts. His pen and his gob got bugger-all rest in the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, given in twenty-nine different countries, and he'd knocked out a book a year, most of 'em, not all, on religious topics. Mind you, there's one odd title in a long list of otherwise unsurprisin' ones: "Rhetoric Made Racy." Didn't see that one comin'. On top of always sendin' articles off to the religious magazines, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

To most folks, of course, Crafts was best known as the wowser who went after everything people did for a bit of fun. Screen vampires, close dancin', "joy rides," which he said often ended up a ride you'd be sorry and red-faced about for the rest of your days; Sunday baseball, ciggies: just a few of the things he took aim at with his never-say-die reformin' zeal. Bloody tireless, he was. The fun police never had a harder-workin' officer.
