Is your medicine cabinet chockers with expired drugs or meds you don't use any more? The best way to get rid of most kinds of expired, unwanted or unused medicines is through a drug take-back program.

To get rid of your prescription or over-the-counter medicines, you can:

Drop them off at a drug take-back spot.

Post them off in a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.

If neither of those works for you, here's what to do:

If your medicine is on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's flush list, flush these potentially dangerous medicines down the dunny.

If your medicine isn't on the flush list, follow the steps below to get rid of it at home.

Drug Take-Back Options

The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration runs National Prescription Drug Take Back Day in communities right across the country. Plenty of communities have their own drug take-back days too. Check with your local police to find a drop-off spot near you, or with the DEA to find a DEA-authorised collector in your area.

Some chemists have medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks in store, mail-back programs and other ways to help you get rid of medicines safely.

Use Prepaid Drug Mail-Back Envelopes

A prepaid drug mail-back envelope is a handy, secure way to get rid of them. You can buy these envelopes in a few places, including chemists and online. Some chemists hand them out for free.

To use a prepaid drug mail-back envelope:

Fill the envelope with your unused or expired medicines and seal it up.

Post it with the U.S. Postal Service.

How to Get Rid of Medicines at Home

When there's no take-back option handy, there are two ways to get rid of medicines at home, depending on the drug.

Flushing medicines: Some medicines could do real harm to someone else, so they come with clear directions to flush them down the sink or the dunny straight away once you're done with them, if there's no take-back option handy.

How will you know? Check the label or the patient information leaflet that comes with your medicine. Or have a squiz at the list of medicines that should be flushed when there's no take-back option handy. Remember, don't flush your medicine unless it's on the flush list.

Putting medicines in the household rubbish: If there's no take-back option, nearly all medicines, except the ones on the FDA flush list, can go in your household bin. That covers prescription and over-the-counter drugs: pills, liquids, drops, patches and creams.

Here's how:

Take the drugs out of their original containers and mix them with something yucky, like used coffee grounds, dirt or kitty litter. That makes the medicine less tempting for kids and pets, and unrecognisable to anyone who might go rummaging through the bin on purpose looking for drugs.

Put the mix in something you can close (a resealable zip-lock bag, an empty tin or some other container) so the drug can't leak or spill out.

Chuck the container in the bin at home.

Scratch out all your personal details on the empty medicine packaging to protect your identity and privacy. Then bin the packaging.

If you've got a question about your medicine, ask your doctor or your chemist.

Getting Rid of Fentanyl Patches

The fentanyl patch is one example of a product with a powerful opioid medicine in it that can be dangerous to anyone it's not prescribed for. This sticky patch delivers a strong painkiller through the skin.

Even after a patch has been used, heaps of the medicine is still left in it. That's why the drug comes with instructions to flush used or leftover patches.
