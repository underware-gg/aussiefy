Good news if you're after a real Chrissie tree: permits go on sale from November 13, 2025, online through Recreation.gov, and in person at all the Santa Fe National Forest District Offices and the Supervisor's Office. You'll find all the details here about where you're allowed to cut, the dates, and which kinds of trees you can take.

How to get your Christmas Tree permit:

Online through Recreation.gov: just search for Santa Fe National Forest Christmas Tree Permits. Make sure you have a good read of the overview and the need-to-know info before you buy. You'll also need to set up a Recreation.gov account, or log in to the one you've got, to pay for it.

In person at the Supervisor's Office in Santa Fe, N.M., or at the Jemez, Cuba, Coyote, Española and Pecos/Las Vegas District Offices. Give us a bell for current opening hours.

The Walatowa Visitor Center will sell permits in person on Saturdays only, from 9:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m., from November 22 through to December 20, 2024.

You might be able to buy a pre-cut Christmas tree at the district offices, too. It all depends on the weather and how many we've got, and people who can't cut their own tree get first dibs.

The SFNF's Christmas tree permits are $10, and there are no refunds. If you buy online through recreation.gov, there's an extra $2.50 service fee on top. One tag covers a tree up to 5 inches across the trunk and 10 feet tall. Anything taller than 10 feet and/or wider than 5 inches needs an extra tag. So a 15-foot tree, for example, needs two tags.

Fourth graders with an Every Kid Outdoors pass can get a free Christmas tree permit. Just pop in the pass or voucher number when you buy the permit (heads up: the $2.50 service fee still applies).

And here's a bonus: cutting a Chrissie tree is actually good for the bush. The permit system helps thin out patches where heaps of skinny little trees are all crammed in together. Local forest health experts pick out the spots that'll do better with a bit of thinning, and the trees there tend to be just the right size for a Christmas tree. Taking those trees out of the set areas gives the others room to grow bigger, and can open up patches where the wildlife can find a feed.
