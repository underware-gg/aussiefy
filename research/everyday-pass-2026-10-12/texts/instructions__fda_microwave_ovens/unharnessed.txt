The microwaves get made inside the oven by an electron tube called a magnetron. They bounce around the metal insides of the oven till the food soaks them up. Microwaves make the water molecules in your tucker jiggle about, and that makes the heat that cooks it. That's why food with plenty of water in it, like fresh veggies, cooks quicker than other stuff. The microwave energy turns into heat as the food soaks it up, and it doesn't make your food "radioactive" or "contaminated."

Even though the heat's made right there in the food, microwaves don't cook food from the "inside out." When you're cooking thick stuff, the outside layers get heated and cooked mostly by the microwaves, while the middle cooks mainly from the heat moving in from the hot outer layers.

Cooking in the microwave can use less energy than the regular way, because food cooks faster and the energy only heats the food, not the whole oven. Microwaving doesn't knock the goodness out of food any more than normal cooking does. Fact is, food done in the microwave might hang on to more of its vitamins and minerals, because the microwave can cook it quicker and without adding water.

Glass, paper, ceramic or plastic containers get used for microwaving because microwaves go straight through them. The microwaves can't heat those containers themselves, but they can still get hot from the food cooking inside. Some plastic containers shouldn't go in the microwave at all, because the heat of the food can melt them. Generally, metal pans or alfoil shouldn't go in either, because the microwaves bounce off them, so the food cooks unevenly and you might wreck the oven. The instructions that come with every microwave tell you what kinds of containers to use. They also cover how to test a container to see if it's right to go in the microwave or not.

Avoiding Injuries from Super-Heated Water in Microwave Ovens

In the past the FDA's had reports of nasty skin burns and scalds on people's hands and faces from hot water bursting out of a cup after it got overheated in the microwave. Super-heated water (water heated past boiling point) doesn't look like it's boiling, and it happens when water's heated on its own in a clean cup. If it's super-heated, the slightest bump or movement, like picking the cup up or tipping in a spoonful of instant coffee, can set off a violent eruption with the boiling water blowing out of the cup. Putting stuff like instant coffee or sugar in before you heat it cuts that risk right down.

Folks should stick closely to the precautions and recommendations in their microwave's instruction manual, especially about heating times. Make sure you don't go over the recommended heating times when you're working out the best settings to get your water to the temperature you want.
