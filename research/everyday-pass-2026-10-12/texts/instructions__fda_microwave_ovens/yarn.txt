Now, ya might've wondered how the microwave actually does it. The microwaves get made inside the oven by an electron tube called a magnetron. They bounce round the metal insides of the oven till the tucker soaks 'em up. Microwaves make the water molecules in your food jiggle about, and that jiggling makes the heat that cooks it. That's why food with heaps of water in it, like fresh veggies, cooks quicker than other stuff. The microwave energy turns into heat as the food soaks it up, and no, it doesn't make your dinner "radioactive" or "contaminated." Nothing glowing on your plate, promise.

Even though the heat's made right there in the food, a microwave doesn't cook from the "inside out." With a thick bit of tucker, the outside gets heated and done mostly by the microwaves, while the middle cooks mainly from the heat creeping in from the hot outside layers.

Cooking in the microwave can be easier on the power bill than doing it the usual way, because the food cooks faster and the energy only heats the food, not the whole box. It doesn't knock the goodness out of your food any more than ordinary cooking does, either. In fact, food done in the microwave can hang on to more of its vitamins and minerals, because it cooks quicker and you don't have to add water.

You use glass, paper, ceramic or plastic containers in the microwave because the microwaves go straight through them. The containers themselves don't get heated by the microwaves, but they can still get hot from the food cooking inside, so watch your mitts. Some plastic containers shouldn't go in at all, because the heat of the food can melt 'em. And as a rule, don't put metal pans or aluminium foil in there either: the microwaves bounce off them, so your tucker cooks unevenly and you could end up cooking the oven too. The instructions that come with every microwave tell you which containers to use, and how to test a container to see if it's right to go in.

Avoiding Injuries from Super-Heated Water in Microwave Ovens

The FDA's had reports in the past of nasty skin burns and scalds on people's hands and faces, from hot water erupting out of a cup after it'd been overheated in the microwave. Super-heated water (water heated past boiling point) doesn't look like it's boiling at all, and it happens when you heat water on its own in a clean cup. It just sits there, quiet as a mouse. But if it's super-heated, the slightest bump, like picking up the cup or tipping in a spoonful of instant coffee, can set off a flamin' great eruption, with boiling water blowing out of the cup. Put the instant coffee or sugar in before you heat it and that risk drops right off.

So follow the warnings and advice in your microwave's instruction book to the letter, especially the heating times. When you're working out the best setting to get your water to the temperature you want, make sure you don't go past the recommended heating times. That's how you end up with a cuppa in your face instead of in your gob.
