RESTON, Va. – Low-flying planes will start going over eastern California and a thin slice of western Nevada in early October, getting a picture of the geology with airborne geophysical gear.

The flights are a joint effort between the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS). The USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is all about bringing the country's maps of geology and mineral resources up to date, and finding energy and groundwater resources and natural hazards.

"Teaming up with the state surveys helps get national goals done, like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is pitching in on the nation's work, and adding to what the state knows about things that really matter for water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

The survey takes in a chunk of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin, which is home to a wide range of mineral deposits and districts that are known mineral resources, like the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., the biggest historic producer of tungsten in the U.S. There's still plenty of potential for exploring and mining in the survey areas. Possible critical minerals and related commodities include antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc.

The new high-resolution geophysical surveys will give the basic geological data that can back up future mineral exploration, and also provide crucial below-ground data for sizing up the potential for geothermal energy, plus volcano and earthquake hazards in the Long Valley caldera, along the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and in Owens Valley.

The survey involves collecting magnetic and radiometric data from choppers flying pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart at a height of 330 feet (100 metres). The USGS has contracted NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to collect the airborne magnetic and radiometric data. Their AS350 choppers will be fitted with a long "stinger" sticking out from the main cabin or the skids, which holds the sensors collecting the data. These scientific instruments are completely passive and give off nothing that's any risk to people, animals or plants. No photos or video will be taken. The aircraft will be flown by experienced pilots who are specially trained and approved for low-level flying. These pilots work with the FAA to make sure the flights are safe and above board under U.S. law. The surveys will only be done in daylight hours.

Flights will be based out of the Bishop and Lone Pine airports, and will fly over these counties: Fresno, Inyo, and Mono in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral in Nevada.

Earth MRI covers airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping with LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys, and geologic mapping projects. The data collected is made freely available to the public on the USGS ScienceBase website.
