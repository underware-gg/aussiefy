CHICAGO, May 4, 189-

_Dear Pierrepont:_ The cashier's just handed me your expense account for the month, and it's enough to make a bloke hunch over like he's been carrying a sack of spuds. When I said I wanted you to get a good all-round education, I didn't mean I wanted to buy Cambridge. Now, the bills won't break me, of course, but they'll break you unless you're dead careful. Very, very careful.

I've noticed for the last two years that your accounts have been getting fatter every month, but I haven't seen a sign of you taking honours to justify the extra running costs; and that's bad business. It's a lot like feeding a mongrel of a bullock his own weight in corn when he won't put on an ounce.

I haven't said anything about it before, because I was banking a fair bit on your own common sense to stop you making a mug of yourself, the way some of these young blokes do when they've never had to work for a quid. But just because I've sat tight, don't go getting it into your head that the old man's rolling in it and can wear it, because he won't wear it once you're out of uni. The sooner you bring your spending down to what you'll actually be earning, the easier the two of them will find it to live together.

The only sure way a bloke can get rich quick is to be handed it or to inherit it. You're not going to get rich that way, at least not until you've proved you can hold down a pretty important job with the firm; and of course there's only one place a fella can start from on the way to that job at Graham & Co. It doesn't make a skerrick of difference whether he's the old man's son or the cellar boss's: that place is the bottom. And the bottom on the office side of this business is a seat at the mailing desk, with eight dollars every Saturday night.

I can't hand you success off the shelf. It'd do you no good, and it'd do the firm harm. There's plenty of room at the top here, but there's no lift in the building. Starting out, like you are, with a good education, you ought to climb quicker than the fella who hasn't got one; but there'll come a time, when you start at the factory, that you can't lick stamps as fast as the other young fellas at the desk. And yet the bloke who's never licked stamps isn't fit to write letters. That's the time, naturally, when knowing whether the pie comes before the ice-cream, and how to drive a motor car, won't be heaps of use to you.

I only mention all this because I've a feeling your ideas about the terms you're coming into the firm on have got a bit big for their boots over there in the East. I can give you a start, but after that you'll have to blast your own way to the front. It's all up to the man. Give some blokes a talent wrapped in a napkin to start their business with, and they'll swap the talent for a gold brick and lose the napkin as well; and there are others you could start off with nothing but the napkin, who'd set up in a small way in the drapery business with it and then sweet-talk the other bloke's talent in as well.

I'm proud enough to believe you've got the right stuff in you, but I want to see some of it come out. You'll never make a decent merchant of yourself by doing it back to front from the way the Lord laid down, learning the spending end of business before the earning end. For the big spender, pay day's always a month off, and he can never get more than sixty cents out of any dollar that comes his way. But to a good businessman a dollar's worth a hundred and six cents, and he never spends the dollar. It's the bloke who keeps his savings up and his expenses down who ends up buying a share of the business. And that's where you'll find yourself coming up short, if your expense accounts aren't telling porkies; and they don't usually tell porkies in that particular direction, though Baron Munchausen was the first travelling salesman, and my reps' bills still show his touch.
