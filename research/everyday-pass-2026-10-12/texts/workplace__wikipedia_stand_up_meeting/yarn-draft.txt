Pull up a stool. Actually, don't. A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone usually stays on their feet the whole time, normally round about 10 am. The idea is that standing about for ages gets uncomfortable, so nobody lets the thing drag on. Sore feet keep it short.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom does its meetings standing up.[2] So if it's good enough for the King's advisers, it's good enough for your lot.

Software development

According to the PMBOK (7th edition) from the Project Management Institute (PMI), the daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3] In other words: what'd you get done, what are you up to today, and what's got in your way or looks like it's about to.

Some ways of building software have the team get together every day so everyone can tell their teammates what they're going to have a crack at. Saying it out loud each day means people hear early about anything that might turn into a headache, and can team up to sort out the curly or slow-going problems. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development,[4][5] like scrum or Kanban, but you can use it with any way of building software you like.

The meeting's usually meant to happen at the same time and in the same spot every working day. Everyone on the team's encouraged to rock up, but if a few are missing, the meeting goes ahead anyway; nobody waits around for the stragglers. One of the big things about it is that it's a chance for the team to talk to each other, not a progress report for the bosses or the stakeholders.[6] People sometimes call it a kind of status meeting, but it's really set up to get follow-up yarns going and to pick up on problems before they turn into a proper mess. Because it happens so often, needs those follow-up chats and is kept short, it also gets people working more closely together, and that means heaps more know-how gets passed round the team. That's a much more hands-on aim than your typical status meeting, where half the room's nodding off. The team take turns to talk, and sometimes they pass a token around to show whose turn it is.[7] Each person says what they've got done since the last stand-up, what they reckon they'll get done before the next one, and anything that's holding them up, and it's their chance to ask for a hand or to team up with someone.[8]

Every so often someone'll ask a quick question to clear something up, or chuck in a short one-liner like "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually doesn't get into a full-blown discussion.[7] Save the deep-and-meaningfuls for later.

The meetings are usually timeboxed to somewhere between 5 and 15 minutes, and everyone stays standing to remind them to keep it short and to the point.[7] Each of 'em belts through their bit, then the lot of 'em leg it back to their desks. The stand-up meeting also gets called the "stand-up" in extreme programming, and the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when you're following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) where the team stop and take stock of how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] It's meant to be quick, under 15 minutes, so any big long natters about what's holding things up get put off till after it's finished. Since some teams do theirs standing up, they might call it the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) reckoned each team member should quickly answer three questions, a minute each, tops, to feed into this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

Those questions got the chop in the 2020 Scrum Guide.
