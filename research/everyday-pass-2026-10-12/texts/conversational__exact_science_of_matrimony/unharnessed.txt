"Like I've told ya before," says Jeff Peters, "I never had much faith in the perfidiousness of women. As partners or co-workers in even the most harmless bit of a lurk, you can't trust 'em."

"Fair enough, they've earned that one," I said. "Reckon they've got every right to be called the honest sex."

"Well, why wouldn't they be?" says Jeff. "They've got the blokes either running lurks or flogging themselves on overtime for 'em. They're right as rain in business till their feelings or their hairdos get worked up too much. Then you want a flat-footed, heavy-breathin' bloke with sandy whiskers, five kids and a building society mortgage standing by as an understudy to take over her desk. Now, there was this widow we roped in, me and Andy Tucker, to give us a hand with that little marriage agency caper we got going out in Cairo.

"When you've got enough advertising money behind ya, say a roll as thick as the skinny end of a wagon tongue, there's a quid to be made in marriage agencies. We had about $6,000 and figured we'd double it in two months, which is about as long as you can keep a caper like ours running without taking out a New Jersey charter.

"We knocked up an ad that went something like this:

"Charming widow, beautiful, home loving, 32 years, possessing $3,000 cash and owning valuable country property, would remarry. Would prefer a poor man with affectionate disposition to one with means, as she realizes that the solid virtues are oftenest to be found in the humble walks of life. No objection to elderly man or one of homely appearance if faithful and true and competent to manage property and invest money with judgment. Address, with particulars.

Lonely, Care of Peters & Tucker, agents, Cairo, Ill.

"'So far, so pernicious,' I says, once we'd finished cooking up that bit of literature. 'And now,' I says, 'where's the sheila?'

"Andy gives me one of his calm, fed-up looks.

"'Jeff,' he says, 'I thought you'd given away them ideas of realism in your art. Why's there gotta be a sheila? When they flog a heap of watered stock on Wall Street, d'you expect to find a mermaid in it? What's a marriage ad got to do with an actual woman?'

"'Now listen here,' I says. 'You know my rule, Andy: in all my dodgy little raids on the letter of the law, whatever's being sold has gotta exist, be seen and be produced. That way, and by having a good hard squiz at the city by-laws and the train timetables, I've stayed out of any strife with the coppers that a five-dollar note and a cigar couldn't fix. Now, to pull this one off we've gotta be able to front up with a real live charming widow, or near enough, with or without the looks, hereditaments and appurtenances set out in the catalogue and writ of errors, or else end up hauled before a justice of the peace.'

"'Well,' says Andy, rejigging his thinking, 'maybe it'd be safer, in case the post office or the peace commission come sniffing round our agency. But where,' he says, 'are you gonna find a widow who'd waste her time on a marriage caper with no marrying in it?'

"I told Andy I reckoned I knew just the one. An old mate of mine, Zeke Trotter, who used to pull soda water and teeth in a travelling tent show, had gone and made his missus a widow a year back by drinking some of the old doctor's indigestion cure instead of the liniment he always got sloshed on. I used to drop in at their place a fair bit, and I reckoned we could get her on board.

"It was only sixty miles to the little town where she lived, so I hopped on the I. C. and there she was, same cottage, same sunflowers, same roosters perched on the washtub. Mrs. Trotter fitted our ad pretty bloody well, except maybe for the looks and the age and what the property was worth. But she looked workable and decent enough to the eye, and it was doing right by Zeke's memory to give her the job.
