"Like I've told ya before," says Jeff Peters, "I never had much faith in the two-facedness of women. As partners, or as offsiders in even the most harmless little lurk, you can't trust 'em as far as you can chuck 'em."

"They've earned the compliment," I said. "Seems to me they've got every right to be called the honest sex."

"And why wouldn't they be?" says Jeff. "They've got the other half either running lurks or staying back at work for 'em. They're right as rain in business till their feelings or their hair get worked up a bit too much. Then you want a flat-footed, heavy-breathing bloke with sandy whiskers, five nippers and a mortgage with the building society waiting in the wings to take over her desk. Now, there was that widow lady me and Andy Tucker took on to help us with that little marriage agency caper we got going out in Cairo.

"When you've got enough to spend on advertising--say a roll of notes as thick as the little end of a wagon shaft--there's good dosh in marriage agencies. We had about $6,000 and we were counting on doubling it in two months, which is about as long as a lurk like ours can run before you'd need a New Jersey charter to keep it legal.

"We knocked up an ad that went something like this:

"Charming widow, beautiful, home loving, 32 years, has $3,000 in hard dosh and owns valuable country property, would remarry. Would prefer a poor man with a loving nature to one with money, as she realises the solid virtues are most often found in the humble walks of life. No objection to an elderly bloke or a plain-looking one if he's faithful and true and can manage property and invest dosh with good sense. Write, with particulars.

Lonely, Care of Peters & Tucker, agents, Cairo, Ill.

"'So far, so crooked,' says I, when we'd finished our little bit of literature. 'And now,' says I, 'where's the lady?'

"Andy gives me one of his looks, calm as you like but fair narked.

"'Jeff,' says he, 'I thought you'd given up them ideas about realism in your art. Why should there be a lady? When they flog heaps of watered-down shares on Wall Street, would you expect to find a mermaid in 'em? What's a marriage ad got to do with a lady?'

"'Now listen here,' says I. 'You know my rule, Andy. In all my dodgy little raids on the letter of the law, the goods I'm selling have got to be real, where you can see 'em, and handed over on the spot. That way, and by keeping a close eye on the council by-laws and the train timetables, I've stayed clear of any strife with the coppers that a five-quid note and a cigar couldn't fix. Now, to pull this off we've got to be able to wheel out a real live charming widow, or near enough, with or without the beauty and all the bits and pieces and goods and chattels listed in the catalogue and the writ of errors, or else end up front of the beak.'

"'Well,' says Andy, rearranging his noggin, 'maybe it'd be safer, in case the post office or the peace commission come sniffing round our agency. But where,' he says, 'are you gonna find a widow who'd waste her time on a marriage lurk with no marriage in it?'

"I told Andy I reckoned I knew just the woman. An old mate of mine, Zeke Trotter, who used to pull soda water and teeth in a travelling tent show, had made his missus a widow a year before by knocking back a bottle of the old doctor's indigestion cure instead of the liniment he always got sozzled on. I used to drop in at their place a fair bit, and I reckoned we could get her to work with us.

"It was only sixty miles to the little town where she lived, so I hopped on the I. C. train and found her in the same cottage with the same sunflowers and the same roosters standing on the washtub. Mrs. Trotter fitted our ad a treat, except maybe for the beauty and the age and what the property was worth. But she looked a goer and easy enough on the eye, and it was a kindness to Zeke's memory to give her the job.
