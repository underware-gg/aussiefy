Sad news on Saturday, this. The family of American musician and songwriter Bob Weir let everyone know he'd died with his loved ones around him. The old Grateful Dead guitarist and singer was 78. His family put it out on Instagram, saying he'd been fighting cancer not long ago but had died from "underlying lung issues". They didn't say what day he died.

Weir played rhythm guitar and sang with the American psychedelic rockers the Grateful Dead for decades, and he wrote a few of their songs too, including the hit "Sugar Magnolia", which was a fixture at just about every Dead show. He had a long solo run as well, putting together all sorts of bands with his old Grateful Dead mates, and he kept on kickin', playing live right up till a few months before he died.

He was born Robert Hall Parber on October 16, 1947, in San Francisco, California, and grew up round the San Francisco Bay Area, with a stint at school over in Colorado, where he met John Perry Barlow, who'd later join the Grateful Dead crew too. Weir picked up the guitar at 13 and drifted into the local bluegrass scene, which is where he first came across Jerry Garcia, plucking away on a banjo. On December 31, 1965, Garcia and Weir shook on starting their own outfit, Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions, and that turned into the Grateful Dead. Not a bad way to see in the new year.

Weir played rhythm guitar and sang for the band for decades. From the 1970s on he also had a crack at a solo career and a whole swag of other bands outside the Grateful Dead, including Kingfish, Bobby and the Midnites, RatDog, Furthur, The Other Ones, The Dead, Wolf Bros, and Dead & Company. Every one of them had other Grateful Dead players in it, and they covered the lot, from country rock to jam band to jazzy roots rock built on blues and country. On the mic he mostly did harmonies and backing vocals, but he sang lead on a fair few tracks, including the Grateful Dead anthem "Truckin'", which the Library of Congress picked out as a national treasure in 1997.

Off the stage, Weir also set up the Tamalpais Research Institute, an online platform for live performance, and he was into his politics, getting behind a few causes, including the environment, helping people doing it tough, and the arts. He'd stayed a regular on the Bay Area music scene, playing with local musos and co-owning the Sweetwater Music Hall in Mill Valley, California. A bloke who never really got off the road, by the sound of it.
