Once a bear has spotted you and is paying attention to you, a few extra tricks can help stop things getting worse. Here are some general tips. But please check the advice for each park you visit: it does vary from park to park depending on how the local bears behave.

Let the bear know who you are by talking calmly, so it knows you're a human and not something to eat. Stay still; stand your ground but slowly wave your arms. Help the bear work out that you're a human. It might come closer or stand up on its hind legs to get a better look or a sniff. A bear standing up is usually just curious, not threatening.

Stay calm and remember most bears don't want to have a go at you; they usually just want to be left alone. Bears might bluff their way out of an encounter by charging and then pulling away at the last second. Bears can also get defensive by woofing, yawning, drooling, growling, snapping their jaws and laying their ears back. Keep talking to the bear in a low voice; it'll help you stay calmer, and it won't come across as threatening to the bear. A scream or a sudden movement might set off an attack. Never copy bear noises or let out a high-pitched squeal.

Pick up small kids straight away. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear might think it's the sound of a prey animal. Slowly wave your arms above your head and tell the bear to back off. Do NOT run or make any sudden movements. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear might think it's the sound of a prey animal.

Hike and travel in groups. Groups of people are usually noisier and smellier than one person on their own. So bears often notice groups from further away, and because of their combined size, groups are intimidating to bears too.

Make yourselves look as big as you can (for example, move to higher ground).

Do NOT let the bear get at your food. Getting your tucker will only encourage the bear and make the problem worse for everyone else.

Do NOT drop your pack, as it can protect your back and stop a bear getting at your food.

If the bear isn't moving, back off slowly and sideways; that way you can keep an eye on the bear and not trip over. Moving sideways also doesn't come across as threatening to bears.

Do NOT run, but if the bear follows you, stop and hold your ground. Bears can run as fast as a racehorse, uphill and down. Like dogs, they'll chase animals that run away.

Do NOT climb a tree. Grizzlies and black bears can both climb trees.

Clear out of the area or take a detour. If you can't, wait until the bear moves off. Always leave the bear a way out.

Be extra careful if you see a female with cubs; never get yourself between a mother and her cub, and never try to go up to them. The chances of an attack go up a lot if she sees you as a danger to her cubs.

Bear Attacks

Bear attacks are rare; most bears are only interested in protecting their food, their cubs or their space. Still, being mentally prepared can help you react the best way. Every situation's different, but below are guidelines on how brown bear attacks can differ from black bear attacks. Help look after others by reporting every bear incident to a park ranger straight away. Above all, keep your distance from bears!

Brown/Grizzly Bears: If you're attacked by a brown/grizzly bear, keep your pack on and PLAY DEAD. Lie flat on your stomach with your hands clasped behind your neck. Spread your legs to make it harder for the bear to flip you over. Stay still until the bear leaves the area. Fighting back usually makes these attacks more intense. But if the attack keeps going, fight back hard. Use whatever you've got on hand to hit the bear in the face.

Black Bears: If you're attacked by a black bear, DO NOT PLAY DEAD. Try to get away to somewhere secure like a car or a building. If you can't get away, try to fight back with any object you can get hold of. Aim your kicks and punches at the bear's face and muzzle.

If any bear attacks you in your tent, or stalks you and then attacks, do NOT play dead—fight back!
