Once a bear's spotted you and is keeping an eye on you, there's a few more things you can do to stop it turning into a real drama. Here's some general tips. Mind you, check what each park you visit recommends: the advice does change from park to park, depending on how the local bears behave.

Let the bear know who you are by talking calmly, so it knows you're a human and not something to eat. Keep still; stand your ground but slowly wave your arms. Help the bear work out that you're a human. It might come closer or stand up on its back legs to get a better look or a sniff. A bear standing up is usually just having a sticky beak, not threatening you.

Stay calm and remember most bears don't want to have a go at you; they usually just want to be left alone. Bears might bluff their way out of it by charging, then pulling up and turning away at the last second. They can also get defensive by woofing, yawning, drooling, growling, snapping their jaws and laying their ears back. Keep talking to the bear nice and low; it'll help you stay calmer, and it won't rattle the bear. A scream or a sudden move might set off an attack. Never copy bear noises or let out a high-pitched squeal.

Pick up the littlies straight away. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear might think it's the sound of an animal it hunts. Hike and get about in groups. A group of people is usually noisier and smellier than one person on their own. So bears often twig to a group from further away, and because there's more of you all up, groups put the wind up bears too.

Make yourselves look as big as you can (for example, get up onto higher ground).

Do NOT let the bear get at your tucker. Getting a feed off you will only egg the bear on and make things worse for the next lot.

Do NOT drop your pack, because it can protect your back and stop a bear from getting into your tucker.

If the bear's staying put, move away slowly and sideways; that way you can keep an eye on the bear and you won't trip over. Moving sideways doesn't threaten bears either.

Do NOT run, but if the bear follows you, stop and hold your ground. Bears can run as fast as a racehorse, uphill and down. Like dogs, they'll chase anything that bolts.

Do NOT climb a tree. Grizzlies and black bears can both climb trees.

Clear out of the area or take a detour. If you can't, wait till the bear moves off. Always leave the bear a way out.

Be extra careful if you see a female with cubs; never get between a mum and her cub, and never try to go up to them. The chances of an attack go way up if she reckons you're a danger to her cubs.

Bear Attacks

Bear attacks are rare; most bears only care about protecting their tucker, their cubs or their space. Still, having your head in the right place can help you react the best way. Every situation's different, but below is a rundown of how brown bear attacks can differ from black bear attacks. Help look after others by reporting every bear run-in to a park ranger straight away. Above all, keep your distance from bears!

Brown/Grizzly Bears: If a brown/grizzly bear has a go at you, keep your pack on and PLAY DEAD. Lie flat on your stomach with your hands clasped behind your neck. Spread your legs so it's harder for the bear to flip you over. Stay still until the bear's cleared off. Fighting back usually makes these attacks worse. But if the attack keeps going, fight back with everything you've got. Use whatever's at hand to hit the bear in the face.

Black Bears: If a black bear attacks you, DO NOT PLAY DEAD. Try to get away to somewhere safe like a car or a building. If you can't get away, fight back with anything you can grab. Aim your kicks and punches at the bear's face and snout.

If any bear goes for you in your tent, or stalks you and then attacks, do NOT play dead—fight back!
