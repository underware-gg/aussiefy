Here's one for ya. I truly believe the Maker who made us all makes everything in New England except the weather. Dunno who makes that, but I reckon it must be raw apprentices down at the weather-clerk's factory, learning the trade in New England for their board and their clothes. Once they've got the hang of it they get promoted to making weather for countries that want a beaut product and will take their business elsewhere if they don't get one.

There's a fair old variety to the New England weather, and a stranger can't help admiring it. And regretting it, a bit. The weather's always up to something there. Always minding its business, always cooking up new designs and trying them out on the locals to see how they go. But it gets through more work in spring than any other time of year. In spring I've counted a hundred and thirty-six different kinds of weather inside twenty-four hours.

It was me that made the name and the fortune of that bloke with the marvellous collection of weather on show at the Centennial, the one that knocked the foreigners for six. He was going to travel the whole world collecting samples from every climate going. I said, "Don't you bother, mate. You come to New England on a good spring day." I told him what we could do for style, variety and quantity. Well, he came, and he had his whole collection done in four days. As for variety, he owned up that he got hundreds of kinds of weather he'd never even heard of. And as for quantity, well, once he'd picked out and chucked away everything with the slightest flaw in it, he still had weather enough, and weather to spare. Weather to hire out, weather to sell, weather to put in the bank, weather to invest, weather to give to the poor.

The people of New England are patient and easygoing by nature, but there are some things they just won't cop. Every year they knock off a heap of poets for writing about "Beautiful Spring." These are mostly blow-ins who bring their ideas about spring from somewhere else, and naturally they can't know how the locals feel about it. So, before they know it, the chance to ask how the locals feel has gone for good.

Old Probabilities, the weather forecaster, has a huge name for getting his predictions right, and fair enough too. You pick up the paper and watch how crisp and sure of himself he is, ticking off what today's weather will be on the Pacific, down South, in the Middle States, round Wisconsin. Watch him cruise along, chuffed with himself, till he gets to New England, and then watch his tail drop. He's got no idea what New England's weather is going to do. Well, he chews it over, and after a while he comes out with something like this: probably north-east to south-west winds, shifting to the south and the west and the east and everywhere in between, high and low barometer swapping round from place to place; probable areas of rain, snow, hail and drought, before or after earthquakes, with thunder and lightning. Then he jots down a postscript, just to cover himself: "But it is possible that the programme may be wholly changed in the meantime."

Yep, one of the shiniest gems in the New England weather is how dazzlingly unpredictable it is. There's only one thing you can be sure of: there's going to be heaps of it, a proper grand parade, but you can never tell which end of the parade is going to move first. You get ready for a drought, leave your umbrella at home and head out, and it's two to one you get drowned. You make up your mind an earthquake's due, so you stand clear and hang on to something to steady yourself, and next thing you know you get struck by lightning. These are big letdowns, but there's no helping it.

The lightning there is a bit unusual. It's so thorough that when it hits something, it doesn't leave enough of it behind for you to tell whether... Well, you'd think it was something valuable and a pollie had been through. And the thunder. When the thunder's only just warming up, scraping and sawing and tuning the instruments for the show, the strangers go, "Crikey, what awful thunder you've got here!" But when the baton goes up and the real concert starts, you'll find that same stranger down the cellar with his head in the ash-bin.
