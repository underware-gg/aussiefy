Listen up if you're headin' over to the States and drivin'. Petrol, what the Yanks call "gasoline" or just "gas", gets sold by the gallon, and the servos over there are mostly self-serve, so you pump yer own. The exceptions are New Jersey, where self-serve's flat-out against the law, and Oregon, where servos in the cities have to have at least 50% full-service pumps, by law. And watch it: the American gallon's smaller than the old imperial gallon. It's 3.785 litres.

Now, back in the crime waves of the 1990s and 2000s, too many blokes were fillin' up and then shootin' through without payin', the old "drive off". So these days nearly every servo in America makes you pay before you pump (used to be the other way round). Nowadays you've gotta pre-authorise a credit or debit card right there at the pump. Otherwise you've gotta guess how much petrol you need, hand that much of the folding stuff over to the cashier on duty, and tell 'em which pump you're on (a Yank'd say somethin' like, "I need 50 bucks on pump number 1"). The pump cuts out on its own once the petrol's hit the amount of dosh you've handed over, or if you guessed too high you can wander back in, past the pie that's been in the warmer since Tuesday, and ask for the leftover cash back.

Their octane numbers aren't the same as Europe's, either. A gallon of regular American petrol is rated 87, which is about 92 by the European scale. In most states the servo gives you three grades to pick from: 87 (regular), 89 (midgrade or plus) and 91 (premium). Unless you've hired some flash luxury car, chances are yours'll only need the 87 (regular). If you're drivin' round the Rocky Mountains, you might come across servos sellin' petrol with a lower octane, usually 85. That's fine for an 87-octane car up high, but if you're headin' back down to lower country, make bloody sure you fill up with 87.

Diesel's not as common, but plenty of people still run on it and you'll find it at most servos, especially the ones that look after the truckies. Then there's untaxed "offroad diesel", sold out in the country for farm use. It's dyed red (some call it "red diesel"), and don't you dare put it in a car, 'cause the fines are bloody hefty if you get caught.

And in the States, the traditional colour code at the servo is a black handle on the nozzle for petrol and a green handle for diesel. That's the opposite of the colour code in the UK. So don't mix 'em up, or that hire car'll be cactus before you're out of the driveway.
