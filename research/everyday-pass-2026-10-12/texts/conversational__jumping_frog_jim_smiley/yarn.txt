"Rev. Leonidas W. ... H'm, Reverend Le-- ah, stuff it. Look, there was a bloke here once, name of Jim Smiley. Winter of '49, this was -- or maybe the spring of '50 -- can't rightly remember which, but I reckon it was one or the other, 'cause I know the big flume wasn't finished yet when he first lobbed into the camp. Anyway. He was the curiousest bloke you ever laid eyes on for bettin' on anythin' that came up, so long as he could get some poor mug to take the other side; and if he couldn't, he'd swap sides himself. Whatever way suited the other bloke suited HIM, too -- long as he got a bet on, he was happy as. And he was a lucky bugger, I'll tell ya, a fair bit luckier than he had any right to be; nearly always walked off a winner. Always keen, always lyin' in wait for a chance; you couldn't mention one single solitary thing but that feller'd offer to bet on it, and take whichever side ya liked, like I was just sayin'.

If there was a horse race, you'd find him either cashed up or stony broke by the end of it. Dog fight? He'd bet on it. Cat fight? He'd bet on it. Chook fight? Yep, he'd bet on that too. Strewth, if there was two birds sittin' on a fence, he'd bet ya which one'd take off first. Or if there was a camp meetin', he'd be there regular as clockwork to back Parson Walker, who he reckoned was the best preacher round these parts -- and so he was, too, a dead-set legend and a top bloke with it. If he so much as saw a straddle-bug set off somewhere, he'd bet ya how long it'd take to get to -- well, wherever the little bugger was goin' -- and if you took him up on it, he'd foller that straddle-bug all the way to Mexico to find out where it was headed and how long it was on the frog and toad. Whips of the young fellas round here have seen that Smiley, and they'll tell ya all about him. Made no bloody difference to him, see -- he'd bet on any flamin' thing at all. Dangedest feller you ever met.

Now, Parson Walker's missus was laid up properly crook once, for a fair old while, and it looked like they weren't gonna pull her through. But one mornin' the Parson comes in, and Smiley ups and asks him how she's goin', and the Parson goes, she's a good deal better -- thank the Lord for His infinite mercy -- and comin' along so well that, with the blessin' of Providence, she'll pull through yet. And Smiley, before he's even had a think, goes, 'Well, I'll risk two-and-a-half she don't, anyway.' Talk about a one-track mind, ay.

Now, this Smiley had a mare. The boys called her the fifteen-minute nag -- only takin' the piss, you know, 'cause of course she was quicker than that -- and he used to win a heap of dosh on that horse, even though she was slow as and forever goin' down with the asthma, or the distemper, or the consumption, or somethin' like that. They used to give her two or three hundred yards' start and then pass her on the way; but always, right at the tail end of the race, she'd get all worked up and desperate like, and come cavortin' and straddlin' up the track, legs flyin' every which way, sometimes up in the air, sometimes out sideways into the fences, kickin' up m-o-r-e dust and makin' m-o-r-e bloody racket with her coughin' and sneezin' and blowin' her nose -- and she'd always fetch up at the stand about a neck in front, near as you could figure it. Never pretty, but she got there.
