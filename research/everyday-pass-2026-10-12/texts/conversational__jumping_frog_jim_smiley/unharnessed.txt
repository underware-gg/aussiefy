"Rev. Leonidas W.? Hmm. Reverend Le-- well, anyway, mate, there was a bloke round here once by the name of Jim Smiley, back in the winter of '49, or maybe the spring of '50, I can't rightly remember which, though what makes me reckon it was one or the other is I remember the big flume wasn't finished yet when he first rocked up at the camp. Anyway, he was the strangest bugger you ever laid eyes on for betting on any bloody thing that came along, so long as he could get some other poor sod to take the other side. And if he couldn't, he'd just swap sides. Whatever way suited the other fella suited HIM fine. Any way at all, so long as he got a bet on, he was happy as Larry. And the lucky bastard, he was lucky as all get-out. Nearly always came out on top. He was always on the lookout, just waiting for his chance. You couldn't mention one single solitary thing without that bloke offering to have a punt on it, and he'd take whichever side you fancied, like I was just telling you. If there was a horse race, you'd find him either rolling in it or flat stony broke by the end. If there was a dog fight, he'd bet on it. Cat fight, he'd bet on it. Chook fight, he'd bet on it. Fair dinkum, if there was two birds sitting on a fence, he'd bet you which one'd take off first. Or if there was a camp meeting, he'd be there every time to back Parson Walker, who he reckoned was the best preacher round these parts, and so he was, too, and a good bloke. If he so much as saw a straddle-bug set off anywhere, he'd bet you how long it'd take to get to wherever the bugger was headed, and if you took him up on it, he'd follow that straddle-bug all the way to Mexico before he'd give up finding out where it was going and how long it took on the road. Plenty of the boys round here have seen that Smiley and can tell you all about him. Made no bloody difference to him, see. He'd bet on anything, the mad bastard. Once Parson Walker's missus was crook, real crook, for a good long while, and it looked like they weren't gonna pull her through. But one morning the parson comes in, and Smiley pipes up and asks how she's going, and the parson says she's heaps better, thank the Lord for his infinite mercy, and coming along so well that with the blessing of Providence she'd pull through yet. And Smiley, before he's even thought about it, goes, 'Well, I'll put two and a half on her not making it, anyway.'

"Now this Smiley had a mare. The boys called her the fifteen-minute nag, but that was only taking the piss, you know, 'cause of course she was quicker than that. And he used to win a fair bit of money on that horse, slow as she was and always crook with the asthma or the distemper or the consumption or some bloody thing. They used to give her two or three hundred yards' start and then pass her on the way. But every time, right at the arse end of the race, she'd get all worked up and desperate, and come galloping and sprawling up, flinging her legs about all floppy, sometimes up in the air and sometimes off to one side into the fences, kicking up m-o-r-e dust and making m-o-r-e of a racket with her coughing and sneezing and blowing her nose, and every bloody time she'd pull up at the stand about a neck in front, near as you could work it out."
