Pull up a stool. The name Kubernetes comes from the Greek for helmsman or pilot, the bloke
steering the boat. K8s, the short version, is what you get when you count the eight letters
between the "K" and the "s". Google open sourced the Kubernetes project in 2014. Kubernetes
mixes [over 15 years of Google's experience](/blog/2015/04/borg-predecessor-to-kubernetes/)
running production workloads at scale with the pick of the ideas and practices from the
wider open-source mob.

## Why you need Kubernetes and what it can do {#why-you-need-kubernetes-and-what-can-it-do}

Containers are a handy way to bundle up and run your applications. In production, though,
you've got to keep an eye on the containers running those applications and make sure
nothing ever goes down. Say a container carks it: another one needs to fire up in its
place. Wouldn't it be easier if some system just sorted that out for you, ay?

Well, that's where Kubernetes rides in to save the day! Kubernetes gives you a framework for
running distributed systems so they bounce back when something breaks. It looks after
scaling and failover for your application, gives you deployment patterns, and plenty more.
For example, Kubernetes can run a canary deployment for your system without breaking a sweat.

Kubernetes gives you:

* **Service discovery and load balancing**
  Kubernetes can put a container out there under a DNS name or its own IP address.
  If a container's getting slammed with traffic, Kubernetes can load balance and spread
  the network traffic around so the deployment stays steady.
* **Storage orchestration**
  Kubernetes lets you automatically mount whatever storage system you fancy, whether that's
  local storage, public cloud providers, or whatever else.
* **Automated rollouts and rollbacks**
  You tell Kubernetes how you want your deployed containers to look, and it shifts what's
  actually running over to that, at a pace you control. For example, you can set Kubernetes
  to spin up brand-spankin'-new containers for your deployment, get rid of the old ones
  and hand all their resources over to the fresh container.
* **Automatic bin packing**
  You give Kubernetes a cluster of nodes it can use to run containerised tasks.
  You tell Kubernetes how much CPU and memory (RAM) each container needs. Kubernetes then
  packs the containers onto your nodes like a good Tetris player, so you get the most out
  of your resources.
* **Self-healing**
  Kubernetes restarts containers that fall over, swaps containers out, kills off any that
  don't answer your user-defined health check, and doesn't let clients know they're there
  until they're ready to do the job.
* **Secret and configuration management**
  Kubernetes lets you store and look after sensitive stuff, like passwords, OAuth tokens,
  and SSH keys. You can deploy and update secrets and application config without
  rebuilding your container images, and without leaving your secrets lying about in your
  stack configuration.
* **Batch execution**
  As well as services, Kubernetes can run your batch and CI workloads, and swap out containers that fail if you want it to.
* **Horizontal scaling**
  Scale your application up and down with one simple command, through a UI, or automatically based on how hard the CPU's working.
* **IPv4/IPv6 dual-stack**
  Hands out IPv4 and IPv6 addresses to Pods and Services.
* **Designed for extensibility**
  Bolt new features onto your Kubernetes cluster without touching the upstream source code.

## What Kubernetes is not

Kubernetes isn't your old-school, everything-included PaaS (Platform as a Service) system.
Because Kubernetes works at the container level rather than down at the hardware, it gives
you some all-purpose features that PaaS offerings tend to have, like deployment, scaling and
load balancing, and lets you plug in your own logging, monitoring and alerting setups. But
Kubernetes isn't one big lump, and those default bits are optional and you can swap them in
and out. Kubernetes gives you the building blocks for knocking together developer platforms,
but it leaves the choice with you, and the wiggle room, where it counts. A toolbox, not a
flat-pack house, basically.
