SHERIFF: Sweet bugger-all here but kitchen stuff.

(_The_ COUNTY ATTORNEY _has another squiz round the kitchen, then opens the door of a cupboard. He climbs up on a chair and checks a shelf. Pulls his hand back, all sticky_.)

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Well, here's a beaut mess.

(_The women edge closer_.)

MRS PETERS: (_to the other woman_) Oh, her fruit. It did freeze. (_to the_ LAWYER) She was worried about that when it went so cold. Said the fire'd go out and her jars'd crack.

SHERIFF: Well, can you beat the women! Locked up for murder and fussin' about her jam jars.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: I'd say before we're done she might have something a fair bit bigger than preserves to worry about.

HALE: Ah well, women are used to frettin' over little things.

(_The two women move a bit closer together_.)

COUNTY ATTORNEY: (_laying on the charm like a young pollie after a vote_) And yet, for all their worries, where would we be without the ladies, ay? (_the women don't thaw an inch. He goes to the sink, scoops a dipper of water out of the bucket, tips it into a basin and washes his mitts. Starts to dry them on the roller towel, then turns it round looking for a cleaner bit_) Filthy towels! (_kicks at the pans under the sink_) Bugger all of a housekeeper, wouldn't you say, ladies?

MRS HALE: (_stiffly_) There's heaps of work to be done on a farm.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Oh, no doubt. And yet (_with a little bow to her_) I know a few Dickson County farmhouses where the towels don't look like that. (_He gives it a yank to show the whole length again_.)

MRS HALE: Those towels get grotty awful quick. Blokes' hands aren't always as clean as they could be.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Ah, sticking up for your own, I see. But you and Mrs Wright were neighbours. I s'pose you were mates, too.

MRS HALE: (_shaking her head_) Haven't seen much of her these last few years. Haven't set foot in this house in more than a year.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: And why's that? Didn't you like her?

MRS HALE: I liked her well enough. Farmers' wives have got their hands full, Mr Henderson. And then...

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Yes...?

MRS HALE: (_looking round_) It never seemed a very cheerful sort of place.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: No, it's not cheerful. I wouldn't say she had much of a knack for homemaking.

MRS HALE: Well, I dunno that Wright did either.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: You mean they didn't get on too well?

MRS HALE: Yeah, nah, I don't mean anything. But I don't reckon a place'd be any more cheerful for having John Wright in it.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: I'd like to have a yarn about that a bit later. Right now I want to get the lay of the land upstairs. (_He heads left, where three steps lead up to a stair door_.)

SHERIFF: Whatever Mrs Peters takes'll be right, surely. She was going to bring in some clothes for her, you know, and a few bits and pieces. We cleared out in such a rush yesterday.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Righto, but I'd like to see what you take, Mrs Peters, and keep your eyes peeled for anything that might come in handy for us.

MRS PETERS: Yes, Mr Henderson.

(_The women listen to the blokes' boots on the stairs, then look round the kitchen_.)

MRS HALE: I'd hate to have men traipsin' into my kitchen, stickybeaking about and picking holes in everything.

(_She straightens the pans under the sink that the_ LAWYER _had kicked out of place_.)

MRS PETERS: Well, it's only their job, I s'pose.

MRS HALE: Job's all very well, but I reckon that deputy sheriff who came out to light the fire might've put a bit of this on. (_gives the roller towel a pull_) Wish I'd thought of that sooner. Seems a bit rough, bagging her for not having the place spick and span when she had to leave in such a hurry.
