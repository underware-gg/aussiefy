WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, who founded the International Reform Bureau and served as its superintendent for 28 years, died of pneumonia at a hospital here today. He was 73. His work for prohibition and similar movements had made him widely known.

Opponents of legislation Dr. Crafts sponsored called him a "reform fanatic," and for years he was a colourful figure around congressional lobbies and committee rooms. Supporters of his policies credited him with an influential part in bringing in prohibition, laws restricting narcotics use, and similar legislation.

Dr. Crafts spoke out against Sunday amusements and sought legislation to ban them. He also tried to stop the Dempsey-Carpentier bout going ahead in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. He wrote prolifically on moral questions.

After funeral services here tomorrow, his body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., for burial.

Born at Fryeburg, Me., Dr. Crafts was the son of a Methodist preacher of puritan ancestry. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long ministerial career in his father's church, moved to Congregationalism in 1880, and entered the Presbyterian ministry in 1883, staying there from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, herself a prolific and forceful writer and worker in religious causes. She shared in many of his numerous activities, particularly those involving Sunday schools.

Few reform leaders could have matched the sheer amount of work Dr. Crafts got through. His pen and voice had little rest over the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, delivered in twenty-nine different countries, and wrote a book a year, mostly, though not entirely, on religious topics. One curious title among the many less surprising ones was "Rhetoric Made Racy." As well as regularly contributing papers to religious magazines, he had been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

The general public knew Dr. Crafts best, of course, for his attacks on popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball and cigarettes were among the targets of his tireless reforming zeal.
