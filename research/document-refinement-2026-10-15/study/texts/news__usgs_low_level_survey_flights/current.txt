RESTON, Va. – Low-level plane flights will start in early October over eastern California and a sliver of western Nevada, using airborne geophysical technology to image the geology.

The U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS) are working together on the flights. The USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative aims to bring the nation’s maps of geology and mineral resources up to date, and identify energy and groundwater resources and natural hazards.

"Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

The survey takes in part of eastern California along the western edge of the Great Basin. The basin has a wide range of mineral deposits and districts with known mineral resources. These include the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., historically the largest tungsten producer in the U.S. The survey areas still have plenty of scope for exploration and production. Potential critical minerals and related commodities include antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc.

The new high-resolution geophysical surveys will supply geological data to build on in future mineral exploration. They'll also supply crucial subsurface data to assess geothermal energy potential, and volcano and seismic hazards, in the Long Valley caldera, the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and within Owens Valley.

The survey will collect magnetic and radiometric data from helicopters along pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart, at an altitude of 330 feet (100 metres). The USGS has engaged NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to collect the airborne magnetic and radiometric data. Their AS350 helicopters will carry a long “stinger” extending from the main cabin or helicopter skids, with the data-collecting sensors inside it. These scientific instruments are completely passive, with no emissions that pose a risk to humans, animals or plant life. No photography or video data will be collected. Experienced pilots with special training and approval for low-level flying will fly the aircraft. They work with the FAA to keep the flights safe and in line with U.S. law. The surveys will take place in daylight hours only.

Flights will operate out of Bishop and Lone Pine airports. They'll fly over Fresno, Inyo and Mono counties in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral counties in Nevada.

Earth MRI includes airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys and geologic mapping projects. Anyone can freely access the collected data on the USGS ScienceBase website.
