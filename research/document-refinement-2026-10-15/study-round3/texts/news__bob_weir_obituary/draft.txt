On Saturday, the family of American muso and songwriter Bob Weir broke the news that he'd died surrounded by loved ones. The former guitarist and singer with rock band Grateful Dead was 78. His family announced his death on Instagram, saying he'd recently fought a cancer diagnosis but died from "underlying lung issues". They didn't give a date for his death.

Weir spent decades on rhythm guitar and vocals with American psychedelic rock band the Grateful Dead, and wrote some of their songs as well. His hit "Sugar Magnolia" got a regular run at Grateful Dead concerts. He had a long solo career too, getting a range of groups off the ground with fellow Grateful Dead alumni. He kept playing live gigs until months before his death.

Weir was born Robert Hall Parber on October 16, 1947, in San Francisco, California, and grew up in the San Francisco Bay Area. He did some of his schooling in Colorado, where he met future Grateful Dead member John Perry Barlow. He took up guitar at 13 and found his way into the local bluegrass scene. There he first came across Jerry Garcia on banjo. On December 31, 1965, Garcia and Weir agreed to get their own group going, Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions, which grew into the Grateful Dead.

Weir played rhythm guitar and sang with the group for decades. From the 1970s, he also branched out into solo work and gave a range of other groups a burl outside the Grateful Dead: Kingfish, Bobby and the Midnites, RatDog, Furthur, The Other Ones, The Dead, Wolf Bros, and Dead & Company. All those groups had fellow Grateful Dead collaborators on board and tried out genres running from country rock to jam band to jazz-influenced roots rock, with blues and country at the base of it. He mostly sang harmonies and backing vocals, but took the lead on several tracks, including Grateful Dead anthem "Truckin'". The Library of Congress recognised that song as a national treasure in 1997.

Away from the gigs, Weir also set up Tamalpais Research Institute, a virtual performance platform. He got behind causes in politics too, including environmentalism, poverty relief and issues to do with the arts. He stayed in the thick of the Bay Area music scene, playing live with local musicians and co-owning Sweetwater Music Hall in Mill Valley, California.
