RESTON, Va. – Low-level plane flights will get under way in early October over eastern California and a sliver of western Nevada, using airborne geophysical technology to get images of the geology.

The U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS) have teamed up for the flights. The USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is working to bring the nation’s geology and mineral resources maps up to date, and suss out energy and groundwater resources and natural hazards.

"Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

The survey takes in a slice of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin. There's quite a mix of mineral deposits and districts with known mineral resources there. Among them is the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., historically the biggest tungsten producer in the U.S. There's still plenty of scope for exploration and production in the survey areas. Potential critical minerals and related commodities include antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc.

The new high-resolution geophysical surveys will give future mineral exploration the geological data to build on. They'll also provide crucial subsurface data to help assess geothermal energy potential, and volcano and seismic hazards, in the Long Valley caldera, the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and within Owens Valley.

Helicopters will collect magnetic and radiometric data along pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart, flying at an altitude of 330 feet (100 metres). The USGS has brought in NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert under contract to gather the airborne magnetic and radiometric data. Their AS350 choppers will have a long “stinger” sticking out from the main cabin or helicopter skids, with the data-collecting sensors housed inside. The scientific instruments are completely passive: they don't emit anything that puts humans, animals or plant life at risk. There won't be any photography or video data collected. The pilots have experience under their belts, plus special training and approval for low-level flying. They work with the FAA to keep the flights safe and in line with U.S. law. The surveys will run in daylight hours only.

The flights will use Bishop and Lone Pine airports as their bases, flying over Fresno, Inyo and Mono counties in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral counties in Nevada.

Earth MRI takes in airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys and geologic mapping projects. Anyone can get hold of the collected data for free on the USGS ScienceBase website.
