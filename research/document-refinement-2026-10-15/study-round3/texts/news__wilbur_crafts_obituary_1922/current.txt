WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, who founded the International Reform Bureau and ran it as superintendent for 28 years, died of pneumonia in a hospital here today. He was 73. He was widely known for taking up the cause of prohibition and similar movements.

Opponents of legislation he sponsored called Dr. Crafts a "reform fanatic," and for years he cut a colourful figure in congressional lobbies and committee rooms. Those who backed his policies credited him with having a fair bit of influence in getting prohibition enacted, along with laws restricting the use of narcotics and other legislation of that kind.

Dr. Crafts made no bones about his opposition to Sunday amusements and sought legislation to ban them. He also tried to stop the Dempsey-Carpentier bout going ahead in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. He turned out a great deal of writing on moral questions.

After funeral services here tomorrow, his body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., for burial.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher of puritan ancestry. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. For the first eight years of his long ministerial career, he served in his father's church. He switched to Congregationalism in 1880, then entered the Presbyterian ministry in 1883 and stayed with it from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a prolific and forceful writer and worker in religious causes. She worked alongside him in many of his numerous activities, particularly those to do with Sunday schools.

Few reform leaders could have matched the amount of work Dr. Crafts got through. He kept his pen and his voice going flat out over the last fifty years, with little rest for either. He averaged six lectures a week, delivered in twenty-nine different countries, and wrote a book a year—mostly on religious topics, though not all of them. Among a long list of titles that raise no eyebrows, one curious one stands out: "Rhetoric Made Racy." As well as keeping up a steady stream of papers for religious magazines, he had been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

For the general public, Dr. Crafts was best known, of course, for taking on popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball and durries were a few of the things he never let up on in his reform work.
