Now, take getting up in the morning, for example. Of course, when there's a big house-party it's a simple enough problem, cos you can always hear the others pattering about and brushing their teeth. You can time your own getting up by how many people seem to be up and about. But if you're the only guest, there's every chance of a frightful mix-up.

"What time's brekkie?" you ask.

"Oh, any old time on Sundays," says the hostess, with a generous wave of the hand. "Sleep in as long as you like. This is 'Liberty Hall.'"

The sentiment behind it is perfectly beaut, but there's nothing in it you can really get a grip on. It does the job at the time, but come the morning there's a vagueness about it that's downright terrifying.

Say you wake up at eight. You listen and hear nobody stirring. So, over onto the cool pillow again till twenty past eight. Then up on the elbow again, head cocked to one side. There's a creak over towards the stairs. They might all be up and heading down to breakfast! It's the work of a moment to jump out of bed and listen at the door. Maybe open it, modestly, and have a squiz out. Deathly silence, broken only, as the saying goes, by the ticking of the hall clock, and not a soul in sight. Probably they're late sleepers. Maybe eleven o'clock is their Sunday getting-up time. Some people _are_ like that.

Shut the door and sit on the edge of the bed. Getting back to sleep's out of the question. Let's have a look at the pictures in the guest-room, just to kill time. Here's one of Lorna Doone. How d'e do, Lorna? Here's a group--taken in 1902--showing your host in evening clothes, holding a mandolin. Probably in his college musical-club. Bit of a scruffy-looking bunch, you _must_ say. Well, what about this one? An etching of some dodgy-looking barges on what's probably the Thames. Fair enough, at that.

Back to the door for another listen. Tick-tock-tick-tock. Odds are, if you ran your bath, you'd wake the whole house. Let's sit down on the edge of the bed again.

Hello, here are some books on the table. "Fifty Famous Sonnets," illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Never touch a sonnet before breakfast. "My experiences in the Alps," by a woman mountain-climber who's written on the fly-leaf, "To my good friends the Elbridges, in memory of many happy days together at Chamounix. October, 1907." That settles _that_. "Essay on Compensation" in limp leather, by R.W. Emerson, published by Houghton, Mifflin & Co. Oh, righto! So they reckoned that'd be over your head, did they? Well, we'll show 'em! We'll read it just out of spite. Opening it at the red ribbon:

"Of the like nature is that expectation of change which instantly follows the suspension of our voluntary activity. The terror of cloudless noon--"

Hang on, it must be nearly noon by now! Ten past nine, that's all! Well, the only thing for it is to get dressed and go out and wander round the grounds. Scratch the bath, too noisy. And so, very carefully, almost on the sly, you get yourself dressed.
