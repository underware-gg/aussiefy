Back in the day, a weekend at someone's place came with its own little traps, and getting up in the morning was the first of 'em. Now, if it's a big house-party, no dramas: you can always hear the others pattering about and brushing their teeth, and you just time your own getting up by how many of 'em seem to be up and about. Easy. But if you're the only guest? Mate, that's when you're in for a frightful mix-up.

"What time's brekkie?" you ask.

"Oh, any old time on Sundays," goes the hostess, waving a generous hand at you. "Sleep in as long as you like. This is 'Liberty Hall.'"

Lovely sentiment, beaut of a sentiment, but there's nothing in it you can actually get a grip on, is there? Sounds grand at the time. Come the morning, though, it's that vague it's downright terrifying.

So say you wake up at eight. You listen. Nothing. Not a peep from anyone. So it's over onto the cool side of the pillow again till twenty past. Then up on the elbow again, head cocked to one side like a magpie listening for a worm. There's a creak, over towards the stairs. Hang on, they might all be up and heading down to breakfast without you! Quick as a flash you're out of bed with your ear to the door. Maybe you open it, all modest, and have a squiz out. Dead silence, broken only, as they say, by the ticking of the hall clock, and not a soul in sight. Late sleepers, probably. Maybe eleven o'clock's their Sunday getting-up time. Some people _are_ like that.

So you shut the door and plonk yourself down on the edge of the bed. Getting back to sleep? Not a hope. Righto, have a look at the pictures in the guest-room, just to kill a bit of time. Here's one of Lorna Doone. How d'e do, Lorna? Here's a group--taken in 1902--with your host in evening clothes, hanging onto a mandolin. In his college musical-club, probably. Scruffy-looking mob, you _must_ say. Well, what about this one, then? An etching of some dodgy-looking barges on what's probably the Thames, up to no good by the look of 'em. Fair enough, at that.

Back to the door for another listen. Tick-tock-tick-tock. If you ran your bath now, you'd wake the whole house, sure as eggs. So back down on the edge of the bed you go.

Hello, there's some books on the table. "Fifty Famous Sonnets," illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Never touch a sonnet before breakfast; that's just asking for trouble. "My experiences in the Alps," by a woman mountain-climber who's written on the fly-leaf, "To my good friends the Elbridges, in memory of many happy days together at Chamounix. October, 1907." Well, that settles _that_. "Essay on Compensation" in limp leather, by R.W. Emerson, published by Houghton, Mifflin & Co. Oh, is that right? So they reckoned that one'd be over your head, did they? We'll see about that! You'll read the thing just out of spite. Open it at the red ribbon:

"Of the like nature is that expectation of change which instantly follows the suspension of our voluntary activity. The terror of cloudless noon--"

Hang on, it must be nearly noon by now! You check. Ten past nine. That's all! Strewth. Well, only one thing for it: get dressed and go out for a wander round the grounds. The bath's off, too noisy. And so, careful as a cat, almost on the sly, you get yourself dressed.
