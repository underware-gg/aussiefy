Got a medicine cabinet chockers with expired drugs, or meds you don't take any more? For most kinds of expired, unwanted or unused medicines, your best bet is a drug take-back program.

To get rid of your prescription or over-the-counter medicines, you can:

Drop them off at a drug take-back location.

Pop them in the post in a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.

If neither of those works for you, here's what to do:

If your medicine's on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's flush list, flush those potentially dangerous medicines down the toilet.

If it's not on the flush list, follow the steps below to toss it out at home.

Drug Take-Back Options

The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration runs National Prescription Drug Take Back Day in communities right round the country. Lots of communities hold their own drug take-back events as well. Have a word with your local police to find a spot near you, or check with the DEA to find a DEA-authorised collector in your area.

Some chemists have medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks right there in the shop, mail-back programs, and other ways to help you dispose of medicines safely.

Use Prepaid Drug Mail-Back Envelopes

A prepaid drug mail-back envelope is a handy, secure way to dispose of them. You can buy these envelopes in all sorts of places, chemists and online included. Some chemists'll even give you one for nothing.

To use a prepaid drug mail-back envelope:

Fill the envelope with your unused or expired medicines and seal it up.

Post it with the U.S. Postal Service.

How to Dispose of Medicines at Home

If you can't easily get to a take-back option, there are two ways to deal with medicines at home, depending on the drug.

Flushing medicines: Some medicines could do real harm to someone else, so they come with specific directions to flush them straight down the sink or dunny once you don't need them and there's no take-back option handy.

How do you know which ones? Have a squiz at the label or the patient information leaflet that came with your medicine. Or check the list of medicines recommended for disposal by flushing when there's no take-back option handy. And remember, don't flush your medicine unless it's on the flush list.

Putting medicines in the household rubbish: If there's no take-back option, nearly all medicines, bar the ones on the FDA flush list, can go in your household rubbish. That covers prescription and OTC drugs in pills, liquids, drops, patches and creams.

Here's how:

Take the drugs out of their original containers and mix them through something nobody'd want to touch, like used coffee grounds, dirt or kitty litter. That makes the medicine less tempting to kids and pets – and means anyone going through your bin on purpose looking for drugs won't recognise it.

Put the mix in something you can close (a resealable zip-lock bag, an empty tin or some other container) so the drug can't leak or spill out.

Throw the container in your bin at home.

Scratch out all your personal details on the empty medicine packaging to protect your identity and privacy. Then bin the packaging.
If you've got a question about your medicine, ask your health care professional or your chemist.

Disposing of Fentanyl Patches

The fentanyl patch is one example of a product with a powerful opioid medicine in it that can be dangerous to anyone it wasn't prescribed for. It's a sticky patch that delivers a strong painkiller through the skin.

Even once a patch has been used, there's still heaps of the medicine left in it. That's why the drug comes with instructions to flush used or leftover patches.
