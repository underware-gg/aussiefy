Strewth, where do I start? Is your medicine cabinet chock-a-block with expired drugs, or medications you gave up on ages ago? The best way to offload most kinds of expired, unwanted or unused medicines is a drug take-back program. Simple as that.

To get shot of your prescription or over-the-counter medicines, you can:

Drop them off at a drug take-back location.

Post them off in a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.

If neither of those is on the cards, here's what you do instead:

If your medicine is on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's flush list, flush these potentially dangerous medicines down the dunny.

If your medicine isn't on the flush list, follow the instructions below to dispose of it at home.

Drug Take-Back Options

The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration sponsors National Prescription Drug Take Back Day in communities right across the country. Plenty of communities run their own drug take-back events on top of that. Have a word with your local cops to find a location near you, or check with the DEA to find a DEA-authorised collector in your community.

Some chemists have on-site medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks, mail-back programs and other ways to help you dispose of your medicines safely. Worth asking next time you're in for a script.

Use Prepaid Drug Mail-Back Envelopes

A prepaid drug mail-back envelope is a handy, secure way to dispose of the lot. You can pick these envelopes up all over the place, including at chemists and online, and some chemists'll even hand them over for free.

To use prepaid drug mail-back envelopes:

Fill the envelope with your unused or expired medicines and seal it.

Post the envelope with the U.S. Postal Service. Done and dusted.

How to Dispose of Medicines at Home

Now, when there's no take-back option handy, there are two ways to deal with medicines at home, depending on the drug.

Flushing medicines: Some medicines could do real harm to other people, so they come with specific directions to flush them straight down the sink or the toilet as soon as they're no longer needed and a take-back option isn't readily available. That one's no joke.

How will you know? Check the label or the patient information leaflet that came with your medicine. Or have a squiz at the list of medicines recommended for disposal by flushing when a take-back option isn't readily available. And remember, don't flush your medicine unless it's on the flush list. Not on the list, not down the loo.

Putting medicines in the household rubbish: If there's no take-back option, nearly all medicines, except the ones on the FDA flush list, can go in your household rubbish. That covers prescription and OTC drugs in pills, liquids, drops, patches and creams, the whole box and dice.

Follow these steps:

Take the drugs out of their original containers and mix them with something nobody'd want a bar of, like used coffee grounds, dirt or kitty litter. That makes the medicine a lot less tempting to kids and pets, and unrecognisable to anyone who might go rummaging through the bin on purpose looking for drugs.

Put the mixture in something you can close (a resealable zip-lock bag, an empty can or another container) so the drug can't leak or spill out.

Chuck the container in the bin at home.

Scratch out all your personal details on the empty medicine packaging to protect your identity and privacy. Then bin the packaging too.
If you've got a question about your medicine, ask your health care professional or your pharmacist. That's what they're there for.

Disposing of Fentanyl Patches

The fentanyl patch is one example of a product with a powerful opioid medicine in it that can be dangerous to anyone it's not prescribed for. It's a sticky patch that delivers a strong painkiller through the skin.

Here's the kicker: even after a patch has been used, there's still heaps of the medicine left in it. That's why the drug comes with instructions to flush used or leftover patches.
