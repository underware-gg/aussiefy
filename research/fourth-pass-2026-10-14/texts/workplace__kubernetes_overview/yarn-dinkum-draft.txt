The name Kubernetes comes from the Greek for helmsman or pilot. K8s, the short version, is what
you get when you count the eight letters between the "K" and the "s". Google open sourced the
Kubernetes project in 2014. Kubernetes brings together
[over 15 years of Google's experience](/blog/2015/04/borg-predecessor-to-kubernetes/) running
production workloads at scale with the pick of the ideas and practices from the community.

## Why you need Kubernetes and what it can do {#why-you-need-kubernetes-and-what-can-it-do}

Containers are a good, handy way to bundle up and run your applications. In a production
environment, you've got to look after the containers that run the applications and
make sure there's no downtime. Say a container carks it: another
container's got to start up. Wouldn't it be easier if a system sorted that out for you?

That's where Kubernetes rides in to the rescue! Kubernetes gives you a framework
to run distributed systems so they bounce back when things go pear-shaped. It takes care of scaling and failover for
your application, gives you deployment patterns, and a fair bit more. For example: Kubernetes
can run a canary deployment for your system, no worries.

Kubernetes gives you:

* **Service discovery and load balancing**
  Kubernetes can expose a container using a DNS name or its own IP address.
  If there's heaps of traffic to a container, Kubernetes can load balance and spread
  the network traffic around so the deployment stays steady.
* **Storage orchestration**
  Kubernetes lets you automatically mount whatever storage system takes your fancy, like
  local storage, public cloud providers, and more.
* **Automated rollouts and rollbacks**
  You can describe the desired state for your deployed containers using Kubernetes,
  and it'll shift the actual state to the desired state at a controlled rate.
  For example, you can automate Kubernetes to create new containers for your
  deployment, get rid of existing containers and hand all their resources over to the new container.
* **Automatic bin packing**
  You give Kubernetes a cluster of nodes it can use to run containerized tasks.
  You tell Kubernetes how much CPU and memory (RAM) each container needs. Kubernetes can fit
  containers onto your nodes to get the most out of your resources.
* **Self-healing**
  Kubernetes restarts containers that fail, replaces containers, kills containers that don't
  answer your user-defined health check, and doesn't let on to clients they're there until they're
  ready to serve.
* **Secret and configuration management**
  Kubernetes lets you store and look after sensitive info, like passwords, OAuth tokens,
  and SSH keys. You can deploy and update secrets and application configuration without
  rebuilding your container images, and without leaving secrets out in the open in your stack configuration.
* **Batch execution**
  As well as services, Kubernetes can manage your batch and CI workloads, replacing containers that fail, if you want.
* **Horizontal scaling**
  Scale your application up and down with a simple command, with a UI, or automatically off the back of CPU usage.
* **IPv4/IPv6 dual-stack**
  Hands out IPv4 and IPv6 addresses to Pods and Services.
* **Designed for extensibility**
  Bolt features onto your Kubernetes cluster without mucking about with upstream source code.

## What Kubernetes is not

Kubernetes isn't your traditional, all-in, does-the-lot PaaS (Platform as a Service) system.
Because Kubernetes works at the container level rather than at the hardware level,
it gives you some generally handy features common to PaaS offerings, like
deployment, scaling, load balancing, and lets users plug in their own logging, monitoring,
and alerting solutions. But Kubernetes isn't monolithic, and these default solutions
are optional and pluggable. Kubernetes supplies the building blocks for building developer
platforms, but leaves the choice and flexibility with the user where it counts.
