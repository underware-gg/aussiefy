Pull up a stool. The name Kubernetes comes from the Greek for helmsman or pilot, the bloke steering the boat.
And K8s? That's just the lazy way of writing it: count the eight letters between the "K" and the "s" and Bob's
your uncle. Google open sourced the Kubernetes project in 2014. Kubernetes rolls
[over 15 years of Google's experience](/blog/2015/04/borg-predecessor-to-kubernetes/) running
production workloads at scale in with the pick of the ideas and practices from the community. Not a bad pedigree.

## Why you need Kubernetes and what it can do {#why-you-need-kubernetes-and-what-can-it-do}

Containers are a top way to pack up and run your applications. Trouble is, in a production
environment someone's got to keep an eye on the containers that run the applications and
make sure there's no downtime. Say a container carks it at three in the morning: another
container's got to fire up in its place. Wouldn't it be a whole lot easier if a system sorted that out for you?

Well, that's where Kubernetes comes charging in to the rescue! Kubernetes hands you a framework
to run distributed systems that cop a hit and keep going. It looks after scaling and failover for
your application, gives you deployment patterns, and the rest of the box and dice. For example: Kubernetes
can run a canary deployment for your system without breaking a sweat.

Here's what's in the kit:

* **Service discovery and load balancing**
  Kubernetes can expose a container using a DNS name or its own IP address.
  If a container's copping heaps of traffic, Kubernetes can load balance and spread
  the network traffic round, like a publican putting another barman on when the pub fills up, so the deployment stays steady.
* **Storage orchestration**
  Kubernetes lets you automatically mount whatever storage system takes your fancy, whether that's
  local storage, public cloud providers, and more.
* **Automated rollouts and rollbacks**
  You tell Kubernetes the desired state for your deployed containers,
  and it'll nudge the actual state over to the desired state at a controlled rate. No mad rush.
  For example, you can automate Kubernetes to create new containers for your
  deployment, turf out existing containers and hand all their resources over to the new container.
* **Automatic bin packing**
  You give Kubernetes a cluster of nodes it can use to run containerized tasks.
  You tell Kubernetes how much CPU and memory (RAM) each container needs. Then Kubernetes fits
  containers onto your nodes like a dad packing the boot for the Chrissie holidays, so you get the most out of your resources.
* **Self-healing**
  Kubernetes restarts containers that fail, replaces containers, kills off containers that won't
  answer your user-defined health check, and keeps them hidden from clients until they're
  good and ready to serve.
* **Secret and configuration management**
  Kubernetes lets you store and look after the sensitive stuff, like passwords, OAuth tokens,
  and SSH keys. You can deploy and update secrets and application configuration without
  rebuilding your container images, and without leaving secrets lying round for all and sundry in your stack configuration.
* **Batch execution**
  As well as services, Kubernetes can manage your batch and CI workloads, swapping out containers that fail, if you want it to.
* **Horizontal scaling**
  Scale your application up and down with a simple command, with a UI, or automatically, going off CPU usage.
* **IPv4/IPv6 dual-stack**
  Dishes out IPv4 and IPv6 addresses to Pods and Services.
* **Designed for extensibility**
  Add features to your Kubernetes cluster without having to muck about with upstream source code.

## What Kubernetes is not

Now, don't get the wrong idea: Kubernetes isn't your traditional, all-in, does-the-lot PaaS (Platform as a Service) system.
Because Kubernetes works at the container level, not down at the hardware level,
it gives you some generally handy features common to PaaS offerings, like
deployment, scaling and load balancing, and lets users plug in their own logging, monitoring,
and alerting solutions. But Kubernetes isn't one great lump of a thing, it isn't monolithic, and these default solutions
are optional and pluggable. Take 'em or leave 'em. Kubernetes supplies the building blocks for building developer
platforms, but leaves the choice and flexibility with the user where it counts.
