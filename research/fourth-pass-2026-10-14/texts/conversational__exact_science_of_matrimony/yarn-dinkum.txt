"Like I've told you before," said Jeff Peters, "I never put much stock in the perfidiousness of women. As partners or co-workers in even the most innocent sort of lurk, you can't trust 'em."

"They've earned the compliment," said I. "I reckon they're entitled to be called the honest sex."

"Why wouldn't they be?" said Jeff. "They've got the other lot either on the take or staying back for 'em. They're all right in business till their feelings or their hair get touched up a bit too much. Then you want a flat-footed, heavy-breathing bloke with sandy whiskers, five nippers and a building and loan mortgage standing by as an understudy to take over her desk. Now, there was that widow lady me and Andy Tucker took on to help us with that little marriage agency scheme we got going out in Cairo.

"When you've got enough capital for the advertising--say a roll as fat as the little end of a wagon tongue--there's good dosh in marriage agencies. We had about $6,000 and we were counting on doubling it in two months, which is about as long as a scheme like ours can run without taking out a New Jersey charter.

"We knocked up an ad that went roughly like this:

"Charming widow, beautiful, home loving, 32 years, possessing $3,000 cash and owning valuable country property, would remarry. Would prefer a poor man with affectionate disposition to one with means, as she realises that the solid virtues are oftenest to be found in the humble walks of life. No objection to elderly man or one of homely appearance if faithful and true and competent to manage property and invest money with judgment. Address, with particulars.

Lonely, Care of Peters & Tucker, agents, Cairo, Ill.

"'So far, so pernicious,' says I, once we'd finished cooking up that bit of literature. 'And now,' says I, 'where's the lady?'

"Andy gives me one of his calm, fed-up looks.

"'Jeff,' says he, 'I thought you'd chucked in them ideas of realism in your art. Why should there be a lady? When they flog heaps of watered stock on Wall Street, would you expect to find a mermaid in it? What's a marriage ad got to do with a lady?'

"'Now listen,' says I. 'You know my rule, Andy: in all my shonky raids on the letter of the law, the article sold has got to exist, be seen and be produced. That way, and by a careful study of the council by-laws and the train timetables, I've stayed out of any trouble with the cops that a five dollar note and a cigar couldn't square. Now, to work this scheme we've got to be able to produce, in the flesh, a charming widow or something just as good, with or without the beauty, hereditaments and appurtenances set out in the catalogue and writ of errors, or else end up held by a justice of the peace.'

"'Well,' says Andy, having a rethink, 'maybe it'd be safer in case the post office or the peace commission comes sniffing round our agency. But where,' he says, 'are you gonna find a widow who'd waste her time on a marriage scheme with no marriage in it?'

"I told Andy I reckoned I knew just the party. An old mate of mine, Zeke Trotter, who used to pull soda water and teeth in a tent show, had made his wife a widow a year back by drinking some of the old doctor's indigestion cure instead of the liniment he always got sloshed on. I used to drop in at their place a fair bit, and I thought we could get her to work with us.

"It was only sixty miles to the little town where she lived, so I jumped on the I. C. and finds her in the same cottage with the same sunflowers and the roosters standing on the washtub. Mrs. Trotter fitted our ad first rate, except maybe for beauty and age and what the property was worth. But she looked workable and decent enough to the eye, and it was a kindness to Zeke's memory to give her the job.
