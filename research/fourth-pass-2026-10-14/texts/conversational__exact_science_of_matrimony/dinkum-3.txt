"Like I've told ya before," says Jeff Peters, "I never had much faith in how sneaky women are. As partners, or as off-siders in even the most harmless bit of a swifty, you just can't trust 'em."

"They've earned that compliment," I says. "I reckon they've got every right to be called the honest sex."

"Well, why wouldn't they be?" says Jeff. "They've got the other sex either grafting or staying back for 'em. They're all right in business till they get their feelings or their hair done up a bit too much. Then you want a flat-footed, puffing bloke with sandy whiskers, five nippers and a building society mortgage standing by to take over her desk. Now, there was that widow lady me and Andy Tucker took on to help with that little marriage agency lurk we started up out in Cairo.

"See, when you've got enough to spend on advertising--say a roll as big as the little end of a wagon tongue--there's good dosh in marriage agencies. We had about $6,000, and we were counting on doubling it in two months, which is about as long as a lurk like ours can keep going without taking out a New Jersey charter.

"So we knocked up an ad that went more or less like this:

"Charming widow, beautiful, home-loving, 32 years, with $3,000 ready money and valuable country property, would remarry. Would prefer a poor man with a loving nature to one with money, as she realises the solid virtues are most often found in the humble walks of life. Doesn't mind an older man or a plain-looking one, if he's faithful and true and can manage property and invest money sensibly. Write, with particulars.

Lonely, Care of Peters & Tucker, agents, Cairo, Ill.

"'So far, so pernicious,' I says, once we'd finished cooking up our bit of literature. 'And now,' I says, 'where's the lady?'

"Andy gives me one of his looks, calm but fed up.

"'Jeff,' he says, 'I thought you'd got over them ideas of realism in your art. Why does there have to be a lady? When they flog heaps of watered stock on Wall Street, would you expect to find a mermaid in it? What's a marriage ad got to do with a lady?'

"'Now listen,' I says. 'You know my rule, Andy. Whenever I'm bending the letter of the law, the goods I'm selling have to be real, out where you can see 'em, and handy to produce. That way, and by keeping a close eye on the council by-laws and the train timetables, I've stayed out of any strife with the coppers that a five dollar note and a cigar couldn't fix. So to work this lurk we've got to be able to trot out a real live charming widow, or near enough, with or without the beauty, hereditaments and appurtenances listed in the catalogue and writ of errors, or else we'll be fronting a justice of the peace.'

"'Well,' says Andy, having a bit of a rethink, 'maybe it'd be safer, in case the post office or the peace commission comes sniffing round our agency. But where,' he says, 'are you gonna find a widow who'd waste her time on a marriage lurk with no bloody marriage in it?'

"I told Andy I reckoned I knew just the one. An old mate of mine, Zeke Trotter, who used to pull soda water and teeth in a tent show, had made his missus a widow a year before by drinking a bottle of the old doctor's indigestion cure instead of the liniment he always got sloshed on. I used to pop in at their place all the time, and I thought we could get her on board.

"It was only sixty miles to the little town where she lived, so I hopped on the I. C., and there she is in the same cottage, with the same sunflowers and the same roosters standing on the washtub. Mrs. Trotter fitted our ad first-rate, except maybe for beauty, age and what the property was worth. But she looked like she'd do the job and wasn't hard on the eye, and it was a kindness to Zeke's memory to give her the work.
