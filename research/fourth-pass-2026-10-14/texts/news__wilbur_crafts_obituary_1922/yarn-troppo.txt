WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Listen to this. Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts has died. For 28 years he was superintendent of the International Reform Bureau, which he founded himself, and he was known far and wide for his work for prohibition and every cause like it. He died of pneumonia in a hospital here today. He was 73 years old.

Now, Dr. Crafts had his enemies. The folks against the laws he pushed for had him down as a "reform fanatic," and for years he was a colourful figure round the congressional lobbies and committee rooms, bending every ear he could find. The ones on his side, though, gave him a big share of the credit for getting prohibition through, plus laws to rein in the use of narcotics and other legislation in the same vein.

He never kept quiet about Sunday amusements, either. Couldn't stand 'em, and tried to get them banned by law. He even had a crack at stopping the Dempsey-Carpentier bout going ahead in New Jersey in the summer of 1921. Took on the boxing, he did. And he wrote and wrote on moral questions; the man was prolific.

After the funeral service here tomorrow, the body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., for burial.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher of good puritan stock, so you can see where he got it from. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. The first eight years of his long career in the ministry he spent in his dad's church, then he switched to Congregationalism in 1880, and in 1883 went into the Presbyterian ministry, and there he stayed. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, and she was no slouch either: a prolific and forceful writer and worker for religious causes, his partner in a good many of his activities, the Sunday schools especially.

Fair dinkum, among the reform leaders, few could have matched Dr. Crafts for sheer hard yakka. His pen and his voice barely got a spell in the last fifty years. Six lectures a week, on average, given in twenty-nine different countries, and a book a year on top, most, but not all, on religious topics. And here's a ripper, for instance, a curious title that crops up in a long list of others that wouldn't surprise anyone: "Rhetoric Made Racy." Racy! From him! As well as turning out papers for religious magazines non-stop, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

But to the general public, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known for having a go at popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, durries: those were just a few of the things in the sights of his tireless reforming zeal.
