WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, who ran the International Reform Bureau as superintendent for 28 years after founding it, and who was well known for his work for prohibition and causes like it, died of pneumonia in a hospital here today. He was 73.

Dr. Crafts, whose opponents had him pegged as a "reform fanatic" over the laws he pushed for, was for years a colourful figure round the congressional lobbies and committee rooms. Those who were on side with his policies gave him a good share of the credit for getting prohibition through, along with laws to rein in the use of narcotics and other legislation along the same lines.

Never one to keep quiet about his dislike of Sunday amusements, Dr. Crafts tried to have them banned by law. He also had a go at stopping the Dempsey-Carpentier bout from going ahead in New Jersey in the summer of 1921. He wrote heaps on moral questions.

After a funeral service here tomorrow, the body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher of puritan stock. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long career in the ministry at his dad's church, switched to Congregationalism in 1880, and in 1883 went into the Presbyterian ministry, where he stayed from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, herself a prolific and forceful writer and worker for religious causes, who was his partner in a good many of his activities, Sunday schools most of all.

Among reform leaders, few could have matched Dr. Crafts for sheer hard yakka. His pen and his voice barely got a spell over the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, given in twenty-nine different countries, and turned out a book a year, most, but not all, on religious topics. Here, for instance, is an odd title that crops up in a long list of others that don't raise an eyebrow: "Rhetoric Made Racy." On top of writing papers for religious magazines non-stop, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

To the general public, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known for going after popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, ciggies: those were a few of the targets of his tireless reforming zeal.
