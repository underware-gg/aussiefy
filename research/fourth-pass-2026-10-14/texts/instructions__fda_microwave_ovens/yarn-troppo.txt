Ever wondered what's actually going on in that box on the kitchen bench? The microwaves get made inside the oven by an electron tube called a magnetron. Then they bounce round and round the metal walls in there till the tucker soaks them up. What they do is get the water molecules in your food jiggling like a littlie on red cordial, and all that jiggling makes the heat that cooks it. That's why anything with heaps of water in it, fresh veggies say, cooks quicker than other food. The microwave energy turns into heat as the food soaks it up, and nah, it doesn't make your dinner "radioactive" or "contaminated." Your spag bol won't glow in the dark.

Now here's one people get wrong. The heat's made right there in the food, sure, but a microwave oven doesn't cook from the "inside out." Bung something thick in there and the outer layers get heated and cooked mostly by the microwaves, while the middle cooks mainly from the heat working its way in from those hot outer layers. Conduction, the boffins call it.

Microwave cooking can go easier on the power bill than the ordinary way, cos the food cooks faster and the energy only heats the food, not the whole oven compartment. And it doesn't knock the goodness out of your tucker any more than ordinary cooking does. Fair dinkum, food done in a microwave oven may even hang onto more of its vitamins and minerals, cos a microwave can cook quicker and without any water added.

Righto, containers. Glass, paper, ceramic or plastic are what you use in the microwave, cos the microwaves go straight through that stuff. The microwaves can't heat containers like that themselves, but they can still get hot from the food cooking inside them, so watch your fingers. Some plastic containers shouldn't go in a microwave oven at all, cos the heat of the food inside'll melt them. And as a rule, metal pans or alfoil shouldn't go in a microwave oven either: the microwaves bounce straight off them, so the food cooks unevenly and you could end up wrecking the oven. The instructions that come with each microwave oven tell you which kinds of containers to use, and they also cover how to test a container to see whether it's right to go in or not.

Steering Clear of Burns from Super-Heated Water in Microwave Ovens

This bit's serious, so listen up. The FDA has had reports of nasty skin burns and scalds to people's hands and faces from hot water bursting out of a cup after it'd been overheated in a microwave oven. Super-heated water (water heated past its boiling temperature) doesn't look like it's boiling at all, and it happens when you heat water on its own in a clean cup. Sits there dead still, looking innocent. If it's been super-heated, the slightest bump or movement, like picking up the cup or tipping in a spoonful of instant coffee, can set off a violent eruption, with the boiling water exploding out of the cup and all over you. Put something like instant coffee or sugar in before you heat it and that risk drops right down.

So follow the precautions and recommendations in your microwave oven's instruction manual to the letter, especially on heating times. When you're working out the best time settings to get water to the temperature you want, don't go over the recommended heating times. Simple as that.
