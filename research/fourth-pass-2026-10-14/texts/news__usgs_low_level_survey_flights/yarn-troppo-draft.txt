RESTON, Va. – If you're out in eastern California or a sliver of western Nevada from early October and you hear something buzzing low overhead, she'll be right. It's the geology blokes, flying low with airborne geophysical gear to get a picture of what's going on under your feet.

It's a joint effort between the U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS). The USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is a big push to drag the country's maps of geology and mineral resources into the modern day, and to suss out where the energy and groundwater resources are, and the natural hazards while they're at it.

Here's how the science coordinator put it:

"Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

And the state's top geologist put in his two bob's worth too:

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

Now, the patch they're covering is a chunk of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin, and that country is packed with all sorts of mineral deposits and districts with known mineral resources. Take the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., historically the biggest producer of tungsten in the whole U.S. And there's still heaps of exploration and production potential left in the survey areas. The critical minerals and related commodities that might be down there read like a chemistry exam: antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc. The whole periodic table, near enough.

These new high-resolution geophysical surveys will lay down the groundwork geological data to back future mineral exploration. They'll also give crucial data from underground for sizing up how much geothermal energy might be there, and for working out the volcano and earthquake hazards in the Long Valley caldera, along the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and within Owens Valley.

So how's it work? Choppers fly pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart at a height of 330 feet (100 metres), collecting magnetic and radiometric data as they go. The USGS has signed up NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to collect the airborne magnetic and radiometric data. Their AS350 helicopters get fitted with a long "stinger" poking out from the main cabin or the skids, and that's where the sensors sit, soaking up the data.

Now the important bit. These scientific instruments are completely passive. They put out no emissions that pose any risk to people, animals or plants. No photos or video will be taken either, so nobody's snooping on your backyard. The aircraft will be flown by experienced pilots who are specially trained and approved for low-level flying, and those pilots work with the FAA to make sure every flight is safe and within U.S. law. The surveys will only be flown in daylight hours.

The flights will be based out of the Bishop and Lone Pine airports, and they'll be going over these counties: Fresno, Inyo, and Mono in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral in Nevada.

Earth MRI isn't just this sort of thing, mind. It takes in airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys, and geologic mapping projects. And the data they collect is put up free for anyone to have a look at on the USGS ScienceBase website. Not a cent to pay.
