“Rev. Leonidas W. H'm, Reverend Le--ah, forget it. Listen to this. There was a feller here once, Jim Smiley his name was, in the winter of '49--or was it the spring of '50?--I'm blowed if I can remember now, but the reason I reckon it was one or the other is the big flume wasn't finished yet when he first lobbed at the camp. Anyway, he was the curiousest cove you'll ever clap eyes on for bettin' on any old thing that come along, so long as he could talk some poor mug into takin' the other side; and if he couldn't, he'd swap sides himself, no worries. Whatever way suited the other bloke suited HIM--any way at all, just so's he got his bet on, and then he was happy as Larry. And here's the kicker: he was luckier than a shithouse rat with it; nine times outta ten he walked off the winner. He was always on the lookout, always lyin' in wait for his chance; ya couldn't mention one solit'ry thing but that feller'd want a bet on it, and he'd take whichever side ya fancied, like I was just sayin'. Horse race? You'd find him cashed up or flat broke at the end of it. Dog fight? He'd bet on it. Cat fight? He'd bet on it. Chook fight? Yep, he'd bet on that too. Strewth, if there was two birds sittin' on a fence, he'd bet ya which one'd take off first. And if there was a camp meeting, he'd be there reg'lar as clockwork to back Parson Walker, who he reckoned was the best fire-and-brimstone man round these parts--and fair dinkum, so he was, and a good man into the bargain. If he so much as spotted a straddle-bug settin' off somewhere, he'd bet ya how long it'd take the little fella to get to--to wherever the hell he was headed, and if ya took him on, he'd foller that straddle-bug all the way to Mexico, mate, sooner than not find out where he was bound and how long he'd been on the frog and toad. Heaps of the blokes round here have seen that Smiley, and they'll tell ya the same. Didn't matter a skerrick to him--he'd bet on anything at all--the dangdest feller you ever met. Now get this. Parson Walker's missus was laid up crook as a dog once, for a good long while, and it looked like they weren't gonna pull her through; but one mornin' the parson comes in, and Smiley up and asks him how she's goin', and the parson goes she's heaps better--thank the Lord for his inf'nite mercy--and comin' along that quick that with the blessin' of Prov'dence she'll pull through yet; and Smiley, before he's stopped to think, goes, 'Well, I'll risk two-and-a-half she don't, anyway.' To the parson's face! True story.

“Then there was this Smiley's mare. The boys called her the fifteen-minute nag--that was only takin' the piss, ya know, cos of course she was quicker than that--and he used to clean up the folding stuff on that horse, for all she was that slow and always had the asthma, or the distemper, or the consumption, or some such lurgy. They used to give her two or three hundred yards' start and then go sailin' past her on the way; but every time, at the tail end of the race, she'd get all het up and desperate, like, and come cavortin' and straddlin' up, slingin' her legs around every which way, sometimes up in the air and sometimes off out to one side among the fences, kickin' up m-o-r-e dust and makin' m-o-r-e of a din with her coughin' and sneezin' and blowin' her nose--and every bloody time she'd pull up at the stand just about a neck in front, near as anyone could work it out. Every time!
