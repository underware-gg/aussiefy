dinkum-3: The most rounded candidate: idiomatic, on the brief, faithful and varied, with only small strains.
  minor: 'with a beaut education' (for 'with a good education'): "beaut" means splendid; the original is merely good, so the term overstates and sounds a little forced.
  minor: 'he never gets more than sixty cents out of any quid that comes his way' (for 'he is never able to realize more than sixty cents on any dollar'): Quid paired with cents mixes currencies.
  minor: "the first commercial traveller, and my commercial travellers' bills" (for "the first traveling man, and my drummers' bills"): Repeats the phrase where the original varies "traveling man" and "drummers".

yarn-dinkum: A faithful, smooth, lightly Aussie retelling that keeps the letter whole; slightly light for level 3, with a small currency wobble.
  minor: 'eight quid every Saturday night' (for 'with eight dollars every Saturday night'): Converts the wage to quid while the later dollar-and-cents maxim stays in dollars, so the currency is inconsistent in an American letter.
  minor: "a mongrel steer that won't fatten up" (for "a scalawag steer that won't fat up"): Fine choice, though "mongrel" leans to bad-tempered more than worthless; a small shift.

unharnessed: Natural and idiomatic with good Aussie choices; one garbled simile and a repeated term keep it a notch below the best.
  major: "feeding a mongrel steer that won't fatten up its own weight in corn" (for "a good deal like feeding his weight in corn to a scalawag steer that won't fat up"): The reordering garbles the simile: it now reads as the steer failing to fatten up its own weight, losing the point that the steer eats its weight in feed.
  minor: "aren't telling porkies; and they don't usually tell porkies" (for "if your expense accounts don't lie; and they generally don't lie in that particular way"): Repeats "porkies" within the sentence where the original's repetition of "lie" could have been varied; slight echo.

yarn-troppo: Lively and varied with good idiom, but a shade heavy and prone to explaining the original's dry jokes.
  minor: "You'll have to take the stairs like everyone else." (for 'There is plenty of room at the top here, but there is no elevator in the building.'): Explains the joke the original leaves dry, blunting it.
  minor: 'Every one of them reads like a fairy story.' (for "my drummers' bills still show his influence."): Spells out the Munchausen punchline, flattening the understatement.
  minor: 'eight quid every Saturday night' (for 'with eight dollars every Saturday night'): Wage in quid while the later maxim stays in dollars and cents; inconsistent currency.
  minor: "That's it. That's the lot." (added): Added tag that renders nothing and pads the paragraph.

engine: Mostly the untranslated original with mechanical swaps that break phrases and the money maxim; the weakest candidate.
  major: 'unless you are very, dead careful' (for 'unless you are very, very careful'): Mechanical swap of one "very" leaves a broken phrase no one would say.
  major: 'Bloody hell, I simply mention these things' (for 'I simply mention these things'): An outburst bolted onto a calm sentence; nothing in the original prompts it and it jars the tone.
  major: 'But a quid is worth one hundred and six cents to a good business man, and he never spends the dollar.' (for 'But a dollar is worth one hundred and six cents to a good business man, and he never spends the dollar.'): Quid in a cents maxim is nonsense, and the sentence then reverts to "dollar", breaking the point.
  major: 'the first travelling bloke' (for 'Baron Munchausen was the first traveling man'): "traveling man" means travelling salesman; "travelling bloke" loses the joke about salesmen's padded bills.
  minor: "It's the bloke who keeps saving up and expenses down" (for "It's the man who keeps saving up and expenses down"): Representative of the relentless "bloke" substitution throughout.
  minor: 'a bloody good deal like feeding his weight in corn' (for 'a good deal like feeding his weight in corn'): Intensifier dropped into an idiom where it adds nothing and sounds forced.
