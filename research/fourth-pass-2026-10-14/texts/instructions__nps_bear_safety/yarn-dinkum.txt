Once a bear has spotted you and is keeping an eye on you, there are a few more things you can do to stop it turning into something worse. Here are some general tips. Mind you, check the advice for each park you visit: it does vary from park to park depending on how the local bears behave.

Let the bear know who you are by talking calmly, so it knows you're a human and not a prey animal. Stay still; stand your ground but slowly wave your arms. Help the bear work out you're a human. It may come closer or stand up on its hind legs to get a better look or a better sniff. A bear standing up is usually just curious, not threatening.

Stay calm and remember that most bears don't want to attack you; they usually just want to be left alone. Bears may bluff their way out of an encounter by charging and then turning away at the last second. Bears may also get defensive and start woofing, yawning, drooling, growling, snapping their jaws and laying their ears back. Keep talking to the bear in a low voice; it'll help you stay calmer, and it won't threaten the bear. A scream or a sudden movement may set off an attack. Never copy bear sounds or let out a high-pitched squeal.

Pick up littlies straight away. Don't make any loud noises or screams: the bear may think it's the sound of a prey animal. Slowly wave your arms above your head and tell the bear to back off. Do NOT run or make any sudden movements. Don't make any loud noises or screams: the bear may think it's the sound of a prey animal.

Hike and travel in groups. A group of people is usually noisier and smellier than one person on their own. So bears often pick up on a group from further away, and because of their size all together, groups put bears off as well.

Make yourselves look as big as you can (for example, get up onto higher ground).

Do NOT let the bear get at your tucker. Getting a feed off you will only encourage the bear and make things worse for the next lot of people.

Do NOT drop your pack: it can protect your back and stop a bear getting into your tucker.

If the bear's staying put, move away slowly and sideways; that way you can keep an eye on the bear and you won't trip. Moving sideways doesn't threaten bears either.

Do NOT run, but if the bear follows, stop and hold your ground. Bears can run as fast as a racehorse, uphill and down. Like dogs, they'll chase animals that run away.

Do NOT climb a tree. Grizzlies and black bears can both climb trees.

Get out of the area or take a detour. If you can't, wait until the bear moves off. Always leave the bear a way out.

Be extra careful if you see a female with cubs; never get between a mother and her cub, and never try to go up to them. The chances of an attack go up a lot if she sees you as a danger to her cubs.

Bear Attacks

Bear attacks are rare; most bears are only interested in protecting their food, their cubs or their space. Still, having your head in the right place beforehand can help you react the best way you can. Every situation is different, but below are guidelines on how brown bear attacks can differ from black bear attacks. Help look after others by reporting every bear incident to a park ranger straight away. Above all, keep your distance from bears!

Brown/Grizzly Bears: If you're attacked by a brown/grizzly bear, leave your pack on and PLAY DEAD. Lie flat on your stomach with your hands clasped behind your neck. Spread your legs to make it harder for the bear to roll you over. Stay still until the bear leaves the area. Fighting back usually makes these attacks more intense. But if the attack keeps going, fight back hard. Use whatever you've got on hand to hit the bear in the face.

Black Bears: If you're attacked by a black bear, DO NOT PLAY DEAD. Try to get away to somewhere secure like a car or a building. If you can't get away, try to fight back with whatever object you can find. Aim your kicks and punches at the bear's face and muzzle.

If any bear attacks you in your tent, or stalks you and then attacks, do NOT play dead: fight back!
