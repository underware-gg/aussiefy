Once a bear's clocked you and it's paying attention to you, there are a few more things you can do to stop it turning into something worse. Here are some general tips. Mind you, check the advice for each park you visit: it does change from park to park, depending on how the local bears behave.

Let the bear know what you are by talking calmly, so it knows you're a human and not a prey animal. Stay still; stand your ground, but slowly wave your arms. Help the bear work out you're a human. It might come closer or get up on its hind legs for a better look or smell. A bear standing up is usually just curious, not threatening.

Stay calm, and remember most bears don't want to attack you; usually they just want to be left alone. A bear might bluff its way out of an encounter by charging, then turning away at the last second. Bears can also get defensive and start woofing, yawning, drooling, growling, snapping their jaws and laying their ears back. Keep talking to the bear in low tones; it'll help you stay calmer, and it won't threaten the bear. A scream or a sudden movement can set off an attack. Never copy bear sounds or let out a high-pitched squeal.

Pick up any littlies straight away. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear might think it's the sound of a prey animal. Slowly wave your arms above your head and tell the bear to back off. Bushwalk and get about in groups. A group's usually noisier and smellier than one person on their own, so bears tend to pick up on a group from further off, and with all of you together, a group's big enough to put a bear off, too.

Make yourselves look as big as you can (say, by moving to higher ground).

Do NOT let the bear get at your food. If it gets your tucker, that only encourages it and makes the problem worse for everyone after you.

Do NOT drop your pack: it can protect your back, and it stops a bear getting into your tucker.

If the bear's not moving, back away slowly and sideways; that way you can keep an eye on it and you won't trip over. Moving sideways doesn't threaten a bear, either.

Do NOT run, but if the bear follows you, stop and hold your ground. Bears can run as fast as a racehorse, uphill and down. Same as dogs, they'll chase anything that runs off.

Do NOT climb a tree. Grizzlies and black bears can both climb trees.

Leave the area or take a detour. If you can't, wait till the bear moves off. Always leave the bear a way out.

Be extra careful if you see a female with cubs; never get between a mum and her cub, and never try to go up to them. The chances of an attack go up heaps if she reckons you're a danger to her cubs.

Bear Attacks

Bear attacks are rare; most bears only care about protecting their food, their cubs or their space. Still, being ready for it in your head can help you react the best way. Every situation's different, but below is a guide to how brown bear attacks can differ from black bear attacks. Help keep others safe by reporting every bear incident to a park ranger straight away. Above all, keep your distance from bears!

Brown/Grizzly Bears: If you're attacked by a brown/grizzly bear, leave your pack on and PLAY DEAD. Lie flat on your stomach with your hands clasped behind your neck. Spread your legs so it's harder for the bear to turn you over. Stay still till the bear leaves the area. Fighting back usually makes these attacks more intense. But if the attack keeps going, fight back hard. Use whatever you've got on hand to hit the bear in the face.

Black Bears: If you're attacked by a black bear, DO NOT PLAY DEAD. Try to get away to somewhere secure, like a car or a building. If you can't get away, try to fight back with anything you can lay your hands on. Aim your kicks and punches at the bear's face and muzzle.

If any bear attacks you in your tent, or stalks you and then attacks, do NOT play dead—fight back!
