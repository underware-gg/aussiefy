A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone usually stays on their feet the whole time, mostly at around 10 am. The idea is that standing about for ages gets uncomfortable, so the meetings stay short.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing up.[2]

Software development

According to the PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI), daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3]

Some software development processes have the team get together every day to make commitments to each other. Those daily commitments let everyone hear about problems that might crop up, and work together on sorting out the tricky or time-consuming ones. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development processes,[4][5] like scrum or Kanban, but you can use it as part of any software development process.

The meeting should usually happen at the same time and place every working day. Everyone on the team's encouraged to rock up, but meetings don't get put off if a few of them are away. One of the big things about it is that the meeting's a chance for the team to have a yarn with each other, not a status update for the bosses or stakeholders.[6] It's sometimes called a kind of status meeting, but it's set up to get follow-up conversations going and to pick up problems before they get too hairy. The practice also builds closer working relationships, what with how often it happens, the follow-up chats it needs and how short it is, and that means heaps more know-how gets passed around – a much more active aim than your typical status meeting. Team members take turns having their say, sometimes passing a token round to show whose turn it is to talk.[7] Each member talks about what they've got done since the last stand-up, the work they're expecting to do before the next one and anything holding them up, and takes the chance to ask for a hand or team up.[8]

Team members might sometimes ask for a quick clarification or say something short, like "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually doesn't get into full discussions.[7]

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and people stand up for them to remind everyone to keep the meeting short and to-the-point.[7] The stand-up meeting's sometimes also called the "stand-up" when you're doing extreme programming, or the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when you're following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) so the team can take stock of how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] This meeting's meant to be brief, less than 15 minutes, so any deep-dive discussions about what's holding things up get left till after it's over. Since some teams have their meetings standing up, they might call this meeting the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) had team members briefly, for a maximum of one minute each, answer three questions as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

These questions got dropped from the 2020 Scrum Guide.
