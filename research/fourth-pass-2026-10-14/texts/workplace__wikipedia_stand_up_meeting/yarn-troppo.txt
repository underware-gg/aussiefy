Now, this one's a ripper. A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone stays on their feet the whole way through, usually at around 10 am, smoko time near enough. And the trick is, standing about for ages gets uncomfortable, so nobody wants to drag it out and the meetings stay short.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing up.[2] Even the toffs don't get a chair.

Software development

According to the PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI), daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3] That's the official word on it, anyway.

Some software development processes have the team get together every day to make commitments to each other. Those daily commitments mean everyone hears early about problems that might crop up, and they can all muck in on sorting out the tricky or time-consuming ones. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development processes,[4][5] like scrum or Kanban, but you can use it as part of any software development process you like.

The meeting should usually happen at the same time and place every working day. Everyone on the team's encouraged to rock up, but if a few of them are away, the meeting goes ahead without 'em. Here's the big thing, though: the meeting's a chance for the team to have a yarn with each other, not a status update for the bosses or stakeholders.[6] Sure, it sometimes gets called a kind of status meeting, but it's set up to get follow-up conversations going and to spot problems before they turn into a proper dog's breakfast. The practice also builds closer working relationships, what with how often it happens, the follow-up chats it needs and how short it is, and that means heaps more know-how gets passed around – a much more active aim than your typical status meeting. Team members take turns having their say, sometimes passing a token round to show whose turn it is to talk.[7] Each member goes through what they've got done since the last stand-up, the work they're expecting to do before the next one and anything holding them up, and that's their chance to ask for a hand or team up with someone.[8]

Team members might sometimes ask for a quick clarification or chuck in something short, like "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually doesn't get into the full discussion. Save that for later.[7]

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and people stand up for them to remind everyone to keep the meeting short and to-the-point. No waffling.[7] The stand-up meeting's sometimes also called the "stand-up" when you're doing extreme programming, or the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when you're following the scrum framework. Same beast, different names.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) so the team can take stock of how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] This meeting's meant to be brief, less than 15 minutes, so any deep-dive discussions about what's holding things up get parked till after it's over. And since some teams have their meetings standing up, they might call this meeting the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) had team members briefly, for a maximum of one minute each, and not a second more, answer three questions as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

Then the 2020 Scrum Guide went and dropped these questions altogether.
