On Saturday, the family of American muso and songwriter Bob Weir announced he'd died with his loved ones around him. The former guitarist and singer for rock band the Grateful Dead was 78. His family broke the news on Instagram, saying he'd recently fought a cancer diagnosis but died of "underlying lung issues". They didn't say what day Weir died.

Weir played rhythm guitar and sang with American psychedelic rock band the Grateful Dead for decades, and wrote some of their songs too, including the hit "Sugar Magnolia", a fixture at Grateful Dead gigs. He also had a long solo career, putting together all sorts of groups with his old Grateful Dead mates, and kept playing live till just months before he died.

Weir was born Robert Hall Parber on 16 October 1947 in San Francisco, California, and grew up in the San Francisco Bay Area, with a stint at school in Colorado, where he met future Grateful Dead member John Perry Barlow. Weir took up the guitar at 13 and found his way into the local bluegrass scene, where he first came across Jerry Garcia playing banjo. On 31 December 1965, Garcia and Weir agreed to start their own group, Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions, which grew into the Grateful Dead.

Weir played rhythm guitar and sang for the band for decades. From the 1970s he also had a crack at a solo career and a string of other groups outside the Grateful Dead, including Kingfish, Bobby and the Midnites, RatDog, Furthur, The Other Ones, The Dead, Wolf Bros, and Dead & Company. Every one of them had fellow Grateful Dead collaborators in it, and they covered the lot, from country rock to jam band to jazz-tinged roots rock built on blues and country. On vocals he mostly sang harmonies and backup, but he took the lead on a few tracks, including Grateful Dead anthem "Truckin'", a song the Library of Congress named a national treasure in 1997.

Away from the stage, Weir also set up Tamalpais Research Institute, a virtual performance platform, and was active in politics, backing a few causes, including the environment, poverty relief, and issues to do with the arts. He'd kept his hand in on the Bay Area music scene, playing live with local musos and co-owning the venue Sweetwater Music Hall in Mill Valley, California.
