Sad news out of the States on Saturday: the family of Bob Weir, the American muso and songwriter, let everyone know he'd passed away with his loved ones round him. The former guitarist and singer for the Grateful Dead was 78. His family put it up on Instagram, saying he'd been fighting a cancer diagnosis lately but it was "underlying lung issues" that got him in the end. They didn't say what day he died.

For decades Weir played rhythm guitar and sang with the Grateful Dead, the American psychedelic rock outfit, and he wrote a few of their songs as well, the hit "Sugar Magnolia" among them, which they played at just about every Grateful Dead gig going. He had a long solo run too, knocking together one group after another with his old Grateful Dead mates, and he was still up there playing live till just months before he died. Couldn't keep him off a stage.

He started out as Robert Hall Parber, born on 16 October 1947 in San Francisco, California, and grew up round the San Francisco Bay Area, with a stretch at school over in Colorado, where he met John Perry Barlow, who'd end up in the Grateful Dead with him. Weir picked up the guitar at 13 and drifted into the local bluegrass scene, and that's where he first ran into Jerry Garcia, plucking away on a banjo. Then on 31 December 1965, New Year's Eve no less, Garcia and Weir shook on starting their own group, Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions, which grew into the Grateful Dead.

Decades of rhythm guitar and singing for the band, and from the 1970s he was off having a go at a solo career and a whole string of other groups outside the Grateful Dead as well: Kingfish, Bobby and the Midnites, RatDog, Furthur, The Other Ones, The Dead, Wolf Bros, and Dead & Company. Every one of them had fellow Grateful Dead collaborators in the line-up, and between them they had a crack at everything from country rock to jam band to jazz-tinged roots rock built on blues and country. Mostly he sang harmonies and backup, but he stepped up to lead on a few tracks, including the Grateful Dead anthem "Truckin'", which the Library of Congress named a national treasure in 1997. Not bad going for a backup singer.

Off the stage, Weir also set up Tamalpais Research Institute, a virtual performance platform, and he was into his politics, getting behind a few causes, including the environment, poverty relief, and issues to do with the arts. And he never drifted far from the Bay Area music scene, getting up to play live with the local musos and co-owning a venue, Sweetwater Music Hall in Mill Valley, California. Good innings, Bobby.
