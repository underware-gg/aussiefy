Snags

Separate a string of snags. Give each snag a few good jabs with a carving fork. Put 'em in the frying pan, cover with boiling water, and cook fifteen minutes; drain, pop 'em back in the frying pan, and fry till they're nicely browned. Serve with fried apples. Snags are often grilled same as bacon, with apples baked in the pan under 'em.

Boston Baked Beans

Pick over one quart of pea beans, cover with cold water, and leave 'em soak overnight. In the mornin', drain, cover with fresh water, heat slowly (keep the water under boiling point), and cook till the skins'll burst. Best way to tell is to take a few beans on the tip of a spoon and blow on 'em: if they're cooked enough, the skins'll burst. Beans ya test like that have gotta be chucked out, of course. Drain the beans, and chuck the bean water outside, not down the sink. Scald the rind of three-quarters of a pound of fat salt pork, scrape it, cut off a one-quarter inch slice and put it in the bottom of the bean pot. Cut through the rind of the rest of the pork every half inch, makin' the cuts one inch deep. Put the beans in the pot and bury the pork in 'em, leavin' the rind showin'. Mix one tablespoon salt, one tablespoon molasses, and three tablespoons sugar; add one cup boiling water, and tip it over the beans; then add enough more boiling water to cover 'em. Put the lid on the bean pot, whack it in the oven, and bake slowly six or eight hours, takin' the lid off for the last hour so the rind goes brown and crisp. Add water as ya need it. Heaps of folks reckon that if ya add half a tablespoon of mustard with the seasonings, the beans go down easier on the guts. If ya'd rather pork with a bit of lean through it, use less salt.

The good name Boston Baked Beans have got for 'emselves gets put down to the earthen bean pot, with its small top and bulgin' sides, they're meant to be cooked in. But beans every bit as good have often been eaten where a five-pound lard pail stood in for a busted bean pot.

Yellow-eyed beans are dead good baked, too.
