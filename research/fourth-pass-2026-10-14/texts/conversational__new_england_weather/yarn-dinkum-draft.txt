Now, ya might've heard this one. I reckon, hand on heart, that the Maker who made us all makes everything in New England except the weather. Dunno who makes that, but I reckon it must be raw apprentices in the weather-clerk's factory, learning the trade in New England for their board and clothes, and then they get promoted to make weather for countries that want a decent article and will take their custom elsewhere if they don't get it. There's a lavish variety to the New England weather that has the stranger admiring it, and then regretting it. The weather's always up to something there; always minding its business; always coming up with new designs and trying them on the people to see how they go. But it gets through more business in spring than any other season. In the spring I've counted one hundred and thirty-six different kinds of weather inside of twenty-four hours.

It was me that made the fame and fortune of that bloke who had that marvellous collection of weather on show at the Centennial, the one that knocked the foreigners flat. He was going to travel all over the world and get specimens from every clime. I said, "Don't you do it; you come to New England on a good spring day." I told him what we could do for style, variety and quantity. Well, he came, and he made his collection in four days. As for variety, he owned up that he got hundreds of kinds of weather he'd never heard of. And as for quantity, well, after he'd picked out and chucked away everything with a blemish on it, he not only had weather enough, he had weather to spare; weather to hire out; weather to sell; to deposit; weather to invest; weather to give to the poor.

The people of New England are patient and easygoing by nature, but there are some things they won't cop. Every year they kill a fair few poets for writing about "Beautiful Spring." These are mostly blow-ins, who bring their ideas of spring from somewhere else, and can't know, of course, how the locals feel about spring. And so the first thing they know, the chance to ask how anyone feels has gone for good.

Old Probabilities has a mighty reputation for getting his forecasts right, and he's earned every bit of it. You pick up the paper and watch how crisp and sure of himself he is, ticking off what today's weather's going to be on the Pacific, down South, in the Middle States, in the Wisconsin region. Watch him sail along, all proud of his powers, till he gets to New England, and then watch his tail drop. He's got no idea what the weather's going to be in New England. Well, he chews it over, and after a while he comes out with something like this: Probably northeast to southwest winds, varying to the southward and westward and eastward, and points between, high and low barometer swapping around from place to place; probable areas of rain, snow, hail, and drought, succeeded or preceded by earthquakes, with thunder and lightning. Then he jots down a postscript off the top of his head, to cover accidents: "But it is possible that the programme may be wholly changed in the mean time."

Yep, one of the brightest gems in the New England weather is how dazzlingly uncertain it is. There's only one thing certain about it: you can be certain there's going to be heaps of it, a proper grand parade; but you can never tell which end of the procession is going to move first. You get set for the drought; you leave your brolly in the house and head out, and two to one you get drowned. You make up your mind the earthquake's due; you get out from under, and grab hold of something to steady yourself, and the first thing you know you get struck by lightning. These are big letdowns, but there's no helping them.

The lightning there is a funny one; it's that convincing, when it strikes a thing it doesn't leave enough of that thing behind for you to tell whether... Well, you'd think it was something valuable, and a pollie had been there. And the thunder. When the thunder's only tuning up and scraping and sawing, and keying up the instruments for the show, strangers say, "Crikey, what awful thunder you've got here!" But when the baton goes up and the real concert starts, you'll find that stranger down the cellar with his head in the ash-barrel.
