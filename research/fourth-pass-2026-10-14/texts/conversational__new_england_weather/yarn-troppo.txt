Here's one for ya. Hand on heart, I reckon the Maker who made the lot of us makes everything in New England bar the weather. Who makes that? Dunno. But my money's on a mob of raw apprentices out the back of the weather-clerk's factory, learning the ropes in New England for their board and clothes. Once they've got the hang of it they get promoted to making weather for countries that want a decent article, the sort who'll take their custom elsewhere if they don't get one. And the variety you get in New England weather, mate. A stranger turns up and can't help admiring it, and then he can't help regretting it. The weather's always up to something there. Always on the job, always minding its business, always cooking up new designs and trying them on the locals to see how they go. But spring's when it really gets cracking, busier than any other season. One spring I counted one hundred and thirty-six different kinds of weather inside twenty-four hours. Counted 'em.

And it was me, if you don't mind, who made the fame and fortune of that bloke with the marvellous collection of weather on show at the Centennial, the one that had the foreigners' jaws on the floor. He was all set to travel the whole world collecting specimens from every clime going. I said, "Don't you do it; you come to New England on a good spring day." I told him what we could do for style, for variety, for sheer quantity. Well, he came, and he had his whole collection done in four days. Variety? He owned up he'd bagged hundreds of kinds of weather he'd never even heard of. And quantity? Well, after he'd picked through it and chucked out everything with the slightest blemish, he not only had weather enough, he had weather to spare. Weather to hire out. Weather to sell. To put in the bank. Weather to invest. Weather to give to the poor. The whole box and dice.

Now, the people of New England are patient, easygoing types by nature, but there's some things they just won't cop. Every year they knock off a fair few poets for writing about "Beautiful Spring." These are mostly blow-ins, who've brought their ideas of spring from somewhere else and can't know, fair enough, how the locals feel about spring. So the first thing they know, their chance to ask how anybody feels has gone for good. Should've kept it to themselves, ay?

Then there's Old Probabilities, the weather forecaster. He's got a mighty reputation for calling it right, and fair dinkum, he's earned every bit. Pick up the paper and watch him: crisp as, cocky as you like, ticking off today's weather on the Pacific, down South, in the Middle States, in the Wisconsin region. He's sailing along, chuffed with himself and his powers, till he hits New England, and then you watch his tail drop. He's got no idea what the weather's going to do in New England. Not a clue. So he chews it over, scratches his noggin, and after a while he coughs up something like this: Probably northeast to southwest winds, varying to the southward and westward and eastward, and points between, high and low barometer swapping around from place to place; probable areas of rain, snow, hail, and drought, succeeded or preceded by earthquakes, with thunder and lightning. Covers the lot, doesn't it? Then, off the top of his head, he jots down a postscript to cover accidents: "But it is possible that the programme may be wholly changed in the mean time." Hedging his bets, the cheeky beggar.

Yep, one of the brightest gems in the New England weather is how dazzlingly you can't pick it. Only one thing's certain: there's going to be heaps of it, a proper grand parade, the full turnout, but you can never tell which end of the procession's going to move off first. You get set for the drought, you leave your brolly at home and head out, and it's two to one you get drowned. You make up your mind the earthquake's due, so you get out from under and grab hold of something to steady yourself, and next thing you know, you get struck by lightning. Big letdowns, those, but there's no helping it.

And the lightning there is a funny one. It's that convincing, when it hits a thing it doesn't leave enough of it behind for you to tell whether... Well, you'd reckon it was something valuable and a pollie had been through. And the thunder! When the thunder's only tuning up, scraping and sawing away, keying up the instruments for the show, the strangers go, "Crikey, what awful thunder you've got here!" But when the baton goes up and the real concert kicks off, you'll find that same stranger down the cellar with his head in the ash-barrel.
