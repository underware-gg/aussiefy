Here's one for ya. If you fancy chopping down your own Chrissy tree this year, Christmas tree permits go on sale from November 13, 2025, online through Recreation.gov and in person at all the Santa Fe National Forest District Offices and the Supervisor's Office. Where you can cut, which dates, and what sorts of trees you're allowed to take the axe to: the details are all here.

Getting your hands on a Christmas Tree permit:

Online through Recreation.gov: punch in Santa Fe National Forest Christmas Tree Permits. Now, do yourself a favour and have a proper read of the overview and the need-to-know bits before you hand over your money. You'll also have to set up or log in to a Recreation.gov account to get the thing paid for.

In person at the Supervisor's Office in Santa Fe, N.M., or the Jemez, Cuba, Coyote, Española, and Pecos/Las Vegas District Offices. Give them a bell for current hours.

Out at the Walatowa Visitor Center they'll be selling permits in person on Saturdays only, mind, from 9:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m., from November 22 through to December 20, 2024.

Can't be bothered with the axe? Pre-cut Christmas Trees might be up for grabs at district offices. No promises, though: that comes down to the weather and how many they've got, and first dibs goes to people who can't cut their own tree.

The SFNF's Christmas tree permits will set you back $10, and there's no refunds. Buy one online through recreation.gov and they'll sting you an extra $2.50 service fee on top. One tag covers a tree up to 5 inches (13 cm) across and 10 feet (3 m) tall. Anything taller than 10 feet and/or wider than 5 inches needs another tag. So a 15-foot (4.6 m) whopper? That's two tags.

Got a fourth grader (that's a Year 4 kid) with an Every Kid Outdoors pass? They get a free Christmas tree permit: just enter the pass or voucher number when you buy the permit (mind you, the $2.50 service fee still gets slapped on).

And here's the good bit: cutting yourself a Chrissy tree actually does the forest a power of good. The permit system helps thin out stands of small-diameter trees packed in like sardines. The local forest health experts pick out the patches of bush that'd be better off thinned, and wouldn't you know it, those trees tend to be the perfect size for Christmas trees. Clear them out of the designated areas and the other trees get room to grow bigger, and it can open up patches with a feed for the wildlife. Everyone's a winner.
