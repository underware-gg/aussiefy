WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, who ran the International Reform Bureau as its superintendent for 28 years after founding it himself, and who was known far and wide for his work for prohibition and causes of that kind, died of pneumonia in a hospital here today. He was 73.

The folks on the other side of the laws he pushed had him down as a "reform fanatic," and for years he was a familiar and colourful sight around the congressional lobbies and committee rooms. Those who were with him on his policies gave him a fair whack of the credit for getting prohibition through, along with laws to rein in the use of narcotics and other legislation along the same lines.

He never minced words about Sunday amusements, and Dr. Crafts tried to have them banned by law. He also had a red-hot go at stopping the Dempsey-Carpentier bout being held in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. And he wrote a stack on moral questions.

After the funeral service here tomorrow, the body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher who came from puritan stock. He finished at Wesleyan in 1869 and at the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long life in the ministry at his dad's church, switched over to Congregationalism in 1880, and in 1883 went into the Presbyterian ministry, where he stayed from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a prolific and forceful writer and worker in religious causes in her own right, who was his partner in plenty of his many activities, Sunday schools most of all.

Among the reform leaders, there wouldn't be many who could match Dr. Crafts for sheer hard yakka. His pen and his voice got precious little rest over the last fifty years. He gave six lectures a week on average, in twenty-nine different countries, and turned out a book a year, most of them, though not all, on religious topics. Here's one curious title, for instance, that pops up in a long list of others that won't raise an eyebrow: "Rhetoric Made Racy." As well as never letting up on papers for religious magazines, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

To the general public, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known for going after popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, durries: those were just a few of the things his tireless reforming zeal took aim at.
