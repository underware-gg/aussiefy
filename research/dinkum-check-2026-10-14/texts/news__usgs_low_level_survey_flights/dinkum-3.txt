RESTON, Va. – Keep an eye on the sky: from early October, planes will be flying low over eastern California and a sliver of western Nevada, using airborne geophysical gear to get a picture of the geology underneath.

It's a team effort between the U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS). The Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is the USGS's push to drag the nation’s maps of geology and mineral resources up to date, and to suss out where the energy, the groundwater and the natural hazards are.

"Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

The survey takes in a chunk of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin, country that's chock-full of all sorts of mineral deposits and districts with known mineral resources. Think the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., the biggest tungsten producer the U.S. has ever had. And there's still a fair bit left to find and dig up out there. The critical minerals and related commodities they reckon could be in the ground include antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc.

The new high-resolution geophysical surveys will lay down the basic geological data to back future mineral exploration. They'll also give crucial data from below the surface for sizing up geothermal energy potential, plus the volcano and earthquake risks in the Long Valley caldera, along the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and within Owens Valley.

The choppers will collect magnetic and radiometric data along pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart, at a height of 330 feet (100 metres). The USGS has contracted NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to do the collecting. Their AS350 helicopters will carry a long “stinger” poking out from the main cabin or the skids, with the sensors housed inside. Don't stress: the scientific instruments are totally passive and give off nothing that's a risk to people, animals or plants. No photos or video will be taken, either. The aircraft will be flown by experienced pilots who are specially trained and approved for low-level flying, and they work with the FAA to make sure every flight is safe and above board under U.S. law. They'll only fly in daylight hours.

The flights will be based out of the Bishop and Lone Pine airports, and will go over these counties: Fresno, Inyo, and Mono in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral in Nevada.

Earth MRI covers airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys, and geologic mapping projects. Everything they collect goes up free for anyone on the USGS ScienceBase website.
