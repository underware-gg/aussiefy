RESTON, Va. – Low-level plane flights kick off in early October over eastern California and a sliver of western Nevada, to get a picture of the geology using airborne geophysical technology.

The flights are a joint effort between the U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS). The USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is all about bringing the country's maps of geology and mineral resources up to date, and finding energy, groundwater and natural hazards while they're at it.

"Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

The survey takes in a chunk of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin, which has a fair few kinds of mineral deposits, and districts with known mineral resources, like the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., the biggest historic producer of tungsten in the U.S. There's still heaps of exploration and production potential left in the survey areas, too. The critical minerals and related commodities that could be down there include antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc.

The new high-resolution geophysical surveys will give them the groundwork geological data to back future mineral exploration, and the crucial subsurface data for sizing up geothermal energy potential, plus volcano and seismic hazards in the Long Valley caldera, the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and right through Owens Valley.

The survey means collecting magnetic and radiometric data from helicopters flying pre-planned flight lines spaced 660 feet (200 metres) apart, at a height of 330 feet (100 metres). The USGS has signed up NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to collect the airborne magnetic and radiometric data. Their AS350 helicopters will be fitted with a long “stinger” sticking out from the main cabin or the helicopter skids, with the data-collecting sensors housed inside it. Now, these scientific instruments are completely passive, with no emissions that pose any risk to people, animals or plants. They won't be taking any photos or video, either. The choppers will be flown by experienced pilots who are specially trained and approved for low-level flying. These pilots work with the FAA to make sure the flights are safe and in line with U.S. law. The surveys will only be flown in daylight.

Flights will be based out of the Bishop and Lone Pine airports, and will fly over these counties: Fresno, Inyo, and Mono in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral in Nevada.

Earth MRI covers airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys, and geologic mapping projects. Whatever data they collect goes up free for anyone on the USGS ScienceBase website.
