RESTON, Va. – Planes flying low will start going over eastern California and a sliver of western Nevada in early October, using airborne geophysical gear to get a picture of the geology underneath.

The flights are a joint effort between the U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS). The USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is a push to bring the country's maps of geology and mineral resources up to date, and to track down energy and groundwater resources and natural hazards.

"Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy," said Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative.

"Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years," said Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey.

The survey takes in a chunk of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin, which is home to a wide mix of mineral deposits and districts with known mineral resources, like the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif., historically the biggest producer of tungsten in the U.S. There's still heaps of exploration and production potential left in the survey areas. Critical minerals and related commodities that could turn up include antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc.

The new high-resolution geophysical surveys will give the groundwork geological data to back future mineral exploration, and they'll also provide crucial data from below the surface for sizing up geothermal energy potential, as well as volcano and earthquake hazards in the Long Valley caldera, along the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and within Owens Valley.

The survey collects magnetic and radiometric data from choppers flying pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart at a height of 330 feet (100 metres). The USGS has signed up NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to collect the airborne magnetic and radiometric data. Their AS350 helicopters will be fitted with a long "stinger" that sticks out from the main cabin or the helicopter skids and holds the sensors that collect the data. These scientific instruments are completely passive, with no emissions that pose any risk to people, animals or plants. No photos or video will be taken. The aircraft will be flown by experienced pilots who are specially trained and approved for low-level flying. These pilots work with the FAA to make sure the flights are safe and within U.S. law. The surveys will only be flown in daylight hours.

Flights will be based out of the Bishop and Lone Pine airports, and will fly over these counties: Fresno, Inyo, and Mono in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral in Nevada.

Earth MRI takes in airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys, and geologic mapping projects. The data collected are put up free for the public on the USGS ScienceBase website.
