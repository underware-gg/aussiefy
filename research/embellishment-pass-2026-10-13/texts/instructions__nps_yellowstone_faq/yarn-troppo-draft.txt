Where do I book a place to stay in Yellowstone?
The nine lodges in the park, the whole box and dice, are run by one mob: Yellowstone National Park Lodges. They're all open from late spring right through autumn, but once winter bites there's only two still goin': Old Faithful Snow Lodge and Mammoth Hot Springs Hotel. Two! In a park that size! So get your booking in early, mate, or you'll miss out.

Do I need a booking to get into the park?
Nah, you don't have to book the car in to get into the park – all you need's a park entrance pass. Buy the pass online before you lob and you'll save yourself a bit of time at the entrance stations. But fair warning: summer's flat chat. Crowds at all the popular spots, queues at the entrance gates, queues through the roadworks, and queues wherever someone's spotted a bison and pulled over for a squiz, along with every other car on the road. Be patient, take a breath, and enjoy your visit. You're on holidays, after all.

How did Yellowstone get its name?
Now, this one's a ripper. Yellowstone National Park's named after the Yellowstone River, the main river running through the park. The way the French-Canadian trappers told it back in the 1800s, they asked the Minnetaree tribe, who live in what's now eastern Montana, what the river was called. And the answer came back "Mi tse a-da-zi," which, word for word, is "Rock Yellow River." So the trappers turn that into French: "Roche Jaune," or "Pierre Jaune." Then along comes explorer-geographer David Thompson in 1797 and puts it into English—"Yellow Stone." Lewis and Clark, not wanting to pick sides, called the Yellowstone River by both the French and the English names. And over time it all got squashed down into the one word: "Yellowstone." Three languages to name one river.

Is Yellowstone the biggest national park?
Nah, nah, not even close. Better than half of Alaska's national park units are bigger, and the biggest unit in the whole National Park System is Wrangell–St. Elias National Park and Preserve, at a whopping 13 million acres (about 5.3 million hectares). Now, up until 1994 Yellowstone (2.2 million acres, about 890,000 hectares) did get to call itself the biggest national park in the lower 48 of the United States. Then that year Death Valley National Monument got itself made bigger and turned into a national park—more than 3 million acres of it (about 1.2 million hectares)—and that was the end of that.

What's the difference between a national park and a national forest?
Two different departments, for a start. National parks are run by the Department of the Interior, and national forests by the Department of Agriculture. And they've got different jobs. The National Park Service has to keep resources just as they are, unharmed, not a thing touched. The Forest Service has to look after resources sensibly so they can be used heaps of different ways, for good, without running 'em into the ground. And Yellowstone National Park's got six national forests all round it.

How many rangers work in Yellowstone?
In the busiest stretch of summer there's about 750 people working for the National Park Service. About 392 of 'em are permanent staff who stick around all year. The park rangers cover education, looking after resources, law enforcement, emergency medical services, and work out in the backcountry. Then the rest of the crew specialise in research, maintenance, management, admin, track maintenance, fire management, and collecting fees. Takes a fair few hands to keep the whole show running.

Can we swim in rivers and lakes?
Swimming isn't recommended, and they mean it: most of the lakes and creeks are dangerously cold. Stay out.

What's the highest peak in the park?
That'd be Eagle Peak, down the south-east end of Yellowstone, at 11,358 feet (3,462 metres).

Why is Yellowstone called a biosphere reserve and a world heritage site?
The United Nations gave Yellowstone National Park both titles, biosphere reserve and world heritage site, because its natural and cultural resources matter to the whole world, not just the locals.

What's the Continental Divide?
Picture the Continental Divide as the top of the ridge running along the spine of the continent. In theory, any rain or snow that lands on the west side of the Divide finds its way to the Pacific Ocean, and any that lands on the east side ends up in the Atlantic Ocean. Now, in Yellowstone (same as everywhere else) this ridgeline isn't straight, it wiggles all over the shop. So much so that you'll cross the Continental Divide three times between the South Entrance and the Old Faithful area. Highest crossing's Craig Pass, at 8,262 feet (2,518 metres).

How did Mt. Washburn form?
At 10,219 feet (3,115 metres), you can spot this peak from all over the park. It's what's left of an extinct volcano from the Absaroka Volcanics, about 50 million years ago. And here's the kicker: 631,000 years ago a volcanic eruption literally cut the thing in half. Only the northern part of the original volcano's still standing for you to see.

Does Yellowstone include a federally designated wilderness?
Nah. Most of the park was put up for this designation back in 1972, but Congress still hasn't done anything about the recommendation. Over fifty years and counting.
