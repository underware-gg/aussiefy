SHERIFF: Nothin' here but kitchen gear.

(_The_ COUNTY ATTORNEY, _after another squiz round the kitchen, opens the door of a cupboard. He hops up on a chair and has a look on a shelf. Pulls his hand back, all sticky_.)

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Well, here's a bloody mess.

(_The women edge closer_.)

MRS PETERS: (_to the other woman_) Oh, her fruit; it did freeze, (_to the_ LAWYER) She was fretting about that when it turned so cold. Reckoned the fire'd go out and her jars'd crack.

SHERIFF: Well, wouldn't it rot your socks, women! Locked up for murder and she's carryin' on about her jam.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: I reckon by the time we're done she might have something a bit more serious than jam to worry about.

HALE: Ah well, women are always fussin' over trifles.

(_The two women move in a little closer together_.)

COUNTY ATTORNEY: (_laying on the charm like a young pollie_) And yet, for all their fussing, where would we be without the ladies, eh? (_the women don't give an inch. He goes over to the sink, scoops a dipperful of water out of the bucket, tips it into a basin and washes his hands. Goes to dry them on the roller towel, turns it round for a cleaner bit_) Filthy towels! (_kicks the pans under the sink_) Not much chop as a housekeeper, would you say, ladies?

MRS HALE: (_stiffly_) There's a fair whack of work to be done on a farm.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: No doubt. And yet (_with a little bow to her_) I know there are a few Dickson county farmhouses that don't have roller towels like this. (_He gives it a tug to show the length of it again_.)

MRS HALE: Those towels get grubby awful quick. Blokes' hands aren't always as clean as they could be.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Ah, sticking up for the sisterhood, I see. But you and Mrs Wright were neighbours. I suppose you were mates, too.

MRS HALE: (_shaking her head_) I haven't seen much of her these last few years. I haven't been in this house--it's more than a year.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: And why's that? You didn't like her?

MRS HALE: I liked her well enough. Farmers' wives have their hands full, Mr Henderson. And then--

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Yes--?

MRS HALE: (_looking round_) It never seemed a very cheery sort of place.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: No--it's not cheery. I wouldn't say she had much of a knack for homemaking.

MRS HALE: Well, I don't know as Wright had either.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: You mean they didn't get on too well?

MRS HALE: No, I don't mean anything. But I don't reckon a place'd be any cheerier for having John Wright in it.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: I'd like to have a yarn about that a bit later. I want to get the lie of the land upstairs now. (_He heads left, where three steps lead up to a stair door_.)

SHERIFF: I s'pose whatever Mrs Peters does'll be right. She was gonna take some clothes in for her, you know, and a few little bits and pieces. We cleared out in such a rush yesterday.

COUNTY ATTORNEY: Yes, but I'd like to see what you take, Mrs Peters, and keep an eye out for anything that might come in handy for us.

MRS PETERS: Yes, Mr Henderson.

(_The women listen to the men's boots on the stairs, then look round the kitchen_.)

MRS HALE: I'd hate to have blokes traipsing into my kitchen, sticking their noses in and bagging everything.

(_She straightens up the pans under the sink that the_ LAWYER _had shoved out of place_.)

MRS PETERS: Course, it's only their job.

MRS HALE: Job's fair enough, but I reckon that deputy sheriff who came out to light the fire might've put a bit of this muck on it himself. (_gives the roller towel a tug_) Wish I'd thought of that earlier. Seems a bit rough, bagging her for not having the place spick and span when she had to clear out in such a hurry.
