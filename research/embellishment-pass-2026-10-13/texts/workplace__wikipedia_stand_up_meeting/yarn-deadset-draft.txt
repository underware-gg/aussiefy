A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where the people there usually take part standing up, mostly at around 10 am. The idea is that standing for ages gets uncomfortable, and that keeps the meetings short.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing up.[2]

Software development

According to the PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI), daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3]

Some software development processes have the team front up every day to make commitments to each other. The daily commitments let everyone find out about likely hurdles and pull together to sort out tricky or time-consuming issues. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development processes,[4][5] such as scrum or Kanban, but it can be used as part of any software development process.

The meeting should usually happen at the same time and place every working day. All team members are encouraged to rock up, but meetings don't get put off if some team members aren't there. One of the key things is that the meeting's a chance for team members to have a yarn with each other, and not a status update for the bosses or stakeholders.[6] It's sometimes called a type of status meeting, but the way the meeting's set up is meant to get follow-up yarns going, and to pick up issues before they turn into too much of a headache. The practice also builds closer working relationships through how often it happens, the follow-up yarns it needs and how short it is, which in turn means a higher rate of knowledge transfer – a much more active aim than the usual status meeting. Team members take turns talking, sometimes handing round a token to show whose turn it is to talk.[7] Each member talks about how they've gone since the last stand-up, the work they expect to do before the next stand-up and anything holding them up, and takes the chance to ask for a hand or work together.[8]

Team members may sometimes ask for quick clarifications and make short statements, such as "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually doesn't include full discussions.[7]

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and happen with everyone standing up to remind people to keep the meeting short and to-the-point.[7] The stand-up meeting is sometimes also called the "stand-up" when doing extreme programming, "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) for the team to look back on and size up progress towards the sprint goal.[9] This meeting's meant to be short, less than 15 minutes, so any deep discussions about what's holding things up get left till after it's done. Since some teams have their meetings standing up, they may call this meeting the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) suggested team members quickly, for a maximum of one minute per member, answer three questions as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

These questions were removed from the 2020 Scrum Guide.
