Now, this one's a ripper. A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone stays on their feet the whole time, usually at around 10 am, just before smoko. And here's the clever bit: standing about for ages gets uncomfortable, your back starts playing up, so nobody's keen to let the thing drag on. Short meetings, built in. Genius.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing up.[2] So if it's good enough for the King's mob, it's good enough for your lot.

Software development

The PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI), and that's a mouthful, reckons daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3] Have a crack at saying that standing on one leg.

Some software development processes have the whole team front up every day and make commitments to each other. With those daily commitments out on the table, everyone gets wind of the hurdles coming at them and can pull together to sort out the curly or time-consuming issues. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development processes,[4][5] such as scrum or Kanban, but you can bung it into any software development process you like.

As a rule it happens at the same time and place every working day. Every team member's encouraged to rock up, but nobody waits around: if a few of them are missing, the meeting goes ahead anyway. And this is the big one: it's a chance for team members to have a yarn with each other, not a status update for the bosses or stakeholders.[6] Some people do call it a type of status meeting, but it's set up to get follow-up yarns going, and to catch issues before they turn into a proper headache. It brings people closer together at work as well, what with how often it happens, the follow-up yarns it needs and how short it is, and all that means a higher rate of knowledge transfer – heaps more active an aim than your typical status meeting, where half the room's asleep. Team members take turns talking, sometimes handing round a token so you know whose turn it is, like the talking stick at school camp.[7] Each one says how they've gone since the last stand-up, what they've got lined up before the next stand-up, and anything holding them up, and grabs the chance to ask for a hand or team up with someone.[8]

Now and then someone might ask for a quick clarification or chuck in a short statement, such as "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but full-on discussions usually don't happen at the stand-up.[7] Save the chinwag for later.

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and everyone stands up to remind people to keep it short and to-the-point.[7] Depends who you ask what it's called, mind: it's the "stand-up" when doing extreme programming, and the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum's got daily meetings (the daily scrum) for the team to look back on and size up how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] It's meant to be short, less than 15 minutes, so any deep-dive discussions about what's holding things up get parked till after it's done. And since some teams do theirs standing up, they might call it the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) had each team member quickly answer three questions, a maximum of one minute per member, mind, as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

These questions were removed from the 2020 Scrum Guide. Back to the drawing board, I s'pose.
