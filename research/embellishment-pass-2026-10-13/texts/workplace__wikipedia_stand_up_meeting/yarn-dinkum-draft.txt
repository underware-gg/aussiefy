A stand-up meeting (STUM[1]) is a meeting where everyone usually stays on their feet the whole time, mostly at around 10 am. The thinking is that standing about for ages gets uncomfortable, so nobody lets the meeting drag on.

Notable examples

By tradition, the Privy Council of the United Kingdom meets standing up.[2]

Software development

The PMBOK (7th edition) by the Project Management Institute (PMI) reckons daily stand-up is a "brief, daily collaboration meeting in which the team review progress from the previous day, declares intentions for the current day, and highlights any obstacles encountered or anticipated."[3]

Some software development processes have the team front up every day and make commitments to each other. With those daily commitments on the table, everyone gets wind of any likely hurdles and can pull together to sort out the tricky or time-consuming issues. The stand-up's especially handy in agile software development processes,[4][5] such as scrum or Kanban, but you can use it as part of any software development process.

As a rule the meeting happens at the same time and place every working day. All team members are encouraged to rock up, but the meeting doesn't get put off if a few of them aren't there. One of the big things about it is that it's a chance for team members to have a yarn with each other, not a status update for the bosses or stakeholders.[6] People do sometimes call it a type of status meeting, but it's set up to get follow-up yarns going and to pick up issues before they turn into too much of a headache. It also brings people closer together at work, what with how often it happens, the follow-up yarns it needs and how short it is, and that in turn means a higher rate of knowledge transfer – heaps more active an aim than your usual status meeting. Team members take turns talking, sometimes handing round a token to show whose turn it is.[7] Each of them says how they've gone since the last stand-up, what work they've got lined up before the next stand-up and anything holding them up, and grabs the chance to ask for a hand or team up with someone.[8]

Now and then team members might ask for a quick clarification or make a short statement, such as "Let's talk about this more after the meeting", but the stand-up usually doesn't go into full discussions.[7]

The meetings are usually timeboxed to between 5 and 15 minutes, and everyone stands up to remind people to keep it short and to-the-point.[7] The stand-up meeting sometimes also goes by the "stand-up" when doing extreme programming, or the "morning rollcall" or "daily scrum" when following the scrum framework.

In Scrum

Scrum has daily meetings (the daily scrum) so the team can look back on and size up how they're tracking towards the sprint goal.[9] This meeting's meant to be short, less than 15 minutes, so any deep discussions about what's holding things up get left till after it's done. Since some teams have their meetings standing up, they might call it the "daily stand-up".

An older Scrum Guide (2017) suggested each team member quickly answer three questions, for a maximum of one minute per member, as input to this planning:

What did I do yesterday that helped the development team meet the sprint goal?

What will I do today to help the development team meet the sprint goal?

Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the development team from meeting the sprint goal?

These questions were removed from the 2020 Scrum Guide.
