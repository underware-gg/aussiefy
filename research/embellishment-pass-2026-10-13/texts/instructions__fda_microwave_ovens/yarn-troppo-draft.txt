Now, ya might've heard this one, but here's how the old microwave actually works. The microwaves get made inside the oven by an electron tube called a magnetron. Sounds like a bloke out of a comic, doesn't it? They bounce round the metal box like a ball in a squash court till your tucker soaks 'em up. What they do is get the water molecules in the food jiggling about, and all that jiggling makes the heat that cooks your feed. That's why anything with heaps of water in it, fresh veggies and that, cooks quicker than other foods. The microwave energy turns into heat as the food soaks it up, and nah, it doesn't make your dinner “radioactive” or "contaminated." Your spag bol's not gonna glow in the dark.

Here's the other furphy. The heat's made right there in the food, sure, but a microwave doesn't cook from the "inside out." Chuck something thick in and the outside layers get heated and cooked mostly by the microwaves, while the middle gets cooked mainly by the conduction of heat working its way in from those hot outside layers. Same as any other oven, really, from the middle's point of view.

Microwave cooking can be more energy efficient than doing it the usual way, cos the food cooks faster and the energy only heats the food, not the whole oven compartment. You're not paying to heat up a big empty box. And it doesn't strip the nutritional value out of your tucker any more than ordinary cooking does. Fair dinkum, food done in a microwave oven may hang onto more of its vitamins and minerals, cos it cooks quicker and you don't have to add water.

Now, containers. Glass, paper, ceramic or plastic are what you use in the microwave, cos the microwaves go straight through 'em. The microwaves can't heat the containers themselves, but they'll still get hot from the heat of the food cooking inside, so watch your fingers. Some plastic containers shouldn't go in a microwave oven at all, cos the heat of the food inside can melt 'em, and then you've got a proper mess. As a rule, metal pans and alfoil stay out of the microwave too: the microwaves bounce off 'em, so the food cooks unevenly and you can damage the oven. The instructions that come with each microwave oven tell you what sorts of containers to use, and how to test a container to see whether or not it's right to go in. Worth a read before you find out the hard way.

Avoiding Injuries from Super-Heated Water in Microwave Ovens

Right, this bit's serious, so listen up. The FDA has had reports in the past of serious skin burns and scalds around people's hands and faces, from hot water erupting out of a cup after it'd been overheated in a microwave oven. Super-heated water (water heated past its boiling temperature) doesn't even look like it's boiling. Not a bubble. It happens when you heat water on its own in a clean cup. If the water's super-heated, the slightest bump or movement, just picking the cup up or tipping in a spoonful of instant coffee, can set off a violent eruption, with the boiling water exploding out of the cup. Straight at you. Put the instant coffee or sugar in before you heat it, though, and that cuts the risk right down.

So follow the precautions and recommendations in your microwave's instruction manual to the letter, especially on heating times. When you're working out the best time settings to get the water to the temperature you're after, make sure you don't go over the recommended heating times. Not worth a trip to emergency for a cuppa.
