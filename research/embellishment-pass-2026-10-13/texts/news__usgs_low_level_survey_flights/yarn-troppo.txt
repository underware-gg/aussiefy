RESTON, Va. – Okay, so. If you're out in eastern California or a sliver of western Nevada from early October and you hear a chopper buzzing about low over your roof, don't go ringing anyone. It's the scientists. They're flying low with airborne geophysical gear to get a squiz at the geology under your feet.

It's a team job between the U.S. Geological Survey’s Earth Mapping Resources Initiative and the California Geological Survey (CGS). The Earth Mapping Resources Initiative is the USGS having a red-hot go at dragging the nation’s maps of geology and mineral resources into this century, and sussing out where the energy, the groundwater and the natural hazards are hiding.

Now, the bloke in charge of the science side, Jamey Jones, science coordinator for the U.S. Geological Survey's Earth Mapping Resources Initiative, put it like this: "Partnership with state surveys helps accomplish national goals like mapping the critical minerals the U.S. needs. The California Geological Survey is helping with the work of the nation—and adding to state knowledge crucial to water resources, hazards and the resource economy."

And the top rock man in California, Jeremy Lancaster, California State Geologist and Director of the California Geological Survey, chucked in his two bob's worth: "Earth MRI datasets are a coordinated federal-state partnership to collect modern data in priority regions of the state, make those data publicly available, and materially improve our understanding of the geology of California. This 2026 airborne geophysical survey is of significant scale and in an area that has been a focus for some years." Which is government for "it's a big one, and we've been keen on this patch for ages."

So where are they looking? A chunk of eastern California on the western edge of the Great Basin, and that country's chock-full of mineral deposits and districts with known mineral resources. Take the Pine Creek mine near Bishop, Calif. Only the biggest tungsten producer the U.S. has ever had, that's all. And the experts reckon there's still a fair bit left out there to find and dig up. The list of critical minerals and related commodities they reckon could be down there goes: antimony, arsenic, beryllium, bismuth, boron, copper, fluorine, gallium, germanium, graphite, indium, lead, lithium, mercury, molybdenum, potash, rubidium, silver, tellurium, tin, tungsten, and zinc. The whole periodic table, near enough.

These new high-resolution geophysical surveys will lay down the basic geological data to back future mineral exploration. On top of that, they'll give crucial data from below the surface for sizing up geothermal energy potential, plus the volcano and earthquake risks in the Long Valley caldera, along the eastern Sierra Nevada range front, and within Owens Valley. Handy thing to know if you live near a volcano, ay?

Here's how it works. The choppers collect magnetic and radiometric data along pre-planned flight lines 660 feet (200 metres) apart, sitting 330 feet (100 metres) up. The USGS has contracted NRG Exploration USA Inc. and Woolpert to do the flying and collecting. Their AS350 helicopters carry a long “stinger” poking out from the main cabin or the skids, like a big mozzie's nose, with the sensors housed inside. And before anyone gets their knickers in a knot: the scientific instruments are totally passive and give off nothing that's a risk to people, animals or plants. No photos and no video, either, so nobody's perving in your backyard. The pilots are experienced, specially trained and approved for low-level flying, and they work with the FAA to make sure every flight is safe and above board under U.S. law. Daylight hours only, too, so no choppers at three in the morning.

The flights will be based out of the Bishop and Lone Pine airports, and they'll go over these counties: Fresno, Inyo, and Mono in California, and Esmeralda and Mineral in Nevada.

Earth MRI does the whole box and dice: airborne geophysical surveys like this one, geochemical reconnaissance surveys, topographic mapping using LiDAR technology, hyperspectral surveys, and geologic mapping projects. And the best bit? Everything they collect goes up free for anyone on the USGS ScienceBase website. Not a cent.
