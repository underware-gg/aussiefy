Back in the day, the trickiest bit of a weekend away was getting out of bed. Fair dinkum. Now, if it's a big house-party, easy, cos you can hear the rest of 'em pattering about and brushing their teeth through the walls. You just get up when enough of 'em sound like they're up and about. But if you're the only guest? Strewth. That's when it all goes pear-shaped, a frightful misunderstanding just waiting to happen.

"What time's brekkie?" you ask.

"Oh, any old time on Sundays," goes the hostess, waving her arm about like she's giving you the whole joint. "Sleep in as long as you like. This is 'Liberty Hall.'"

Lovely sentiment. Ripper, even. But try getting a grip on it. It sounds grand the night before, but come morning it's about as solid as a cloud, and that vagueness is downright terrifying.

So say you wake up at eight. You lie there and listen. Nothing. Not a peep. So it's back over onto the cool side of the pillow till twenty past. Then up on the elbow again, head cocked like a kelpie hearing the ute. There's a creak, over near the stairs. Hang on, they might all be up and heading down to breakfast without ya! Quick as a flash you're out of bed with your ear stuck to the door. Maybe you open it a polite little crack and have a squiz out. Dead silence, broken only, as the saying goes, by the ticking of the hall clock, and not a soul to be seen. They must be late sleepers. Maybe eleven o'clock's their Sunday getting-up time. Some people _are_ like that.

So you shut the door and plonk yourself on the edge of the bed. No chance of getting back to sleep now. Might as well have a gander at the pictures in the guest room to kill the time. Here's one of Lorna Doone. How d'e do, Lorna? Here's a group photo--taken in 1902--of your host in his evening clothes, hanging onto a mandolin for dear life. Probably his uni musical club. And a scruffier-looking mob you'd never see, you _must_ say. Righto, what about this one? An etching of some dodgy-looking barges on what's probably the Thames, looking like they're up to no good. Fair enough, at that.

Back to the door for another listen. Tick-tock-tick-tock. And you know if you turned on the bath, the pipes'd start banging and you'd wake the whole house. So back you go, edge of the bed again.

Hello, what's this? Some books on the table. "Fifty Famous Sonnets," illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Nah. Never touch a sonnet before brekkie, that's the rule. "My experiences in the Alps," by a lady mountain-climber who's written on the fly-leaf, "To my good friends the Elbridges, in memory of many happy days together at Chamounix. October, 1907." Well, that settles _that_, doesn't it. "Essay on Compensation" in limp leather, by R.W. Emerson, published by Houghton, Mifflin & Co. Oh, very well then! Reckoned that'd be over your head, did they? Well, we'll show 'em! We'll read it out of pure spite. Flip it open at the red ribbon:

"Of the like nature is that expectation of change which instantly follows the suspension of our voluntary activity. The terror of cloudless noon--"

Hang on, it's gotta be nearly noon by now! And it's ten past nine. Ten past nine! Well, nothing for it but to get dressed and go for a wander round the grounds. The bath's off the list, too noisy. And so, careful as a cat burglar, you sneak your clothes on.
