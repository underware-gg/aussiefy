Take, for example, the matter of getting up in the morning. Of course, where there's a big house-party the problem is a simple one, cos you can always hear the others pattering about and brushing their teeth. You can time your own getting up by the number of people who seem to be up and about. But if you're the only guest there's apt to be a frightful misunderstanding.

"What time's brekkie?" you ask.

"Oh, any old time on Sundays," says the hostess with a generous wave. "Sleep in as late as you like. This is 'Liberty Hall.'"

The feeling behind this is perfectly ripper, but there's nothing you can really get hold of in it. It does the job at the time, but in the morning there's a vagueness about it that's simply terrifying.

Let's say you wake up at eight. You listen and hear no one stirring. Then, over on the cool pillow again till twenty past eight. Up on the elbow again, with your head cocked to one side. There's a creak over towards the stairs. They might all be up and going down to breakfast! It's the work of a moment to jump out of bed and listen at the door. Maybe open it modestly and have a squiz out. Deathlike silence, broken only, as the saying goes, by the ticking of the hall clock, and not a soul in sight. Probably they sleep in. Maybe eleven o'clock is their Sunday getting-up time. Some people _are_ like that.

Shut the door and sit on the edge of the bed. More sleep is out of the question. Let's have a look at the pictures in the guest room, just to pass the time. Here's one of Lorna Doone. How d'e do, Lorna? Here's a group--taken in 1902--showing your host in evening clothes, holding a mandolin. Probably a member of his uni musical club. Pretty scruffy-looking mob, you _must_ say. Well, how about this one? An etching, showing dodgy-looking barges on what's probably the Thames. Fair enough, at that.

Back to the door and listen again. Tick-tock-tick-tock. Odds are, if you ran your bath, you'd wake the whole house. Let's sit down on the edge of the bed again.

Hello, here are some books on the table. "Fifty Famous Sonnets," illustrated by Maxfield Parrish. Never touch a sonnet before brekkie. "My experiences in the Alps," by a woman mountain-climber who has written on the fly-leaf, "To my good friends the Elbridges, in memory of many happy days together at Chamounix. October, 1907." That settles _that_. "Essay on Compensation" in limp leather, by R.W. Emerson, published by Houghton, Mifflin & Co. Oh, righto! You suppose they reckoned that'd be over your head, did they? Well, we'll just show them! We'll read it just out of spite. Opening, at the red ribbon:

"Of the like nature is that expectation of change which instantly follows the suspension of our voluntary activity. The terror of cloudless noon--"

By the way, it must be nearly noon now! Only ten past nine! Well, the only thing for it is to get dressed and go out and walk round the grounds. Cross off the bath as too noisy. And so, very carefully, almost on the sly, you get dressed.
