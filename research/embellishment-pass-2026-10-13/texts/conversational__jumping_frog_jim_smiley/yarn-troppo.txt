“Rev. Leonidas W. H'm, Reverend Le--ah, stuff it. Listen to this. There was a bloke here once, Jim Smiley, and he lobbed at the camp in the winter of '49--or was it the spring of '50? Dunno, can't remember exactly, but I know it was one or the other cos the big flume wasn't finished when he first rocked up. Anyway. Strangest cove you ever laid eyes on, Smiley. He'd bet on anythin' that come up, long as he could find some poor bugger to take the other side; and if he couldn't, he'd just swap sides, wouldn't he? Didn't matter to him. Whatever suited the other bloke suited HIM--any way at all, long as he got a bet on, he was happy as Larry. And lucky? Luckier than a shithouse rat; near enough always come out on top.

He was always ready, always sniffin' round for a chance. You couldn't mention one single thing but that feller'd offer to bet on it, and take whichever side ya liked, like I was just sayin'. Horse race? He'd be cashed up or flat stony broke by the end of it. Dog fight, he's on it. Cat fight, he's on it. Chook fight, he's on it. Strewth, if there was two birds sittin' on a fence, he'd bet ya which one'd piss off first. Camp meetin'? He's there regular, backin' Parson Walker, who he reckoned was the best preacher round these parts--and fair's fair, so he was, and a good bloke with it. If Smiley so much as seen a straddle-bug startin' off somewhere, he'd bet ya how long it'd take to get to--to wherever the little bugger was headed, and if ya took him on, he'd follow that straddle-bug all the way to Mexico, just to find out where it was off to and how long it was on the frog and toad. To Mexico, mate. After a bug. Heaps of the blokes round here have seen him at it, and they'll tell ya.

Why, it was all the same to him--he'd bet on anythin'--strangest bloke goin'. Now get this. Parson Walker's missus is bloody crook once, for a fair while, and it's lookin' like they're not gonna save her. Then one mornin' the parson comes in, and Smiley up and asks him how she's goin', and the parson says she's a fair bit better--thank the Lord for his endless mercy--and comin' good so quick that with the blessin' of Providence she'll pull through yet. And Smiley, before he can stop himself, goes, 'Well, I'll risk two-and-a-half she doesn't anyway.' To the parson. About his own missus. Talk about not knowin' when to shut his trap.

Now, this Smiley had a mare. The blokes called her the fifteen-minute nag, but that was only a joke, ya know, cos course she was quicker than that--and he'd win the folding stuff on that horse, slow as buggery as she was, always crook with the asthma, or the distemper, or the consumption, or some bloody thing. They'd give her a two or three hundred yard start, and then go past her on the way. But every time, at the tail end of the race, she'd get all het up and desperate like, and here she comes, prancin' and sprawlin' up, legs flyin' every which way, sometimes up in the air, sometimes off to one side in the fences, kickin' up m-o-r-e dust and makin' m-o-r-e racket with her coughin' and sneezin' and blowin' her nose like a busted bagpipe--and she'd always pull up at the stand about a neck in front, near as ya could work it out. Every flamin' time.
