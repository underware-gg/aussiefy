The average household's leaks can add up to more than 9,300 gallons (about 35,000 litres) of water down the drain every year, and nine percent of homes have leaks that waste 50 gallons (about 190 litres) or more a day. The usual leaks round the house are worn dunny flappers, dripping taps and other leaky valves. These sorts of leaks are often easy to fix, needing only a few tools and bits of hardware that pay for themselves in water savings. Fixing the easy household leaks can knock about 10 percent off your water bills.

To check for leaks at your place, first you need to work out whether you're wasting water and then track down where the leak's coming from. Here are some tips for finding leaks:

Have a squiz at your water use during a colder month, like January or February. If a family of four goes over 12,000 gallons (about 45,000 litres) a month, there could be some big leaks.

Check your water meter before and after a two-hour stretch when no water's being used. If the meter moves at all, you've probably got a leak.

Find dunny leaks by putting a few drops of food colouring in the cistern. If any colour turns up in the bowl after 10 minutes, you've got a leak. (Make sure you flush straight after the test so it doesn't stain the cistern.)

Look over tap washers and pipe fittings for any water on the outside of the pipe to check for surface leaks.

Dunny Leaks

Old or worn-out dunny flappers (e.g., valve seal) can cause leaks. This simple rubber gizmo holds water in the cistern, then lets it go into the bowl when you flush the dunny. Because the rubber can wear out, the flapper should be checked every so often and replaced at least every five years to make sure it seals properly and doesn't leak. An old or worn flapper can make your dunny flush on its own or quietly leak thousands of gallons a year. Swapping a flapper is a quick and easy fix that'll save water and the folding stuff. Tap Leaks

Showerhead Leaks

A showerhead leaking at 10 drips a minute wastes more than 500 gallons (about 1,900 litres) a year. That's the water it takes to run 60 loads of dishes through your dishwasher. Some leaky showerheads can be fixed by making sure the showerhead's screwed on tight to the pipe stem and using pipe tape to hold it. Pipe tape, also called teflon tape, is sold at most hardware shops, is easy to put on, and can help stop leaks. For trickier valve leaks in showers that drip when they're not in use, get onto an experienced handyman or a licensed plumber.

Tip: While you're doing this repair, it's also a good idea to check and, if needed, replace the washer or "o" ring inside the showerhead.
