"Like I've told you before," said Jeff Peters, "I never had much faith in the perfidiousness of woman. As partners or co-workers in even the most innocent line of shonky business, they can't be trusted."

"They deserve the compliment," said I. "I reckon they've earned the right to be called the honest sex."

"Why shouldn't they be?" said Jeff. "They've got the other sex either grafting or staying back for 'em. They're all right in business until their feelings or their hair get touched up too much. Then you want a flat-footed, heavy-breathing bloke with sandy whiskers, five nippers and a building and loan mortgage ready as an understudy to take over her desk. Now, there was that widow lady me and Andy Tucker took on to help us with that little marriage agency scheme we got going out in Cairo.

"When you've got enough advertising capital--say a roll as big as the little end of a wagon tongue--there's dosh in marriage agencies. We had about $6,000 and we expected to double it in two months, which is about as long as a scheme like ours can run without taking out a New Jersey charter.

"We knocked up an ad that went something like this:

"Charming widow, beautiful, home-loving, 32 years, with $3,000 ready money and owning valuable country property, would remarry. Would prefer a poor man with a loving nature to one with money, as she realises that the solid virtues are most often found in the humble walks of life. No objection to an older man or a plain-looking one if he's faithful and true and able to manage property and invest money with good sense. Write, with particulars.

Lonely, Care of Peters & Tucker, agents, Cairo, Ill.

"'So far, so pernicious,' says I, when we'd finished cooking up the literary masterpiece. 'And now,' says I, 'where's the lady?'

"Andy gives me one of his looks of calm irritation.

"'Jeff,' says he, 'I thought you'd given up them ideas of realism in your art. Why should there be a lady? When they flog heaps of watered stock on Wall Street, would you expect to find a mermaid in it? What's a marriage ad got to do with a lady?'

"'Now listen,' says I. 'You know my rule, Andy: in all my illegitimate raids on the letter of the law, the article sold has to exist, be seen and be produced. That way, and by a careful study of council by-laws and train timetables, I've kept clear of any strife with the coppers that a five dollar note and a cigar couldn't square. Now, to work this scheme we've got to be able to produce in the flesh a charming widow or the equivalent, with or without the beauty, hereditaments and appurtenances set out in the catalogue and writ of errors, or else get hauled up before a justice of the peace.'

"'Well,' says Andy, rethinking things, 'maybe it'd be safer in case the post office or the peace commission tries to look into our agency. But where,' he says, 'are you gonna find a widow who'd waste her time on a marriage scheme with no marriage in it?'

"I told Andy I reckoned I knew just the party. An old mate of mine, Zeke Trotter, who used to draw soda water and teeth in a tent show, had made his wife a widow a year before by drinking some of the old doctor's indigestion cure instead of the liniment he always got sloshed on. I used to drop in at their place often, and I thought we could get her to work with us.

"'Twas only sixty miles to the little town where she lived, so I jumped on the I. C. and finds her in the same cottage with the same sunflowers and roosters standing on the washtub. Mrs. Trotter fitted our ad first-rate except, maybe, for beauty and age and what the property was worth. But she looked workable and easy on the eye, and it was a kindness to Zeke's memory to give her the job.
