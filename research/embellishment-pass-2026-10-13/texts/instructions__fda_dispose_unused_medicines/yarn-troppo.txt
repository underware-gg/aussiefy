Righto, open up that medicine cabinet. Chockers, is it? Expired drugs, old meds you haven't touched in years, the lot? Well, for most kinds of expired, unwanted or unused medicines, your best bet by a mile is a drug take-back program.

So, to get shot of your prescription or over-the-counter medicines, you've got two choices:

Drop them off at a drug take-back location.

Or pop them in the post in a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.

Can't do either of those? No dramas, here's the go:

If your medicine's on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's flush list, flush those potentially dangerous medicines down the toilet. Simple as that.

If it's not on the flush list, follow the steps further down to toss it out at home.

Drug Take-Back Options

Now, the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration runs a thing called National Prescription Drug Take Back Day, in communities right round the country. Plenty of communities put on their own drug take-back events too. Have a word with your local coppers to find a spot near you, or check with the DEA to track down a DEA-authorised collector in your neck of the woods.

And some chemists have medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks right there in the shop, plus mail-back programs and other ways to help you dispose of medicines safely. Worth asking next time you're in.

Use Prepaid Drug Mail-Back Envelopes

These prepaid drug mail-back envelopes are dead handy, and secure as anything for getting rid of the stuff. You can buy them all over the place, chemists and online included. Some chemists'll even hand you one for nothing. Can't argue with that.

To use a prepaid drug mail-back envelope:

Fill the envelope with your unused or expired medicines and seal it up nice and tight.

Post it off with the U.S. Postal Service. Done.

How to Dispose of Medicines at Home

Right, say there's no take-back option you can easily get to. There are still two ways to deal with medicines at home, depending on the drug.

Flushing medicines: Some medicines could do real harm to someone they weren't meant for, so they come with specific directions to flush them straight down the sink or the dunny once you're done with them and there's no take-back option handy. No mucking about with those ones.

How do you know which ones they are? Have a squiz at the label, or the patient information leaflet that came with your medicine. Or look up the list of medicines recommended for disposal by flushing when there's no take-back option handy. But remember, and this is the important bit: don't flush your medicine unless it's on the flush list.

Putting medicines in the household rubbish: If there's no take-back option, nearly every medicine, bar the ones on the FDA flush list, can go out with your household rubbish. We're talking prescription and OTC drugs in pills, liquids, drops, patches and creams, the whole box and dice.

Here's how you do it:

Take the drugs out of their original containers and mix them through something nobody in their right mind would want to touch, like used coffee grounds, dirt or kitty litter. That way the kids and the pets aren't tempted – and anyone rummaging through your bin on purpose looking for drugs won't have a clue what it is.

Put the mix in something you can close (a resealable zip-lock bag, an empty tin or some other container) so the drug can't leak or spill out.

Throw the container in your bin at home.

Scratch out every bit of your personal details on the empty medicine packaging, to protect your identity and your privacy. Then bin the packaging.
And if you've got a question about your medicine, don't guess: ask your health care professional or your chemist.

Disposing of Fentanyl Patches

Now, the fentanyl patch. It's one example of a product with a powerful opioid medicine in it, and it can be dangerous to anyone it wasn't prescribed for. It's a sticky patch that feeds a strong painkiller in through the skin.

And here's the thing: even after a patch has been used, there's still heaps of the medicine left in it. That's why the drug comes with instructions to flush used or leftover patches. Serious stuff, that one.
