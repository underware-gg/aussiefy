Is your medicine cabinet chockers with expired drugs or medications you don't use any more? The best way to get rid of most types of expired, unwanted or unused medicines is through a drug take-back program.

To dispose of your prescription or over-the-counter medicines, you can:

Drop them off at a drug take-back location.

Post them using a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.

If you can't use those options, follow these guidelines:

If your medicine is on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's flush list, flush these potentially dangerous medicines down the toilet.

If your medicine isn't on the flush list, follow the instructions below to toss it out at home.

Drug Take-Back Options

The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration sponsors National Prescription Drug Take Back Day in communities right across the country. Lots of communities also have their own drug take-back events. Check with your local police to find a location near you, or with the DEA to find a DEA-authorised collector in your community.

Some chemists offer on-site medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks, mail-back programs and other ways to help you safely dispose of medicines.

Use Prepaid Drug Mail-Back Envelopes

A prepaid drug mail-back envelope is a handy and secure disposal option. These envelopes are sold at all sorts of places, including chemists and online. Some chemists hand them out for free.

To use prepaid drug mail-back envelopes:

Fill the envelope with your unused or expired medicines and seal it.

Post the envelope using the U.S. Postal Service.

How to Dispose of Medicines at Home

When a take-back option isn't easy to get to, there are two ways to get rid of medicines at home, depending on the drug.

Flushing medicines: Because some medicines could be especially harmful to others, they come with specific directions to flush them straight down the sink or dunny when they're no longer needed and a take-back option isn't readily available.

How will you know? Check the label or the patient information leaflet that comes with your medicine. Or check the list of medicines recommended for disposal by flushing when a take-back option isn't readily available. Remember, don't flush your medicine unless it's on the flush list.

Disposing of medicines in the household rubbish: If a take-back option isn't available, almost all medicines, except those on the FDA flush list, can be chucked in your household rubbish. These include prescription and OTC drugs in pills, liquids, drops, patches and creams.

Follow these steps:

Take the drugs out of their original containers and mix them with something off-putting, such as used coffee grounds, dirt or kitty litter. This makes the medicine less tempting to kids and pets – and unrecognisable to anyone who might go through the bin on purpose looking for drugs.

Put the mixture in something you can close (a resealable zip-lock bag, empty tin or other container) to stop the drug from leaking or spilling out.

Throw the container in the bin at home.

Scratch out all your personal details on the empty medicine packaging to protect your identity and privacy. Bin the packaging.
If you've got a question about your medicine, ask your health care professional or chemist.

Disposing of Fentanyl Patches

The fentanyl patch is an example of a product that contains a powerful opioid medicine that can be dangerous to people it's not prescribed for. This sticky patch delivers a strong painkiller through the skin.

Even after a patch is used, heaps of the medicine is left in it. That's why the drug comes with instructions to flush used or leftover patches.
