Listen up, cos this is the stuff that keeps you in one piece. Say a bear's clocked you and it's watching you. Now there are a few more things you can do to stop it going from bad to a whole lot worse. Here are some general tips. But check the advice for every park you visit, mind: it changes from park to park, depending on how the local bears carry on.

First up, let the bear know what you are by talking calmly, so it twigs you're a human and not a prey animal. Not lunch. Stay still; stand your ground, but slowly wave your arms. Help the bear work out you're a human. It might come closer, or get up on its hind legs for a better look or a sniff. Don't panic if it does: a bear standing up is usually just having a stickybeak, not threatening you.

Stay calm, and remember most bears don't want to attack you; usually they just want to be left in peace, same as you. A bear might bluff its way out of it by charging at you, then pulling up and turning away at the last second. And bears can get defensive and start woofing, yawning, drooling, growling, snapping their jaws and pinning their ears back. That's a bear telling you to give it some room. Keep talking to it in low tones, nice and steady; it'll help you stay calmer, and it won't threaten the bear. A scream or a sudden movement can set off an attack. And whatever you do, never copy bear sounds or let out a high-pitched squeal.

Got littlies with you? Pick them up straight away. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear might think it's the sound of a prey animal. Slowly wave your arms above your head and tell the bear to back off. Do NOT run or make any sudden movements. And again, cos it matters: don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear might think it's the sound of a prey animal.

Bushwalk and get about in groups. A mob of people is usually noisier and smellier than one person on their own (anyone who's shared a tent after a week on the track knows it), so bears tend to pick up on a group from further off. And with all of you bunched up together, a group's big enough to put a bear off, too.

Make yourselves look as big as you can (say, get up on higher ground).

Do NOT let the bear get at your food. Once it's had a feed of your tucker, it'll only be keener, and it makes the problem worse for every poor soul coming down the track after you.

Do NOT drop your pack: it's armour for your back, and it keeps a bear out of your tucker.

If the bear's not moving, back away slowly and sideways, like a crab; that way you can keep an eye on it and you won't go head over heels on a tree root. Moving sideways doesn't threaten a bear, either.

Do NOT run. If the bear follows you, stop and hold your ground. A bear can run as fast as a racehorse, uphill and down, so you've got no hope of outrunning it. Same as dogs, they'll chase anything that bolts.

Do NOT climb a tree. Grizzlies and black bears can both climb trees, and better than you.

Leave the area or take a detour. If you can't, wait till the bear wanders off. Always leave the bear a way out.

Be extra careful if you see a female with cubs. Never get between a mum and her cub, and never try to go up to them. The chances of an attack go up heaps if she reckons you're a danger to her cubs, and you do not want to be on the wrong end of that.

Bear Attacks

Now, bear attacks are rare; most bears only care about protecting their food, their cubs or their patch. Still, getting your head right beforehand can help you react the best way. Every situation's different, but here's how a brown bear attack can differ from a black bear attack. Help keep everyone else safe by reporting every bear incident to a park ranger straight away. And above all, keep your distance from bears!

Brown/Grizzly Bears: If you're attacked by a brown/grizzly bear, leave your pack on and PLAY DEAD. Lie flat on your stomach with your hands clasped behind your neck. Spread your legs so it's harder for the bear to flip you over. Stay still till the bear's left the area. Fighting back usually just makes these attacks more intense. But if the attack keeps going, fight back hard, with everything you've got. Grab whatever's on hand and hit the bear in the face.

Black Bears: If it's a black bear that goes for you, DO NOT PLAY DEAD. Try to get away to somewhere secure, like a car or a building. If you can't get away, try to fight back with anything you can lay your hands on. Aim your kicks and punches at the bear's face and muzzle.

And if any bear attacks you in your tent, or stalks you and then attacks, do NOT play dead—fight back!
