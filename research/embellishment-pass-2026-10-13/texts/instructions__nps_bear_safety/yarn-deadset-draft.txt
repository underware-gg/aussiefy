Once a bear has spotted you and is paying attention to you, a few more strategies can help stop the situation getting worse. Here are some general tips. But please check the advice for each park you visit: the advice does vary from park to park depending on local bear behaviour.

Let the bear know who you are by talking calmly, so it knows you're a human and not a prey animal. Stay still; stand your ground but slowly wave your arms. Help the bear work out you're a human. It may come closer or stand up on its hind legs to get a better look or smell. A standing bear is usually curious, not threatening.

Stay calm and remember that most bears don't want to attack you; they usually just want to be left alone. Bears may bluff their way out of an encounter by charging and then turning away at the last second. Bears may also react defensively by woofing, yawning, drooling, growling, snapping their jaws and laying their ears back. Keep talking to the bear in low tones; this will help you stay calmer, and it won't be threatening to the bear. A scream or sudden movement may set off an attack. Never copy bear sounds or let out a high-pitched squeal.

Pick up littlies straight away. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear may think it's the sound of a prey animal. Slowly wave your arms above your head and tell the bear to back off. Do NOT run or make any sudden movements. Don't make any loud noises or screams—the bear may think it's the sound of a prey animal.

Bushwalk and travel in groups. Groups of people are usually noisier and smellier than one person on their own. So bears often pick up on groups of people from further away, and because of their combined size, groups also put bears off.

Make yourselves look as big as you can (for example, move to higher ground).

Do NOT let the bear get at your food. Getting your tucker will only encourage the bear and make the problem worse for others.

Do NOT drop your pack, as it can protect your back and stop a bear getting at your tucker.

If the bear isn't moving, move away slowly and sideways; this lets you keep an eye on the bear and not trip over. Moving sideways is also non-threatening to bears.

Do NOT run, but if the bear follows, stop and hold your ground. Bears can run as fast as a racehorse, uphill and down. Like dogs, they'll chase animals that run away.

Do NOT climb a tree. Both grizzlies and black bears can climb trees.

Leave the area or take a detour. If you can't, wait until the bear moves off. Always leave the bear a way out.

Be extra careful if you see a female with cubs; never put yourself between a mother and her cub, and never try to go near them. The chances of an attack go up heaps if she sees you as a danger to her cubs.

Bear Attacks

Bear attacks are rare; most bears are only interested in protecting their food, cubs or space. But being mentally prepared can help you react in the best way. Every situation is different, but below are guidelines on how brown bear attacks can differ from black bear attacks. Help protect others by reporting every bear incident to a park ranger straight away. Above all, keep your distance from bears!

Brown/Grizzly Bears: If you're attacked by a brown/grizzly bear, leave your pack on and PLAY DEAD. Lie flat on your stomach with your hands clasped behind your neck. Spread your legs to make it harder for the bear to turn you over. Stay still until the bear leaves the area. Fighting back usually makes these attacks more intense. But if the attack keeps going, fight back hard. Use whatever you've got on hand to hit the bear in the face.

Black Bears: If you're attacked by a black bear, DO NOT PLAY DEAD. Try to get away to somewhere secure, like a car or building. If you can't get away, try to fight back with any object you can find. Aim your kicks and punches at the bear's face and muzzle.

If any bear attacks you in your tent, or stalks you and then attacks, do NOT play dead—fight back!
