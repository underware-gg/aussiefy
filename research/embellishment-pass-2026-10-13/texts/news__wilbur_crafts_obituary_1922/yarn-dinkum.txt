WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts has died of pneumonia in hospital here today, aged 73. For 28 years he was superintendent of the International Reform Bureau, which he started himself, and he was well known right across the country for his work for prohibition and causes like it.

The mob on the other side of the laws he pushed called him a "reform fanatic," and for years he was a colourful sight around the congressional lobbies and committee rooms. Those on side with him, though, reckoned he'd had a big hand in getting prohibition through, along with laws to rein in the use of narcotics and others of the same kind.

He never kept quiet about being against Sunday amusements, either, and he tried to have them banned by law. He also did his best to stop the Dempsey-Carpentier fight going ahead in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. And he wrote a fair bit on moral questions.

After the funeral service here tomorrow, the body will go to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., the son of a Methodist preacher from puritan stock. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long career in the ministry at his father's church, switched over to Congregationalism in 1880, then in 1883 went into the Presbyterian ministry and stayed put from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a prolific and forceful writer and worker for religious causes in her own right, who was his partner in a good many of his activities, Sunday schools most of all.

Among reform leaders, there can't have been many who got through as much work as Dr. Crafts did. His pen and his voice barely got a rest in the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, given in twenty-nine different countries, and turned out a book a year, most of them, but not all, on religious topics. In a long list of titles that won't surprise anyone, for instance, there's this odd one that crops up: "Rhetoric Made Racy." On top of a steady stream of papers for religious magazines, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

To most people, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known for having a go at popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, ciggies: those were just a few of the things his tireless reforming zeal took on.
