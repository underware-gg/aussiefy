WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Listen to this. Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts has died, in hospital here today, of pneumonia. He was 73. Now, for 28 years he ran the show at the International Reform Bureau as its superintendent, and he'd started the thing himself, too. He was known far and wide for the work he put into prohibition and every other movement of that sort.

The mob on the other side of the laws he pushed had a name for him: a "reform fanatic." A wowser, we'd say. Either way, for years he was one of the real characters of the congressional lobbies and committee rooms. And the people on his side? They reckoned he'd had a big hand in getting prohibition through, plus the laws reining in the use of narcotics, and a fair few others along the same lines.

Sunday amusements, he couldn't stand, and he never kept it to himself. He tried to get the lot of them banned by law. He even had a crack at stopping the Dempsey-Carpentier fight going ahead in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. Took on a prizefight, he did. And he wrote heaps on moral questions.

The funeral service is here tomorrow, and after that the body goes to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

So where'd he come from? He was born at Fryeburg, Me., son of a Methodist preacher out of good puritan stock. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. His first eight years in a long life in the ministry he spent in his father's church; then in 1880 he switched over to Congregationalism, and in 1883 he went into the Presbyterian ministry and stayed put for good. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a prolific, forceful writer and worker for religious causes in her own right, and she was his partner in a good many of his ventures, Sunday schools above all.

And the work this man got through! Of all the reform leaders, you'd be hard pressed to find many who could keep up with Dr. Crafts. His pen and his voice barely got a breather in fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, mind, given in twenty-nine different countries, and he turned out a book a year, most of them, though not all, on religious topics. Run your eye down the list and it's pretty much what you'd expect, till this odd one pops up: "Rhetoric Made Racy." Rhetoric Made Racy! From him! On top of a steady stream of papers for religious magazines, he'd been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly since 1896.

But to the general public, of course, Dr. Crafts was best known for going after popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, ciggies: that's only a few of the things his tireless reforming zeal took on. Fair to say he'd not have thought much of this yarn being told over a coldie.
