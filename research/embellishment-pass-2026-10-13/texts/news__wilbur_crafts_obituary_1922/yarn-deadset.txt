WASHINGTON, Dec. 27.—Dr. Wilbur F. Crafts, for 28 years superintendent of the International Reform Bureau, which he started, and well known for his work on behalf of prohibition and movements like it, died in hospital here today of pneumonia. He was 73.

Dr. Crafts, who opponents of the laws he backed said was a "reform fanatic," was for years a colourful figure around the congressional lobbies and committee rooms. Those who were on side with his policies gave him credit for a big hand in getting prohibition through, along with laws to curb the use of narcotics and other laws along the same lines.

Never one to keep his opposition to Sunday amusements to himself, Dr. Crafts tried to get them banned by law. He also tried to stop the Dempsey-Carpentier fight going ahead in New Jersey in the Summer of 1921. He wrote a fair bit on moral questions.

After the funeral service here tomorrow the body will be sent to Westerly, R. I., to be buried.

Dr. Crafts was born at Fryeburg, Me., a son of a Methodist preacher of puritan stock. He graduated from Wesleyan in 1869 and from the Divinity School of Boston University in 1871. He spent the first eight years of his long career in the ministry in his father's church, switching to Congregationalism in 1880, and in 1883 going into the Presbyterian ministry, where he stayed from then on. In 1874 he married Sara J. Timanus, a prolific and forceful writer and worker for religious causes, and his partner in a lot of his many activities, especially the ones to do with Sunday schools.

Among reform leaders, few can have matched Dr. Crafts for the sheer amount of work he got through. His pen and his voice got very little rest over the last fifty years. He averaged six lectures a week, given in twenty-nine different countries, and had written a book a year, most, but not all, on religious topics. Here, for instance, is an odd title that crops up in a long list of others that don't surprise: "Rhetoric Made Racy." On top of always writing papers for religious magazines, he had since 1896 been chief editor of the Twentieth Century Quarterly.

To the general public Dr. Crafts was, of course, best known for having a go at popular amusements. Screen vampires, close dancing, "joy rides," which he said "often proved a ride of lifelong shame and woe"; Sunday baseball, ciggies: these were a few of the things his tireless reforming zeal went after.
