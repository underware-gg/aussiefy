I reverently believe that the Maker who made us all makes everything in New England but the weather. I dunno who makes that, but I reckon it must be raw apprentices in the weather-clerk's factory who muck about and learn how, in New England, for board and clothes, and then get promoted to make weather for countries that need a good article, and will take their custom elsewhere if they don't get it. There's a sumptuous variety about the New England weather that wins the stranger's admiration—and regret. The weather's always up to something there; always sticking strictly to business; always coming up with new designs and trying them on the people to see how they'll go. But it gets through more business in spring than in any other season. In the spring I've counted one hundred and thirty-six different kinds of weather inside of four-and-twenty hours. It was me that made the fame and fortune of that bloke that had that marvellous collection of weather on show at the Centennial, that so gobsmacked the foreigners. He was going to travel all over the world and get specimens from all the climes. I said, "Don't you do it; you come to New England on a good spring day." I told him what we could do in the way of style, variety, and quantity. Well, he came and he made his collection in four days. As to variety, why, he owned up that he got hundreds of kinds of weather that he'd never heard of before. And as to quantity, well, after he'd picked out and chucked all that was blemished in any way, he not only had weather enough, but weather to spare; weather to hire out; weather to sell; to deposit; weather to invest; weather to give to the poor. The people of New England are by nature patient and forbearing, but there are some things they won't put up with. Every year they kill a lot of poets for writing about "Beautiful Spring." These are mostly blow-ins, who bring their notions of spring from somewhere else, and can't, of course, know how the locals feel about spring. And so the first thing they know the chance to ask how they feel has gone for good. Old Probabilities has a mighty reputation for spot-on prophecy, and thoroughly deserves it. You pick up the paper and watch how crisply and confidently he ticks off what today's weather is going to be on the Pacific, down South, in the Middle States, in the Wisconsin region. See him sail along in the joy and pride of his power till he gets to New England, and then see his tail drop. He's got no idea what the weather's going to be in New England. Well, he mulls it over, and by and by he comes out with something about like this: Probably northeast to southwest winds, varying to the southward and westward and eastward, and points between, high and low barometer swapping around from place to place; probable areas of rain, snow, hail, and drought, followed or preceded by earthquakes, with thunder and lightning. Then he jots down his postscript from his wandering mind, to cover accidents. "But it is possible that the programme may be wholly changed in the mean time." Yep, one of the brightest gems in the New England weather is the dazzling uncertainty of it. There's only one thing certain about it: you're certain there's going to be heaps of it—a perfect grand review; but you never can tell which end of the procession is going to move first. You get set for the drought; you leave your brolly in the house and head out, and two to one you get drowned. You make up your mind that the earthquake's due; you stand from under, and grab hold of something to steady yourself, and the first thing you know you get struck by lightning. These are big letdowns; but they can't be helped. The lightning there is peculiar; it's so convincing that when it strikes a thing it doesn't leave enough of that thing behind for you to tell whether—Well, you'd think it was something valuable, and a pollie had been there. And the thunder. When the thunder starts to just tune up and scrape and saw, and key up the instruments for the performance, strangers say, "Strewth, what awful thunder you've got here!" But when the baton's raised and the real concert starts, you'll find that stranger down in the cellar with his head in the ash-barrel.
