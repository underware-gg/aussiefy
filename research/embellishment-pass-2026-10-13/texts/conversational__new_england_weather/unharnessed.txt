I reverently reckon that the Maker who made us all makes everything in New England except the weather. I don't know who makes that, but I reckon it must be raw apprentices in the weather-clerk's factory who muck about and learn the trade in New England, for board and clothes, and then get promoted to make weather for countries that want a decent product and will take their business elsewhere if they don't get it. There's a lavish variety about the New England weather that has strangers admiring it—and regretting it. The weather's always up to something there; always sticking strictly to business; always coming up with new designs and trying them out on the people to see how they go. But it gets through more business in spring than in any other season. In spring I've counted one hundred and thirty-six different kinds of weather inside twenty-four hours. It was me that made the name and fortune of that bloke who had that marvellous collection of weather on show at the Centennial, the one that so flabbergasted the foreigners. He was going to travel all round the world and get samples from every climate. I said, "Don't you do it; you come to New England on a good spring day." I told him what we could manage in the way of style, variety and quantity. Well, he came, and he made his collection in four days. As for variety, why, he owned up that he got hundreds of kinds of weather he'd never heard of before. And as for quantity, well, after he'd picked out and chucked away everything with any sort of blemish on it, he not only had weather enough, but weather to spare; weather to hire out; weather to sell; to bank; weather to invest; weather to give to the poor. The people of New England are patient and long-suffering by nature, but there are some things they just won't put up with. Every year they knock off a lot of poets for writing about "Beautiful Spring." These are mostly blow-ins who bring their ideas about spring from somewhere else, and of course can't know how the locals feel about it. And so, before they know it, the chance to ask them how they feel has gone for good. Old Probabilities has a mighty reputation for getting his forecasts right, and he thoroughly deserves it. You pick up the paper and watch how crisply and confidently he ticks off what today's weather's going to be on the Pacific, down South, in the Middle States, in the Wisconsin region. Watch him sail along in all the joy and pride of his powers till he gets to New England, and then watch his tail drop. He hasn't got a clue what the weather's going to be in New England. Well, he chews it over, and after a while he comes out with something like this: Probably northeast to southwest winds, shifting to the southward and westward and eastward, and points in between, high and low barometer swapping around from place to place; likely patches of rain, snow, hail and drought, followed or preceded by earthquakes, with thunder and lightning. Then he jots down a postscript from his wandering mind, to cover any accidents. "But it is possible that the programme may be wholly changed in the mean time." Yes, one of the brightest gems in the New England weather is how dazzlingly uncertain it is. There's only one thing certain about it: you can be sure there's going to be plenty of it—a proper grand parade; but you can never tell which end of the procession's going to move off first. You get ready for the drought; you leave your umbrella at home and head out, and it's two to one you get drowned. You make up your mind the earthquake's due; you get out from under, and grab hold of something to steady yourself, and the next thing you know you're struck by lightning. These are big letdowns; but they can't be helped. The lightning there is a funny one; it's so convincing that when it hits something it doesn't leave enough of that thing behind for you to tell whether—Well, you'd think it was something valuable, and a Congressman had been through. And the thunder. When the thunder only starts tuning up and scraping and sawing and getting the instruments ready for the performance, strangers say, "Strewth, what awful thunder you've got here!" But when the baton goes up and the real concert starts, you'll find that stranger down in the cellar with his head in the ash-barrel.
