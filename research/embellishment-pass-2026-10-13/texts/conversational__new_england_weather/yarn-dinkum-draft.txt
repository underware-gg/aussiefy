Here's one for ya. I reckon, hand on heart, the Maker who made the lot of us makes everything in New England except the weather. Dunno who makes that. My guess is it's raw apprentices in the weather-clerk's factory, mucking about and learning the trade in New England for board and clothes, and then they get bumped up to making weather for countries that want a decent article and'll take their custom somewhere else if they don't get one. There's a lavish variety to New England weather that gets a stranger admiring it—and regretting it. The weather's always up to something over there; always minding its business; always coming up with new designs and trying them on the people to see how they go. But spring's when it does the most business. One spring I counted a hundred and thirty-six different kinds of weather inside twenty-four hours.

It was me, too, that made the name and the fortune of that bloke with the marvellous collection of weather on show at the Centennial, the one that knocked the foreigners for six. He was all set to travel the world collecting specimens from every clime. I said to him, "Don't you do it, mate; you come to New England on a good spring day." I told him what we could turn out for style, variety and quantity. So he came, and he made his whole collection in four days. Variety? He owned up he'd got hundreds of kinds of weather he'd never even heard of. And quantity? Well, after he'd picked out and chucked everything with the slightest blemish, he still had weather enough and weather to spare; weather to hire out, weather to sell, weather to bank, weather to invest, weather to give to the poor.

The people of New England are patient, easygoing folk by nature, but there's some things they just won't put up with. Every year they kill off a fair few poets for writing about "Beautiful Spring." Mostly they're blow-ins who've brought their idea of spring from somewhere else, and of course they can't know how the locals feel about it. So before they know it, their chance to ask how the locals feel is gone for good.

Old Probabilities has a mighty name for getting his forecasts right, and he's earned every bit of it. You pick up the paper and watch him tick off, crisp and confident as you like, what today's weather's going to do on the Pacific, down South, in the Middle States, round Wisconsin. Watch him sail along, proud as punch with his powers, till he hits New England, and then watch his tail drop. He's got no idea what New England's going to do. So he chews it over, and after a while he comes out with something like this: Probably northeast to southwest winds, varying to the southward and westward and eastward, and points between, high and low barometer swapping around from place to place; probable areas of rain, snow, hail, and drought, succeeded or preceded by earthquakes, with thunder and lightning. Then, with his mind off wandering, he jots down a postscript to cover accidents: "But it is possible that the programme may be wholly changed in the mean time."

Yep, one of the shiniest gems in New England's weather is how dazzlingly uncertain it is. There's only one thing you can be sure of: there'll be heaps of it—a proper grand parade; but you can never tell which end of the procession's going to move off first. You get yourself ready for the drought, leave your brolly at home and head out, and it's two to one you get drowned. You make up your mind the earthquake's due, so you stand clear and grab hold of something to steady yourself, and next thing you know you're struck by lightning. These are big letdowns, but there's nothing you can do about them.

The lightning there's a funny thing. It's that convincing that when it hits something it doesn't leave enough of it behind for you to tell whether—well, you'd reckon it was something valuable and a pollie had been through. And then there's the thunder. When it's only tuning up, scraping and sawing and getting the instruments ready for the show, strangers go, "Strewth, that's awful thunder you've got here!" But once the baton goes up and the real concert kicks off, you'll find that same stranger down the cellar with his head in the ash-barrel.
