Sad news, this one. Bob Weir's gone. The American muso and songwriter died surrounded by his loved ones, his family let everyone know on Saturday. He'd played guitar and sung for the Grateful Dead, and he was 78. The family put it up on Instagram: he'd been fighting a cancer diagnosis lately, but it was "underlying lung issues" that got him in the end. They never said what day he died, mind.

Now, for decades this bloke played rhythm guitar and sang with the Grateful Dead, the big American psychedelic rock outfit, and he wrote a few of their songs and all. Take the hit "Sugar Magnolia", and you didn't get out of a Grateful Dead gig without hearing it. On top of that he had a long solo career, starting up band after band with his old Grateful Dead mates, and the man was still up there playing live till just months before he died. Can't fault that for staying power.

He came into the world as Robert Hall Parber, on 16 October 1947, in San Francisco, California, and grew up round the San Francisco Bay Area. Did a stint at school over in Colorado too, which is where he met John Perry Barlow, who'd end up in the Grateful Dead himself. Young Bob picked up a guitar at 13 and found his way into the local bluegrass scene, and who does he run into there but Jerry Garcia, plonking away on a banjo. Then on 31 December 1965, New Year's Eve, no less, Garcia and Weir shook on starting their own group. And the name? Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions. Bit of a mouthful, that. Lucky it grew into the Grateful Dead.

So he played rhythm guitar and sang for the band for decades. From the 1970s on he was off doing his own thing as well, solo and in a whole string of other groups outside the Grateful Dead: Kingfish, Bobby and the Midnites, RatDog, Furthur, The Other Ones, The Dead, Wolf Bros, and Dead & Company. Every last one of 'em had old Grateful Dead collaborators in it, and between them they had a crack at the lot: country rock, jam band, jazz-influenced roots rock built on blues and country. Mostly he was the harmony man, singing backup, but he did step up to the mic for lead on several tracks. Like the Grateful Dead anthem "Truckin'", which in 1997 the Library of Congress named a national treasure. Not bad for a backup singer.

Off the stage he kept busy too. He set up Tamalpais Research Institute, a virtual performance platform, and he got stuck into politics, backing several causes: the environment, poverty relief, and issues to do with the arts. And he never drifted far from the Bay Area music scene, playing live with the local musos and co-owning a venue, Sweetwater Music Hall in Mill Valley, California. A good innings, that.
