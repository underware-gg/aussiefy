unharnessed: The most natural and accurate version: idiomatic, varied, keeps dollars, and keeps the dry wit; one clumsy simile.
  minor: "feeding a mongrel steer that won't fatten up its own weight in corn" (for "feeding his weight in corn to a scalawag steer that won't fat up"): Reordering attaches 'its own weight in corn' to 'fatten up'; the simile blurs.

yarn-dinkum: A faithful, natural level-3 retelling that keeps the letter's dry humour; only the dollar-to-quid swap and a forced "beaut" grate.
  minor: 'with eight quid every Saturday night' (for 'with eight dollars every Saturday night'): Converts a Chicago wage to pounds; and then "sixty cents out of any quid" mixes currencies.
  minor: "a quid's worth one hundred and six cents" (for 'a dollar is worth one hundred and six cents'): A quid is not a hundred cents; the arithmetic of the aphorism goes slightly wrong.
  minor: 'a beaut education' (for 'a good education'): "beaut" as a plain attributive for an education sounds forced.
  minor: "the first commercial traveller, and my commercial travellers' bills" (for "the first traveling man, and my drummers' bills"): Repeats the same phrase where the original varies it.

yarn-deadset: A close, light-handed level-3 version that keeps the tone; one garbled simile and the quid/cents mix are its faults.
  major: "feeding a no-hoper steer that won't fatten up its own weight in corn" (for "feeding his weight in corn to a scalawag steer that won't fat up"): The reordering attaches 'its own weight in corn' to 'fatten up', garbling the simile.
  minor: 'have got a bit big for their boots back East' (for 'have swelled up a little in the East'): A person-idiom applied to ideas; strains.
  minor: 'eight quid every Saturday night' (for 'eight dollars every Saturday night'): Chicago wage converted to pounds; then "sixty cents out of any quid" mixes currencies.
  minor: "the first commercial traveller, and my commercial travellers' bills" (for "the first traveling man, and my drummers' bills"): Repetition where the original varies.

yarn-troppo: Lively and varied, but heavier than the brief, with one changed claim and commentary that loosens the dry letter.
  major: "He can't, and he won't wear it" (for "he can stand it, because he won't stand it"): The original says the old man could afford it but won't; 'He can't' changes the claim and contradicts 'the bills won't send me broke'.
  minor: "Eight quid. Not a lot, but it's a start." (for 'with eight dollars every Saturday night'): Added commentary, and the wage converted to pounds.
  minor: 'Dead, dead careful.' (for 'unless you are very, very careful'): Padding that adds a yarn tic above level 3.
  minor: 'Ya take the stairs, same as every man and his dog.' (added): Added aside; spells out the original's dry joke.
  minor: 'live under the one roof' (for 'live together'): Stretches the spending-and-earning metaphor.
  minor: "To him a quid's worth one hundred and six cents" (for 'a dollar is worth one hundred and six cents'): Quid and cents mixed.

engine: Mostly the original with mechanical word swaps, some of which break grammar or sense, and a bolted-on "Bloody hell".
  major: 'unless you are very, dead careful' (for 'unless you are very, very careful'): Broken substitution; ungrammatical.
  major: 'Bloody hell, I simply mention these things because I am scared stiff' (for 'I simply mention these things because I am afraid'): Bolted-on swear, and 'I am afraid' as a hedge rendered as fear: wrong sense.
  major: 'It is all with the bloke.' (for 'It is all with the man.'): Mechanical swap that garbles the idiom.
  major: "Baron Munchausen was the first travelling bloke, and my drummers' bills" (for "Baron Munchausen was the first traveling man, and my drummers' bills"): Loses the trade sense of travelling salesman and leaves US 'drummers'.
  minor: 'a beaut business man, and he never spends the dollar' (for 'a good business man, and he never spends the dollar'): "beaut" forced; and quid/dollar mismatch in one sentence.
  minor: "a scalawag steer that won't fat up" (for "a scalawag steer that won't fat up"): US period forms left untranslated here and in "dry-goods business" and "run an automobile".
