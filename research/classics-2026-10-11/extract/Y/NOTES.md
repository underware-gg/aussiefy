## Voice and discourse habits for the style guide

- **Defanged swearing.** Mild oaths stand in for profanity: *flamin'*, *bloomin'*, *blimed*, *strike!*,
  *strewth/Gorstrooth*, *spare me days*. Lawson's printed "(adjective)" and "blanky" mark the habitual
  *bloody*. Dennis glosses *bli'me* as "an oath with the fangs drawn". Modern output can use *bloody*
  and *flamin'*. The rest read as period pieces.
- **Understatement and the deadpan sting.** "He is careless, but a good enough husband." "There are
  things that a bushwoman cannot do." "but he had built that woodheap hollow." A flat closing clause
  undercuts what came before. Feeling shows in practical acts, not words: "she does not gush or make a
  fuss about it. She gets him something good to eat".
- **Stoic dismissal.** "No use fretting." Endurance is a virtue (*crack hardy*, *don't drop your bundle*).
- **Self-deprecation.** "Aw, I'm a chump!", "Like any mug", "wot a mug / I must 'a' seemed".
- **Ironic "nothing".** The glossary entry "Nothing (ironically)" means "something considerable".
- **Grammar of broad speech.** *me* for *my*. First-person *-s* in the narrative present ("I backs me
  barrer in", "I gives me pal", "sez I", "Thinks I"). *seen/done/give* as past tense ("I seen 'er",
  "done a duck"). *them* for *those*. Plural subjects with *is* ("the leaves is shootin'"). Double
  negatives ("I won't never go drovin'", "It wer'n't no guyver neither"). *bit* for *bitten*.
- **Clipping and rhyming slang.** *intro*, *footer*, *bookie*, *Little Lons.*, *'Loo*, *'Roy*; *old pot*
  (pot and pan = old man), *John* (John Hop = cop), *on one's pat* (Pat Malone).
- **Intensifiers.** *fair* ("fair outs me"), *dead* ("dead crook"), *a treat* ("turn me down a treat",
  often ironic), *clean* ("clean done me block").
- **Similes from pub, sport and boxing.** "The air is like a long, cool swig o' beer". Fortune has a
  "dirty left". Cupid gets a "hammer-lock".
- **Eye-dialect.** Dennis respells heavily: dropped h, *wiv*, *orf/arst/carn't*, *crool/choon/fucher*,
  and comic long words (*intrajuiced*, *phonergraft*). These are recorded as patterns. A translator
  should use them sparingly, because the dropped h and *wiv* read Cockney rather than modern Australian.

## Sense shifts to watch

- *tart*: neutral "young woman" in 1915, insulting today. Probably reject.
- *barrack*: here "jeer at girls". Today it means "cheer for a team".
- *larrikin*: a street tough in 1915, now a lovable rogue.
- *slab*: in Dennis, a portion or a lanky man. Today a carton of beer.
- *lash*: in Dennis, violence. Today "have a go".
- *togs*: clothes in Dennis. Today swimwear in Queensland and Victoria.
- *chew the rag*: in Dennis, brood. The US sense is chat.
- *chuck up*: in Dennis, give up. Today it means vomit.
- *nark*: in Dennis, a spoilsport. Not the British "informer".
- *sundowner*: a swagman in Lawson. Today an evening drink.

## Web checks

- "Andy's Gone with Cattle", *riders*: the Representative Poetry Online (University of Toronto)
  annotation, after Stevens's 1906 anthology, glosses riders as timbers holding down bark roofs.
- *guyver/guiver*: Green's Dictionary of Slang and Wiktionary give it as Australian/NZ for pretence or
  flattering talk, which confirms the Intro sense.
