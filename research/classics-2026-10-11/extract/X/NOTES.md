## Voice and discourse habits for the style guide

- **Laconic understatement, framed by an ironic narrator.** "It's dry, all right," said young
  O'Neil, "With which astute remark..." The man's throat is cut, and he says "thank the Lord it's
  tough". A barber calls a near-murder "a trifle overdone". In Geebung a spectator's leg is broken
  "just from merely looking on".
- **Deadpan irony about Dad's optimism (Rudd).** "It must have been fine land, the way Dad talked
  about it!" "There was very valuable timber on it, too, so he said". The narrator undercuts Dad
  with a trailing "so he said" or "the way he talked".
- **Tall-talk hyperbole stated flatly.** "At every creek we came to we drank till it stopped
  running." "they waddied one another till the plain was strewn with dead".
- **Pessimist refrain.** The comedy of Hanrahan is that he gives the same doom line whatever the
  weather. The poem also shows how farmers talk: in short declaratives with tags ("we do",
  "no doubt", "without a doubt", "all right").
- **Tags and dislocation.** "They were demons, were the members...", "We want an inch of rain,
  we do", "There will, without a doubt".
- **Exclamatory "talk about X!"** and the sentence-opener "No mistake, ...".
- **Bush grammar** (use sparingly, for characters rather than narrators): "them ... what keeps",
  "the flats is", "has seasons", "rain don't come", "where he are", "I've rode", "I seen",
  "comed off", zero relatives ("there's none can ride like me", "a yell might wake the dead"),
  "this here", "me" for "my", embedded questions without "if" ("ask a wild duck can it swim").
- **Boasting while disclaiming boasting.** Mulga Bill says "I'm not the one to talk—I hate a man
  that blows" in the middle of a brag. The opposite of laconic, and played for laughs.
- **Spellings.** Dropped -g (keepin', singin', bloomin'); Irish-Australian vowels (rooned, cruke);
  -ow as -er (narrer); h-dropping ('Ere); child and broad-rural elisions (muster bin, thet, th',
  y', 'n, 'bout, nater). Literary elisions ('Twas, o'er, wond'rous, murd'rous, list'ning) are
  general poetic style, not Australian, and are not recorded.
- **Direction words carry geography.** "up the country" means the bush; "down the country" means
  town; "way out to Back-o'-Bourke"; "down the Lachlan", "down the Cooper" (along a river
  district).
- **Gestures.** Men "squatted on his heel / And chewed a piece of bark" (Hanrahan). A good scene
  detail, not a term.

## Uncertain items

- **on the wing** (low): "riding the flank of the mob" is inferred from the wheeling context. It
  may just mean "flying fast".
- **geebung** (low): Green's records 19th-century slang senses (an uncultured native-born
  colonist; any out-of-the-way place). Whether Paterson's club name leans on them is my inference.
- **hop scrub** (low): the plant identification (Dodonaea hop bush) is from general knowledge.
- **Cuff and Collar** (low): looks like Paterson's own coinage, not established slang.
- **gone to Queensland droving** (low): famous as a quotation, but not an idiom in its own right.
- **tote** (medium): it's unclear whether the barber runs an official totalisator or an
  unofficial betting pool.
- **mimosa** (medium): read as the colonial name for wattle.
- **Shared British/US colloquialisms** (fairly, lark, toff, blooming, peeler, like blazes, all
  the go, from the jump, make one's pile, on spec, pub, drag, thundering, by George): these are
  in common Australian use but not exclusively Australian. Their notes say so, and confidence is
  medium or low.
- **Not recorded:** "weedy", "game/gameness", "stop away", "chaps", "yokel", "shark",
  "saplings", "Dad and Dave". "Dad and Dave" later became a byword for country bumpkins, but this
  chapter uses the names only for characters.

## Web checks (search only; meanings written in my own words)

- "tuppy": Green's Dictionary of Slang lists it as Australian for a worn-out, worthless animal
  (greensdictofslang.com).
- "give (someone) best": Green's lists it as Australian for conceding defeat, citing Paterson and
  Lawson.
- "run a banker": current Australian newspaper and CSIRO usage for a river or creek flowing bank
  high (e.g. fassifernguardian.com headline "Dry creek bed to a banker").
- "geebung": Green's and dictionary.com give the slang senses noted above; Persoonia, from Dharug.
